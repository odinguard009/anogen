# Forty Minutes of Grass

> "Nobody looks up at a roof. That is why the wise sleep there." - Saying of the Hallowcrest groundskeepers

Professor Brandmeier took the register twice in every Spell Grammar lesson. Once at the first bell. Once forty minutes before the end.

She believed students ran off at the halfway point, like geese crossing a road. She wasn't wrong about the geese. It also meant I had eighty minutes before anyone noticed I'd missed both rolls.

Eighty minutes. Minus the stairs.

The Old Observatory had five flights, and the last door was locked. I had the key. The door opened with a small cough of rust. Behind it was the only flat place on the plateau where nobody ever came.

Someone had planted a garden up there once. Then, I always figured, they got bored. A dwarf apple tree in a cracked half-barrel. Three herb beds gone leggy. A brass telescope on an iron stand, with no eyepiece, pointed at a piece of sky that stopped being interesting eighty years ago.

And grass. Real grass, thick enough to swallow your hand up to the wrist.

It was the last week of the ninth month. Down in the Lower Court, the maples had turned the color of a scab. The groundskeepers were raking them into wet piles. Up here, the grass was green like midsummer, and blue at the roots. The apple tree still had every leaf.

I'd filed that a month ago under *none of my business*. It sat next to the vice-headmaster's waistcoats.

Seventy-six minutes.

I lay down in the shade between the second herb bed and the wall. I laced my gloved fingers over my stomach. I was asleep before I'd finished deciding to be.

The dream starts with the smell. It always does.

Wet ash. The kind a town leaves behind when it burns and then gets rained on. Then comes the field. It runs to every horizon, and it's planted with banners. Black cloth on black poles. No crest on any of them. Thousands.

I stopped counting years ago. Under each banner, men stand in rows, waiting for an order.

Nobody breathes. That's where it goes wrong. I notice the soldier next to me. His chest isn't moving. His eyes don't blink. Then I notice mine isn't moving either.

I try to breathe.

The air comes in thick and stops behind my teeth. It's ash, ground fine like flour. Every pull drags more of it down my throat and packs it tight. The banners crack in a wind I can't feel. The whole field turns its head toward me at once, with a sound like wheat bending. They're waiting for me to say it. The word. Whatever the word is. And I can't, because my mouth is full, and my throat is full, and there's no room left in my chest for anything but gray, and gray, and

Something cried.

Thin. Wet. Furious. It went through the field like a needle through cloth, and every banner went still.

Air.

I woke with it already in my lungs. That was the first wrong thing. Usually I woke up clawing at the grass, and the taste stayed in my mouth for an hour. A taste nobody else could smell. I kept a peppermint in my left glove for it.

This time there was only cool grass against my cheek. And the green smell of crushed thyme.

The second wrong thing was sitting a meter away.

A girl. Her back was against the telescope stand, knees pulled up, face pressed into her elbow. She wore a red armband on her left sleeve, exactly two fingers below the shoulder seam. That's where the student handbook says to wear it. It's also where nobody wears it.

Dark-gold hair, pinned so tight it looked like a prison sentence. A tin watering can by her shoe, still dripping on the stones.

She was crying like someone who'd given herself five minutes and not one second more. Hard, fast bursts. Between them, she talked into her sleeve.

"Stupid. Stupid, stupid, *stupid*."

I knew her. Everyone in Year Five knew her, the way a village knows where the wasp nest is.

Lysette Hollen. Fourth on the Ladder. Vice-chair of the Discipline Committee. In second year, she wrote up a boy for whistling "with intent."

She hadn't seen me. That wasn't strange. People never do. I keep my mana folded small and flat and very still. I've done it so long I don't have to think about it. People walk past me in the halls and say sorry to the wall afterward.

So I counted my options. That's what I do.

One: lie still. She'd finish, wipe her face, water her plants and leave. She'd never know a boy from her own year watched her fall apart behind a broken telescope. That was probably the kind choice. It was definitely the easy one.

Two: sit up and cough. She'd be so embarrassed. And she'd make me pay for it, in some small, exact Committee way, for the rest of the year.

There was no three. Unless I counted the forty-foot drop into the rhododendrons.

I lay still. I'm good at it.

Then she lifted her head and wiped her nose on her armband.

The Committee armband. She saw what she'd done. Her mouth went crooked. She started crying again, worse, at the armband now, like it had betrayed her personally.

I had the handkerchief out of my breast pocket before I decided anything. That is not how I do things.

Old linen, washed thin as paper. In one corner, a silver feather, stitched a little crooked. My mother did it while arguing with someone across a kitchen. I never lent it out. I never used it. I just carried it. That was all it was for.

I held it out across the meter of grass.

For a second, she didn't see that either.

Then she did. She went from sitting to crouching in one move. A hand trowel appeared in her fist, held point-down along her forearm. Someone had taught her properly. Her eyes were red, her face was blotchy, and she looked ready to kill me.

"How long," she said, "have you been *there*?"

"Since a bit after the first roll."

"That's an hour."

"Fifty-two minutes," I said. "I was asleep for most of it."

"Most."

"The last four, I was awake."

She stared at me. I stayed on my back, holding the handkerchief up like a tiny white flag.

"Put that away."

"You've got some on your armband."

Her eyes dropped to her sleeve. Her jaw twitched. She took the handkerchief by one corner, between two fingers, like evidence. Then she turned her back on me to use it.

I sat up. Slowly, so the trowel would stay where it was.

* * *

It took her a while. I looked at the apple tree, so she'd have some privacy. I counted the leaves on the nearest branch. Eleven.

When she turned around, her face was mostly her own again. She'd folded the handkerchief into a neat square. She held it in both hands, like a paper she meant to hand in.

"This roof," she said, "is closed to students."

"Then we're both in trouble."

"I'm here on Committee business."

I looked at the watering can.

"The thyme," I said, "is breaking a rule?"

Her ears went red. I hadn't thought she had any red left. So she was the gardener. Someone who only had the dawn and a few stolen hours. Someone who never had the heart to cut the leggy plants back. Dirt under her nails, and two of them broken short.

"Here's what happens," she said. "You saw nothing. I didn't see you. You go down those stairs and never come back up. And I don't file a report for trespassing in a sealed building. That's generous. You should know it's generous."

I did have a permit. It was in my desk, signed by the headmistress. *Medical exemption.* If I showed it to Lysette Hollen, she'd ask what was medical about a roof. Then she'd ask why the headmistress signed for a fifth-year with a boring record. Then, being who she was, she'd find out.

"I can do the first two," I said.

"And the third?"

"I sleep here."

"You can sleep in your dorm like a normal person."

"I tried. It's loud."

"It's a *stone tower*."

"The stone's loud."

She opened her mouth. Shut it. I think she couldn't tell if I was mocking her. I wasn't. Somebody on the fourth floor of my tower played the same wooden flute every night. Eleven notes. He got the ninth one wrong every single time.

"Fine," she said. "Do the first two. And if a word of this gets out, I'll know it came from you, because you're the only one who—" She stopped. "Before you start making up reasons."

"I wasn't going to."

"Everyone makes up reasons. It's what people *do*." She looked at the folded linen instead of me. Her thumb found the feather and stopped there. "Rowan Tallis asked Clara Belmont to the Lantern Ball. At lunch. In the middle of the atrium. In front of the whole year. That's it. That's the whole reason. So you don't have to invent one."

I knew Rowan Tallis from far away, like most people. Second on the Ladder. The Dawn Boy. Cheerful before breakfast, which should be illegal. He ate standing up. He said hello to the groundskeepers by name.

"He folded her a lantern," Lysette said. "Out of paper. Himself. He's hopeless at folding. He's hopeless at anything with his hands that isn't a sword. It was all lopsided, and everyone went *aww* like it was a puppy."

Her voice cracked. She stopped and waited for it to finish.

"Then he came over to me after. And asked if I was going. With anyone. Because he's *nice*. He's nice to everybody. He has no idea. He's never had any idea. And the stupid part is, I don't even—"

She didn't finish that one at all.

I could've said what people say. That he was an idiot. That there'd be other balls. None of it would help her.

"Did it light?" I said.

She blinked. "What?"

"The lantern. Did he get it to light?"

A pause.

"No," she said. "It caught fire. A little. On one side." Her mouth twitched. "Clara put it out with her tea."

Then she laughed. Once. Short and wet, through her nose. She looked horrified with herself. She pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth, like she could push the laugh back in.

I didn't laugh. I don't, really. But something in my chest, something held flat for a long time, moved about a quarter of an inch. I noticed it. I put it aside to look at later.

She looked down at the handkerchief properly. Maybe for the first time. She turned it over and ran the edge between her fingers.

"This is good linen."

"It was my mother's."

She went still. I watched her do the math. *Was.*

"Then why would you give it to some girl who was—" She waved a quick, cross circle at her own face.

"You were using your armband."

Her chin came up. For a second I thought she'd throw it at me. Instead she folded it smaller, with exact corners. She slid it into her jacket pocket and buttoned the flap.

"I'll wash it," she said. "And give it back."

"It's fine. It's clean."

"It is *not* clean. Not now." She picked up the watering can. It was empty. She put it down again with great dignity. "I'll wash it."

I looked at the buttoned flap. I could have held out my hand. I didn't.

She walked to the door. Halfway through, she turned.

"Not a word."

"About the thyme?"

She gave me the look she saves for duelists who forget to bow. Then the door shut. Her steps went down the first flight fast, slowed on the second, and then I couldn't hear them anymore.

* * *

Downstairs, Brandmeier would be reading my name into a silent room about now. Someone near the back would say, "Roof, Professor."

I lay back down in the shade. Far below, a groundskeeper was swearing at a wheelbarrow.

I closed my eyes and waited for the field.

It didn't come. No ash. No banners. No taste of anything. And I couldn't sleep either, which was new, and annoyed me. I put my hand on my breast pocket. Empty. I knew it was empty. A minute later I checked again anyway.

So she watered the beds. That explained the thyme and the sage. It explained why the mint hadn't taken over the wall. It didn't explain the grass. Nobody waters grass into midsummer in the ninth month.

It didn't explain the apple tree either. Looking up from underneath, I saw it. A single white blossom on the highest twig. Small, stupid, and completely out of season.

I was still staring at it when the stair door banged open.

She was breathing hard. Four flights down and four back up, fast. She held a slim black book with the Committee's bell stamped on the cover in red. And a pencil.

"I didn't get your name," she said.

"No."

"That wasn't a question."

I thought about lying. She'd find out by supper. She was, I was starting to see, a person who found things out by supper.

"Eden Wren."

The pencil stopped in the air. "Wren," she said. "*Eighth* on the Ladder Wren?"

"Most terms."

"I've never seen you in my life."

"You sit two rows in front of me in Strategy."

"I do not."

"Third seat from the window. You underline things with a ruler."

Her face did several things in a row. She opened the book and ran her finger down the columns until it stopped. She read whatever was there twice.

When she looked up, the crying girl was gone. The person in her place had a pencil.

"Eden Wren," she said. "Sixty-one absences. Tomorrow, you attend class." She shut the book. "I will see to it personally."
