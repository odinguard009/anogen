# Forty Minutes of Grass

> "Nobody looks up at a roof. That is why the wise sleep there." - Saying of the Hallowcrest groundskeepers

Professor Brandmeier took the register twice in every Spell Grammar lesson. Once at the first bell, and once forty minutes before the end, because she believed students fled at the midpoint like geese crossing a road. She wasn't wrong about the geese. It meant I had eighty minutes before anyone would bother to notice I'd missed both rolls.

Eighty minutes. Less the stairs.

The Old Observatory had five flights, and the last one was locked. I had the key. The door opened with a small cough of rust onto the only flat place on the plateau where nobody ever came.

Somebody had planted a garden up there once and then, I'd always assumed, lost interest. A dwarf apple tree in a cracked half-barrel. Three herb beds gone leggy. A brass telescope on an iron stand, its eyepiece missing, aimed at some piece of sky that had stopped being interesting eighty years ago. And grass, real grass, thick enough to swallow your hand to the wrist.

It was the last week of the ninth month. Down in the Lower Court the maples had gone the color of a scab and the groundskeepers were raking them into wet heaps. Up here the grass was green the way grass is green at midsummer, blue at the roots. The apple tree still had every leaf. I'd filed that a month ago under things that were none of my business, next to the vice-headmaster's waistcoats.

Seventy-six minutes.

I lay down in the strip of shade between the second herb bed and the parapet, laced my gloved fingers over my stomach, and was asleep before I'd finished deciding to be.

The dream starts with the smell. It always does.

Wet ash. The kind a town leaves when it's burned and then been rained on. Then the field, which runs to every horizon and has been planted with banners, black cloth on black poles, no crest on any of them, thousands. I stopped trying to count them years ago. Under each banner men stand in rank, waiting for an order.

Nobody breathes. That's where it goes wrong. I notice the soldier beside me, his chest not moving, his eyes not blinking, and then I notice that mine isn't moving either.

I try.

The air comes in thick and stops somewhere behind my teeth. It's ash, ground fine as flour, and every pull drags more of it down, into my throat, packing it, and the banners crack in a wind I can't feel, and the whole field turns its head toward me at once with a sound like wheat bending, and they're waiting for me to say it, the word, whatever the word is, and I can't, because my mouth is full and my throat is full and there's no room left in my chest for anything except gray, and gray, and

Something cried.

Thin. Wet. Furious. It went through the field like a needle through cloth, and every banner went still.

Air.

I woke with it already in my lungs, and that was the first wrong thing. Usually I woke clawing at the grass, and the taste stayed for an hour, a taste nobody else could smell. I kept a peppermint in my left glove for it. This time there was only cool grass against my cheek and the green smell of crushed thyme.

The second wrong thing was sitting a meter away.

A girl. Back against the telescope's iron stand, knees drawn up, face pressed into the crook of her elbow. Red armband on the left sleeve, worn exactly two fingers below the shoulder seam, which is where the student handbook says to wear it and where nobody wears it. Dark-gold hair pinned up so tightly it looked like a sentence being served. A tin watering can stood by her shoe, its rose still dripping onto the flagstones.

She was crying the way people cry when they've given themselves five minutes and not one second more. In hard, efficient bursts. Between them she said things into her sleeve.

"Stupid. Stupid, stupid, *stupid*."

I knew her. Everyone in Year Five knew her, the way everyone in a village knows where the wasp nest is. Lysette Hollen. Fourth on the Ladder. Vice-chair of the Discipline Committee. In our second year she had written up a boy for whistling "with intent."

She hadn't seen me. That part wasn't strange. People don't. I keep my mana folded small and flat and very still, and I've done it for so long that I no longer have to think about it; people walk past me in corridors and apologize to the wall afterward.

So I counted the options, since that's what I do.

One: lie still. She'd finish, wipe her face, water whatever she'd come to water and go. She'd never learn that a boy from her own year had watched her come apart behind a broken telescope. That was probably the kind choice. It was certainly the easy one.

Two: sit up and cough. She'd be humiliated, and she'd make me pay for it in some small, exact Committee way for the rest of the year.

There was no three, unless I counted the forty-foot drop into the rhododendrons behind me.

I lay still. I'm good at it.

Then she lifted her head and wiped her nose on the armband.

On the Committee armband. She saw what she'd done, and her mouth went crooked, and she started crying again, worse, at the armband now, as if it had betrayed her personally.

I had the handkerchief out of my breast pocket before I'd decided anything, which is not how I do things.

Linen, old, washed thin as paper. A feather stitched in silver thread in one corner, a little crooked, because my mother had done it while arguing with someone across a kitchen. I didn't lend it. I didn't use it. I carried it, and that was the whole of what it was for.

I held it out across the meter of grass.

For a second she didn't see that either.

Then she did, and she went from sitting to crouching in one motion, and there was a hand trowel in her fist, held point-down along the forearm, which told me someone had taught her properly. Her eyes were red and her face was blotched and absolutely murderous.

"How long," she said, "have you been *there*."

"Since a bit after the first roll."

"That's an hour."

"Fifty-two minutes," I said. "I was asleep for most of it."

"Most."

"The last four, I was awake."

She stared at me. I stayed on my back, holding the handkerchief up like a very small flag of truce.

"Put that away," she said.

"You've got some on your armband."

Her eyes went to her sleeve. Something happened in her jaw. She reached out and took the handkerchief between two fingers, by the corner, the way you'd lift evidence, and turned her back on me to use it.

I sat up. Slowly, so the trowel would stay where it was.

* * *

It took her a while. I looked at the apple tree so she'd have something like privacy, and counted the leaves on the nearest branch: eleven. When she turned around again her face was mostly her own, and she'd folded the handkerchief into a neat square and was holding it in both hands in front of her, like a document she meant to present.

"This roof," she said, "is closed to students."

"Then we're both in trouble."

"I'm here on Committee business."

I looked at the watering can.

"The thyme," I said, "is in breach of something?"

Her ears went red, which was interesting, because I hadn't thought there was more red available. So she was the gardener. Someone who only had the dawn and the odd stolen hour, and who'd never had the heart to cut the leggy ones back. Dirt under her nails. Two of them broken short.

"Here's what's going to happen," she said. "You saw nothing. I didn't see you. You'll go down those stairs and you won't come back up them, and I won't file a report for trespass in a sealed building. That's generous. You should know it's generous."

I did have a permit. It was in my desk, signed by the headmistress: *medical exemption*. If I showed it to Lysette Hollen she'd want to know what was medical about a roof, and then she'd want to know why the headmistress had signed for a fifth-year with an unremarkable record, and then, being who she was, she'd find out.

"I can do the first two," I said.

"And the third?"

"I sleep here."

"You can sleep in your dormitory like a human being."

"I've tried. It's loud."

"It's a *stone tower*."

"The stone's loud."

She opened her mouth and shut it again. I think she was trying to decide whether I was mocking her. I wasn't. Somebody on the fourth floor of mine practiced a wooden flute every night, the same eleven notes, and got the ninth one wrong every single time.

"Fine," she said. "You can do the first two. And if a word of this gets out, I'll know where it came from, because you're the only one who—" She stopped. "Before you start inventing reasons."

"I wasn't going to."

"Everyone invents reasons. It's what people *do*." She looked at the folded linen in her hands instead of at me, and her thumb found the feather and stopped on it. "Rowan Tallis asked Clara Belmont to the Lantern Ball. At lunch. In the middle of the atrium, in front of the whole year. That's all. That's the whole reason, so you don't need to make one up."

I knew Rowan Tallis from a distance, the way I knew most people. Second on the Ladder. The Dawn Boy, cheerful before breakfast, which should be illegal. He ate standing up. He said hello to the groundskeepers by name.

"He folded a lantern for her," she said. "Out of paper. Himself. He's hopeless at folding, he's hopeless at anything with his hands that isn't a sword, it was all lopsided, and everybody went *ahh* like it was a puppy." Her voice did something and she stopped and waited for it to finish. "And then afterward he came over and asked me if I was going. With anyone. Because he's *nice*. He's nice to everyone. He doesn't have the faintest idea, he's never had the faintest idea, and the stupid part is I don't even—"

She didn't finish that one at all.

I could have said the things people say. That he was an idiot. That there'd be other balls. None of it was any use to her.

"Did it light?" I said.

She blinked. "What?"

"The lantern. Did he get it to light?"

There was a pause.

"No," she said. "It caught fire. A little. On one side." Her mouth moved. "Clara put it out with her tea."

Then she laughed. Once, wet and short, through her nose, and looked absolutely appalled with herself for doing it, and pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth as if she could push it back in.

I didn't laugh. I don't, really. But something in my chest that had been held flat for a long time moved about a quarter of an inch, and I noticed it, and set it aside to look at later.

She looked down at the handkerchief properly then, maybe for the first time. Turned it over. Ran the edge between finger and thumb.

"This is good linen," she said.

"It was my mother's."

She went still. I watched her do the sum: *was*.

"Then why would you hand it to some girl who was—" She gestured at her own face, a quick, cross circle.

"You were using your armband."

Her chin came up. For a second I thought she was going to throw it at me. Instead she folded it again, smaller, the corners exact, and put it in the pocket of her uniform jacket and buttoned the flap over it.

"I'll wash it," she said. "And return it."

"It's fine. It's clean."

"It is *not* clean. Not now." She picked up the watering can, discovered it was empty, and set it down again with great dignity. "I'll wash it."

I looked at the buttoned flap. I could have held out my hand. I didn't.

She went to the door. Halfway through it, she turned back.

"Not a word."

"About the thyme?"

That got me a look I expect she normally reserved for duelists who'd forgotten to bow. Then the door shut, and her footsteps went down the first flight fast, and slowed on the second, and then I couldn't hear them any longer.

* * *

Downstairs, Brandmeier would be reading my name into a silence about now, and someone near the back would say "Roof, Professor."

I lay back down in the shade. Somewhere far below, a groundskeeper was swearing at a wheelbarrow.

I closed my eyes and waited for the field.

It didn't come. No ash, no banners, no taste of anything, and I couldn't sleep either, which was new and which annoyed me. I put my hand on my breast pocket. Empty. I knew it was empty. I put my hand there again a minute later anyway.

So she watered the beds. That explained the thyme and the sage and why the mint hadn't taken over the parapet. It didn't explain the grass. Nobody waters grass into midsummer in the ninth month. It didn't explain the apple tree either, which was, now that I looked at it properly from underneath, putting out a single white blossom on its highest twig, small and stupid and completely out of season.

I was still looking at it when the stair door banged open.

She was breathing hard. Four flights down and four back up, then, and fast. She had a slim black book with the Committee's bell stamped on the cover in red, and a pencil.

"I didn't get your name," she said.

"No."

"That wasn't a question."

I considered lying. She'd find out by supper. She was, I was beginning to understand, a person who found things out by supper.

"Eden Wren."

She stopped with the pencil in the air. "Wren," she said. "*Eighth* on the Ladder Wren."

"Most terms."

"I've never seen you in my life."

"You sit two rows in front of me in Strategy."

"I do not."

"Third seat from the window. You underline things with a ruler."

Her face did several things in a row. Then she opened the book and went through it, her finger running down columns, until it stopped. She read whatever was there twice. When she looked up at me, the crying girl had gone somewhere I couldn't follow, and the person in her place had a pencil.

"Eden Wren," she said. "Sixty-one absences. Tomorrow you attend class." She shut the book. "I will see to it personally."
