# The Timetable

*EDEN WREN, Year Five. Corrected schedule, effective at once.*
*Sixth bell: refectory, south tables. You will eat something.*
*Seventh bell: Spell Grammar, Hall Four. Front half of the room.*
*Ninth bell: Strategy. You may keep your seat by the window, since apparently you have one.*
*Any absence will be treated as a flight risk and pursued.*
*L. M. H., Discipline Committee (Vice-Chair)*

It slid under my door sometime before dawn. The handwriting was so upright it looked offended. By then, I'd already lost the war.

* * *

The board was where it always is.

A white floor, perfectly flat. It runs about forty paces in every direction and then just stops. Past the edge there's nothing. I mean that exactly. It isn't dark and it isn't a drop. If you stare too long, your eyes start to water.

The Tutor sat on the far side with his face turned away. He always does. I've known him since I was ten, and I couldn't tell you what his nose looks like. He's tall. He sits like a man who's been sitting for a very long time. He moves pieces with two fingers and never touches the ones he's already decided about.

"Your tower," he said.

My tower was a small stone piece in the middle of my side, ringed by nine pawns. His pieces had been closing in for an hour, dream time. I'd held the east road with three pawns. I'd lost the west to a trick I saw coming and walked into anyway.

"I see it."

"You saw it four moves ago. You moved toward the noise."

"The noise was a knight."

"The noise was a knight that could not reach you." He set a slim black piece behind my tower. "You guard a place like the place is the point. It never is. Again."

From behind me came a long, grumpy sigh. It came from a door I never look at straight on. It sounded like a cathedral settling.

"Some of us," said the Dragon, "are *trying* to sleep."

"You live in me," I said. "Pay rent."

"I *am* the rent. Every morning you wake up breathing, that's the rent." Its voice was deep enough to rattle my back teeth. It was also fussy enough for a duchess. "And while I have you. That was *good linen*. Very good linen. You gave it to a crying stranger like a used napkin."

"She was using her armband."

"Whatever that means."

The Tutor didn't look up. The two of them never talk to each other. They talk past each other, through me. Like neighbors who share a wall and have opinions about the noise.

I reset the pieces. That's when I noticed the doors.

There are doors along the edge of the board, where it meets the nothing. I don't know how many. The number changes, and I stopped counting. Most are plain. Tonight, one was locked, with a hairpin stuck in the keyhole. Silver. Thin. A little flat head like a seed. It looked like someone had picked the lock halfway and then got called away.

I stood up to look closer.

The floor under my left foot went soft.

There's no other way to say it. The edge of the board had moved while I wasn't looking. The white under my heel was thinning like spring ice. Past it was the nothing, and the nothing pulled.

It didn't feel like falling. It felt like being *erased* from the feet up. A cold started at my ankle and reached my knee before I could turn. I threw my weight back. My hand shot out for anything and caught the doorframe beside the hairpin. The silver head was an inch from my knuckles. For some reason, I couldn't look at anything else.

"Not that one," said the Tutor.

He hadn't moved. He set a white pawn on the board, and the floor under me was hard again.

"Not yet," he said. "Again."

I woke with my left leg asleep up to the hip. A sheet of paper lay on the floor by the door.

* * *

She knocked at half past the fifth bell. Three times, evenly spaced.

I opened the door. Lysette Hollen stood in the corridor of the Year Five tower. Clean armband (she had at least two). Hair pinned a degree tighter than yesterday. A copy of the timetable in her hand, in case I'd lost mine in the last forty minutes.

She looked at me. She looked past me at my room. A bed, a desk, a chair, and nothing on the walls.

"You're dressed," she said, like I'd cheated.

"I sleep dressed."

"That's—" She let it go. "South tables. Walk."

We walked. She stayed half a step ahead the whole way. So she had to look back every eleven steps or so, to check I was still there. Two second-years saw us coming and flattened themselves against the wall.

She didn't mention the handkerchief. Neither did I. Her left jacket pocket stayed buttoned.

* * *

The refectory at the sixth bell sounds like a mill.

Lysette delivered me to the south tables. She looked at me once more, like she was memorizing where she'd put me. Then she went to the Committee bench by the windows. From there, she had a clear view of me.

Felix Ashgrove sat at the end of our usual table. Porridge in front of him, a book propped against the salt. He didn't look up when I sat. Felix never does, for the first minute. That's how he says hello.

"Eaten today?" he said.

"Technically."

He turned a page. "What does technically mean today?"

"A peppermint."

"That's not food."

"It's in the food family."

He pushed the bread basket two inches toward me, eyes still on his book. Then he looked up. Over my shoulder, at the Committee bench.

"Ah," he said.

"Yes."

"She's watching you eat."

"She's making sure I eat."

"Eden. What did you do?"

"I offered someone a handkerchief."

Felix put his book down. He only does that for fires and exam results. He's sat next to me since the first week of Year One. He walked up to the only empty seat in the hall, the one beside me. He asked if it was taken. Then he sat before I answered.

In five years, he's never once asked where I go at night. Some mornings I'm sure he knows I went somewhere. He just pushes the bread over.

His tea had gone cold while he read. I took the pot and filled his cup to a finger below the rim. Half a spoon of honey. I pushed it back. He drinks it too sweet for a guy his size, and he's embarrassed about it.

"Thanks," he said. He picked up his book again. He didn't ask about the handkerchief.

That was the whole conversation. I ate two rolls.

* * *

Hall Four is a steep half-bowl of benches facing a slate wall. Professor Ilse Brandmeier stands at the bottom of it. She looks like someone who fell in a pit and is waiting for you to climb down.

I'd planned to sit in the back third. But Lysette had a seat ready in the front half, exactly as written. Her bag was on it. She moved the bag when I came. She didn't look at me.

Brandmeier called the first roll. When she got to my name, I said, "Here." The whole hall went quiet in a way I didn't like.

"*Who?*" somebody said behind me. Too loud.

Brandmeier took off her glasses. She looked at me over the place where they'd been. She put them back on and made a mark.

"How nice," she said. "A sighting."

The lesson was siege wards. She chalked a sentence on the slate as she talked. A guard-ward, the kind they carve into gatehouses on the Southern Wall. Old grammar. A subject, a verb, and a long clause naming what's being protected.

Her chalk was mixed with vein-stone dust. When she finished each line, the letters pulled a little mana from the air. They lifted off the slate with a faint blue glow. Live.

"Wards are sentences," she said. "A sentence with a mistake is a lie. A gatehouse built on a lie falls on the people inside." She drew the last stroke. "This one has a mistake. A small one."

The letters began to hum.

It was a low sound, almost too low to hear. You felt it in your back teeth. A thin curl of smoke rose from the third line. The front row leaned back as one. Somebody's inkpot walked an inch across the bench on its own.

Brandmeier didn't turn. She looked up into the benches, all the way up, like a hawk checking a field. She found me in the front half, where I'd never been.

"Mr. Wren," she said. "Your absence has been so steady this year that the Bursar asked if you'd died. So tell me. Where is the mistake?"

The hum climbed. The smoke from the third line thickened and drifted toward the second.

Brandmeier knew what she was doing. She'd done it for thirty years and never lost a student. She'd let it run until I panicked. Then she'd fix it with one stroke and let the room enjoy me.

I could've panicked. It would've been easy. Rank eight, a nice middle answer. *Maybe the verb tense, Professor.* Nobody remembers a middle answer.

Next to me, Lysette had gone very still. Her pencil hung above her page.

"The ward has no subject, Professor."

Six words. And they were true. She'd written a sentence that kept, and guarded, and held. It never said what. A ward like that has nothing to point at. So it points at itself.

Brandmeier stared at me. It felt long.

Then, without turning, she reached back. She drew one short word into the gap at the front of the sentence. The hum stopped. The smoke thinned and vanished. The inkpot stayed where it was.

"Yes," she said. She sounded annoyed. "Write that down, all of you. Mr. Wren will not be repeating it."

You can feel a room decide it dislikes you. It's like a change in air pressure. I'd spent five years never being the guy in lecture who knew the answer. In four seconds, I'd become him. Nobody near me looked my way for the rest of the hour.

Lysette underlined something with her ruler. So hard that the page tore a little at the end.

* * *

After Strategy, the corridor outside the west lecture halls was packed. It always is at the ninth bell and a half. My bag hung off one shoulder. Lysette walked half a step ahead and a bit to the left, steering me toward the stairs like a tugboat with a barge.

Valerius Aurenmark stood at the top of them.

He wasn't waiting for me. He was talking to two Steward Council officers, a stack of papers in his hand. A bronze pin of the Ten on his collar. Nothing else on him said *Crown Prince*, except the empty space people kept around him without being told.

He'd heard about Spell Grammar. I saw it land when he turned his head and found me.

He sent the officers off with a nod. They went.

"Hollen," he said to Lysette. Polite.

"Your Highness."

Then he looked at me. Valerius looks at a person like he's reading a contract and just found the clause he was afraid of.

"Brandmeier," he said. "A ward with no subject."

"It was smoking."

"I know it was smoking. Half the year is talking about it." He stepped closer. Not a threat. He doesn't do threats. "Six words. In front of a hundred and twenty people."

I could feel Lysette looking from him to me and back. She was doing a sum, and she didn't have the numbers yet.

"So," Valerius said, quietly, so only the three of us could hear. "You've finally stopped hiding?"

I had a few honest answers. Every one cost something I wasn't going to pay in a corridor at the ninth bell and a half. Not with the Discipline vice-chair at my elbow and her pencil already out.

"I'm on a timetable," I said.

Something shut in his face. Like a man closing a ledger he's tired of reading. He held my eyes one more second.

"Pity," he said.

He stepped aside to let us pass. He was back to his papers before we reached the second stair.

Lysette said nothing all the way down. At the bottom, she stopped and opened her black book. She wrote one line. She tilted it so I couldn't read it. Not quite enough.

*Why does the Prince know him?*
