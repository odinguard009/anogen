# The Timetable

*EDEN WREN, Year Five. Corrected schedule, effective at once.*
*Sixth bell: refectory, south tables. You will eat something.*
*Seventh bell: Spell Grammar, Hall Four. Front half of the room.*
*Ninth bell: Strategy. You may keep your seat by the window, since apparently you have one.*
*Any absence will be treated as a flight risk and pursued.*
*L. M. H., Discipline Committee (Vice-Chair)*

It came under my door sometime before dawn, written in a hand so upright it looked offended. By then I'd already lost the war.

* * *

The board was where it always is. A white floor, perfectly flat, that runs for about forty paces in every direction and then simply stops. Past the edge there's nothing, and I mean that exactly. There's no dark and no drop. Looking at it too long makes your eyes try to water.

The Tutor sat on the far side of the board with his face turned away from me. He always does. I've known him since I was ten, and I couldn't tell you his nose. He's tall, he sits like a man who's been sitting for a very long time, and he moves the pieces with two fingers, never touching the ones he's already decided about.

"Your tower," he said.

My tower was a small stone piece in the middle of my side, ringed by nine pawns. His pieces had been closing on it for an hour, by dream time. I'd held the east road with three pawns and lost the west to a feint I'd seen coming and moved toward anyway.

"I see it."

"You saw it four moves ago. You moved toward the noise."

"The noise was a knight."

"The noise was a knight that could not reach you." He set a slim black piece on the square behind my tower. "You defend a place as if the place were the point. It never is. Again."

From somewhere behind me, behind the wall of the board, through a door I never look at directly, came a long, aggrieved exhalation. Like a cathedral settling.

"Some of us," said the Dragon, "are *trying* to sleep."

"You live in me," I said. "Pay rent."

"I am the rent. Every day you wake up breathing is the rent." Its voice was deep enough to rattle my back teeth and petulant enough to belong to a duchess. "And while I have you, that was *good linen*. Very good linen. You hand it to a weeping stranger like a used napkin."

"She was using her armband."

"Whatever that means."

The Tutor didn't look up. The two of them never speak to each other. They speak past each other, through me, like neighbors who share a wall and have opinions about the noise.

I reset the pieces. While I did it, I noticed the doors.

There are doors along the edge of the board where it meets the nothing. I don't know how many; the number changes and I've stopped counting. Most are plain. One, tonight, was locked, and something was stuck in its keyhole: a hairpin. Silver, thin, with a little flat head like a seed. As if someone had picked the lock halfway and been called away.

I stood up to look closer.

The floor under my left foot went soft.

There isn't another way to say it. The edge of the board had moved, quietly, while I wasn't watching, and the white under my heel was thinning like ice in spring, and past it was the nothing, and the nothing pulled. It didn't feel like falling. It felt like being *erased* from the feet up, a cold that started at the ankle and was at my knee before I'd turned around. I threw my weight back. My hand went out for anything and closed on the doorframe beside the hairpin, and the silver head of the pin was an inch from my knuckles, and for some reason that was all I could look at.

"Not that one," said the Tutor.

He hadn't moved. He set a white pawn down on the board, and the floor under me was hard again.

"Not yet," he said. "Again."

I woke with my left leg asleep to the hip and a sheet of paper on the floor by the door.

* * *

She knocked at the fifth bell and a half. Three times, evenly spaced.

When I opened the door, Lysette Hollen was standing in the corridor of the Year Five tower with her armband on (a clean one; she had at least two), her hair pinned up a degree tighter than yesterday, and a copy of the timetable in her hand in case I'd lost mine in the last forty minutes.

She looked at me. She looked past me at my room, which had a bed, a desk, a chair, and nothing on the walls.

"You're dressed," she said, as if I'd cheated.

"I sleep dressed."

"That's—" She let that one go. "South tables. Walk."

We walked. She was half a step ahead the whole way, which meant she had to keep looking back to make sure I was still there, which she did every eleven steps or so. Two second-years saw us coming and flattened themselves against the wall.

She didn't mention the handkerchief. I didn't either. Her left jacket pocket was buttoned.

* * *

The refectory at the sixth bell sounds like a mill. Lysette delivered me to the south tables, looked at me once more as though memorizing where she'd put me, and went to the Committee bench by the windows, from which she had a clean line of sight.

Felix Ashgrove was at the end of our usual table with a bowl of porridge and a book propped against the salt cellar. He didn't look up when I sat down. Felix doesn't, for the first minute; it's how he says hello.

"Eaten today?" he said.

"Technically."

He turned a page. "What does technically mean, today?"

"A peppermint."

"That's not food."

"It's in the food family."

He pushed the bread basket two inches toward me without taking his eyes off the book. Then he did look up, and over my shoulder, at the Committee bench.

"Ah," he said.

"Yes."

"She's watching you eat."

"She's ensuring I eat."

"Eden, what did you do?"

"I offered someone a handkerchief."

Felix set his book down, which he only does for fires and exam results. He's been sitting next to me since the first week of Year One, when he walked up to the only empty seat in the hall, which was the one beside me, and asked if it was taken, and then sat down before I'd answered. For five years he's never once asked me where I go at night. Some mornings I'm fairly sure he knows I've been somewhere. He just pushes the bread over.

His tea had gone cold while he read. I took the pot, topped his cup to a finger below the rim, put in half a spoon of honey, and pushed it back. He drinks it too sweet for a person his size and he's embarrassed about it.

"Thanks," he said, and picked up his book again, and didn't ask about the handkerchief, and that was the whole conversation, and I ate two rolls.

* * *

Hall Four is a steep half-bowl of benches facing a slate wall, and Professor Ilse Brandmeier stands at the bottom of it like something that's fallen into a pit and is waiting for you to come down.

I'd meant to sit in the back third. Lysette had a seat waiting in the front half, exactly as written, with her bag on it. She moved the bag when I arrived. She didn't look at me.

Brandmeier called the first roll. When she reached my name, I said "Here," and the hall went quiet in a way I didn't care for. Somebody behind me said "*Who?*" too loudly. Brandmeier took her spectacles off, looked at me over the top of where they'd been, put them back on, and made a mark.

"How nice," she said. "A sighting."

The lesson was siege wards. She chalked the sentence on the slate as she talked: a guard-ward of the kind they cut into gatehouses on the Southern Wall, the old grammar, subject and verb and the long clause that names what's being kept. She didn't use ordinary chalk. Hers was cut with vein-stone dust, and as she finished each line the letters took a little mana from the air and sat up off the slate with a faint blue edge, live.

"Wards," she said, "are sentences. A sentence with an error is a lie. A gatehouse built on a lie falls on the people inside it." She drew the last stroke. "This one has an error. A small one."

The letters on the slate began to hum.

It was a low sound, below hearing almost, the kind you feel in your molars. A thin curl of smoke came off the third line. The front row leaned back as one person. Somebody's inkpot walked an inch across the bench on its own.

Brandmeier didn't turn around. She looked up into the benches, all the way up, the way a hawk looks at a field, and found me in the front half where I'd never been.

"Mr. Wren," she said. "Whose absence has been so consistent this year that the Bursar asked whether you'd died. Where is the error?"

The hum climbed. The smoke from the third line thickened and went sideways toward the second. Brandmeier knew exactly what she was doing; she'd been doing it for thirty years and she'd never lost a student. She'd let it run until I flailed, then fix it with one stroke and let the room enjoy me.

I could have flailed. It would've been easy. Rank eight, a good, middling answer, *perhaps the verb tense, Professor*. Nobody remembers a middling answer.

Lysette, beside me, had gone very still, her pencil stopped above her page.

"The ward has no subject, Professor."

Six words. It was true. She'd written a sentence that kept, and guarded, and held, and never said what. A ward like that has nothing to point at, so it points at itself.

Brandmeier looked at me for a long time. Then, without turning, she reached back and drew one short word into the gap at the head of the sentence, and the hum stopped, and the smoke thinned out and went, and the inkpot stayed where it was.

"Yes," she said. She sounded put out. "Write that down, all of you. Mr. Wren will not be repeating it."

You can feel a room decide it dislikes you. It's like a change in pressure. I'd worked five years to never be the person in a lecture who knew the answer, and in about four seconds I'd become him, and nobody near me looked at me again for the rest of the hour.

Lysette underlined something with her ruler. Hard enough to tear the page a little at the end.

* * *

After Strategy, the corridor outside the west lecture halls was full, the way it always is at the ninth bell and a half. I had my bag on one shoulder and Lysette half a step ahead and slightly to the left, steering me toward the stairs like a tug with a barge.

Valerius Aurenmark was standing at the top of them.

He wasn't waiting for me. He was talking to two Steward Council officers, holding a sheaf of papers, the bronze pin of the Ten on his collar and nothing else on him to say *Crown Prince* except the way people had left a space around him without being told to. He'd heard about Spell Grammar. I could see it land when he turned his head and found me.

He dismissed the two officers with a nod. They went.

"Hollen," he said, to Lysette. Polite.

"Your Highness."

Then he looked at me. Valerius has a way of looking at a person as if he's reading a contract and has found the clause he was worried about.

"Brandmeier," he said. "A ward with no subject."

"It was smoking."

"I'm aware it was smoking. Half the year is talking about it." He stepped a little closer. Not a threat. He doesn't do threats. "Six words in front of a hundred and twenty people."

I could feel Lysette looking from him to me and back, doing a sum she didn't have the numbers for yet.

"So," said Valerius, quietly enough that only the three of us heard it, "you've finally stopped hiding?"

There were a few honest answers. Every one of them cost something I wasn't willing to pay in a corridor at the ninth bell and a half, with the vice-chair of the Discipline Committee at my elbow and her pencil already out.

"I'm on a timetable," I said.

Something shut in his face, the way a man closes a ledger he's tired of reading. He held my eye for one more second.

"Pity," he said, and stepped aside to let us pass, and turned back to his papers before we reached the second stair.

Lysette didn't say anything all the way down. At the bottom, she stopped, opened her black book, and wrote a single line. She angled it so I couldn't read it, but not quite well enough.

*Why does the Prince know him?*
