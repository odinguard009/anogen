# The Timetable

*EDEN WREN, Year Five. Corrected schedule, effective at once.*
*Sixth bell: refectory, south tables. You will eat something.*
*Seventh bell: Spell Grammar, Hall Four. Front half of the room.*
*Ninth bell: Strategy. You may keep your seat by the window, since apparently you have one.*
*Any absence will be treated as a flight risk and pursued.*
*L. M. H., Discipline Committee (Vice-Chair)*

It slid under my door before dawn. The handwriting was so straight it looked angry. By then, I had already lost the war.

* * *

The board was where it always is.

It is a white floor, perfectly flat. It goes about forty steps in every direction and then it stops. Past the edge there is nothing. I mean that exactly. It is not dark, and it is not a drop. If you stare too long, your eyes start to water.

The Tutor sat on the far side with his face turned away. He always does. I have known him since I was ten, and I could not tell you what his nose looks like. He is tall. He sits as if he has sat for a very long time. He moves pieces with two fingers. He never touches the ones he has already decided about.

"Your tower," he said.

My tower was a small stone piece in the middle of my side. Nine pawns stood around it. His pieces had been closing in for an hour, in dream time. I had held the east road with three pawns. I had lost the west to a trick I saw coming and walked into anyway.

"I see it."

"You saw it four moves ago. You moved toward the noise."

"The noise was a knight."

"The noise was a knight that could not reach you." He put a thin black piece behind my tower. "You guard a place as if the place is the point. It never is. Again."

A long, grumpy sigh came from behind me. It came from a door I never look at straight on. It sounded as if a big old church was settling in the night.

"Some of us," said the Dragon, "are *trying* to sleep."

"You live in me," I said. "Pay rent."

"I *am* the rent. Every morning you wake up breathing, that is the rent." Its voice was deep enough to shake my back teeth. It was also fussy, as a rich old lady is fussy. "And while I have you. That was *good linen*. Very good linen. You gave it to a crying stranger like old paper."

"She was using her armband."

"Whatever that means."

The Tutor did not look up. The two of them never talk to each other. They talk past each other, through me. They are like neighbors who share a wall and complain about the noise.

I set the pieces up again. That is when I saw the doors.

There are doors along the edge of the board, where it meets the nothing. I don't know how many. The number changes, and I stopped counting. Most are plain. Tonight one was locked, with a hairpin stuck in the keyhole. It was silver and thin, with a small flat head, shaped like a seed. It looked like someone had started to pick the lock and then got called away.

I stood up to look closer.

The floor under my left foot went soft.

There is no other way to say it. The edge of the board had moved while I was not looking. The white under my heel was getting thin, like ice in spring. Past it was the nothing, and the nothing pulled.

It did not feel like falling. It felt like being *erased* from the feet up. A cold started at my ankle and reached my knee before I could turn. I threw my weight back. My hand shot out and caught the doorframe next to the hairpin. The silver head was an inch from my knuckles. I could not look at anything else.

"Not that one," said the Tutor.

He had not moved. He put a white pawn on the board, and the floor under me was hard again.

"Not yet," he said. "Again."

I woke with my left leg asleep up to the hip. A sheet of paper lay on the floor by the door.

* * *

She knocked at half past the fifth bell. Three knocks, evenly spaced.

I opened the door. Lysette Hollen stood in the hall of the Year Five tower. She wore a clean armband. She had at least two. Her hair was pinned a little tighter than yesterday. She held a copy of the timetable, in case I lost mine in the last forty minutes.

She looked at me. She looked past me at my room. There was a bed, a desk, a chair, and nothing on the walls.

"You're dressed," she said, as if I had cheated.

"I sleep dressed."

"That's—" She let it go. "South tables. Walk."

We walked. She stayed half a step ahead the whole way. So she had to look back every eleven steps to check I was still there. Two second-years saw us coming and pressed themselves against the wall.

She did not mention the handkerchief. Neither did I. Her left jacket pocket stayed buttoned.

* * *

The refectory at the sixth bell sounds like a mill.

Lysette brought me to the south tables. She looked at me once more, as if she wanted to remember where she had put me. Then she went to the Committee bench by the windows. From there, she could see me clearly.

Felix Ashgrove sat at the end of our usual table. He had porridge in front of him and a book against the salt. He did not look up when I sat down. Felix never does, for the first minute. That is how he says hello.

"Eaten today?" he said.

"Technically."

He turned a page. "What does technically mean today?"

"A peppermint."

"That's not food."

"It's in the food family."

He pushed the bread basket two inches toward me. His eyes stayed on the book. Then he looked up. He looked over my shoulder at the Committee bench.

"Ah," he said.

"Yes."

"She's watching you eat."

"She's making sure I eat."

"Eden. What did you do?"

"I offered someone a handkerchief."

Felix put his book down. He only does that for fires and test results. He has sat next to me since the first week of Year One. He walked up to the only empty seat in the hall, the one beside me. He asked if it was taken. Then he sat down before I answered.

In five years, he has never asked where I go at night. Some mornings I am sure he knows I went somewhere. He just pushes the bread over.

His tea had gone cold while he read. I took the pot and filled his cup to one finger below the top. I added half a spoon of honey. I pushed it back. He drinks it too sweet for a boy his size, and he is embarrassed about it.

"Thanks," he said. He picked up his book again. He did not ask about the handkerchief.

That was the whole talk. I ate two rolls.

* * *

Hall Four is a steep half-bowl of benches that face a slate wall. Professor Ilse Brandmeier stands at the bottom. She looks as if she fell in a pit and is waiting for you to climb down.

I had planned to sit in the back third. But Lysette had a seat ready in the front half, exactly as written. Her bag was on it. She moved the bag when I came. She did not look at me.

Brandmeier called the first name list. When she got to my name, I said, "Here." The whole hall went quiet in a way I did not like.

"*Who?*" someone said behind me. Too loud.

Brandmeier took off her glasses. She looked at me over the place where they had been. She put them back on and made a mark.

"How nice," she said. "A sighting."

The lesson was about siege wards. A ward is a spell that guards a place. She wrote a sentence on the slate as she talked. It was a guard-ward, the kind carved into gatehouses on the Southern Wall. It used old grammar. It had a subject, a verb, and a long part that named what the ward protects.

Her chalk was mixed with vein-stone dust. When she finished each line, the letters pulled a little mana from the air. They lifted off the slate with a faint blue glow. They were alive.

"Wards are sentences," she said. "A sentence with a mistake is a lie. A gatehouse built on a lie falls on the people inside." She drew the last line. "This one has a mistake. A small one."

The letters began to hum.

It was a low sound, almost too low to hear. You felt it in your back teeth. A thin curl of smoke rose from the third line. The front row leaned back together. Someone's inkpot moved an inch across the bench by itself.

Brandmeier did not turn. She looked up into the benches, all the way to the top, as a hawk checks a field. She found me in the front half, where I had never sat.

"Mr. Wren," she said. "You have been away so often this year that the Bursar asked if you had died. So tell me. Where is the mistake?"

The hum got louder. The smoke from the third line grew thick and drifted toward the second.

Brandmeier knew what she was doing. She had done it for thirty years and never lost a student. She would let it run until I panicked. Then she would fix it with one stroke and let the room enjoy me.

I could have panicked. It would have been easy. I was rank eight. A nice middle answer was ready. *Maybe the verb tense, Professor.* No one remembers a middle answer.

Next to me, Lysette had gone very still. Her pencil hung above her page.

"The ward has no subject, Professor."

Six words. And they were true. She had written a sentence that kept, and guarded, and held. It never said what. A ward like that has nothing to point at. So it points at itself.

Brandmeier stared at me. It felt long.

Then, without turning, she reached back. She drew one short word into the gap at the front of the sentence. The hum stopped. The smoke got thin and was gone. The inkpot stayed where it was.

"Yes," she said. She sounded annoyed. "Write that down, all of you. Mr. Wren will not say it again."

You can feel a room decide it dislikes you. It feels like the air changes. For five years, I had never been the boy in class who knew the answer. In four seconds, I became him. No one near me looked my way for the rest of the hour.

Lysette underlined something with her ruler. She did it so hard that the page tore a little at the end.

* * *

After Strategy, the hall outside the west classrooms was full. It always is at the half past ninth bell. My bag hung off one shoulder. Lysette walked half a step ahead and a little to the left. She pushed me toward the stairs as if she were a small boat pulling a big one.

Valerius Aurenmark stood at the top of them.

He was not waiting for me. He was talking to two Steward Council officers, with a stack of papers in his hand. A bronze pin of the Ten shone on his collar. Nothing else on him said *Crown Prince*, except the empty space people kept around him without being told.

He had heard about Spell Grammar. I saw it hit him when he turned his head and found me.

He sent the officers away with a nod. They went.

"Hollen," he said to Lysette. He was polite.

"Your Highness."

Then he looked at me. Valerius looks at a person as if he is reading a contract and just found the part he feared.

"Brandmeier," he said. "A ward with no subject."

"It was smoking."

"I know it was smoking. Half the year is talking about it." He stepped closer. It was not a threat. He does not make threats. "Six words. In front of a hundred and twenty people."

I could feel Lysette looking from him to me and back. She was doing a sum, and she did not have all the numbers yet.

"So," Valerius said, quietly, so only the three of us could hear. "You have finally stopped hiding?"

I had a few honest answers. Each one cost something I would not pay in a hall at half past the ninth bell. Not with the Discipline vice-chair at my elbow and her pencil already out.

"I'm on a timetable," I said.

Something closed in his face, as a man shuts a book he is tired of reading. He held my eyes one more second.

"Pity," he said.

He stepped aside to let us pass. He was back to his papers before we reached the second step.

Lysette said nothing all the way down. At the bottom, she stopped and opened her black book. She wrote one line. She tilted the book so I could not read it. Not quite enough.

*Why does the Prince know him?*
