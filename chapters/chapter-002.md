# The Timetable

*EDEN WREN. Corrected schedule, effective at once.*
*Sixth bell: refectory, south tables. You will eat something.*
*Seventh bell: Spell Grammar. Front half of the room.*
*Ninth bell: Strategy. You may keep your seat by the window, since apparently you have one.*
*Any absence will be treated as a flight risk and pursued.*
*L. M. H., Committee (Vice-Chair)*

It slid under my door before dawn. The handwriting was so straight it looked angry. By then, I had already lost the war.

* * *

The board was where it always is.

It is a white floor, perfectly flat. It goes about forty steps in every direction and then it stops. Past the edge there is nothing. I mean that exactly. It is not dark and it is not a drop, and if you stare too long, your eyes start to water. The air is cool and has no smell at all.

The Tutor sat on the far side with his face turned away. He always does. I have known him since I was ten, and I could not tell you what his nose looks like. He is tall and thin. He sits as if he has sat for a very long time, and he moves the pieces with two fingers. He never touches the ones he has already decided about.

"Your tower," he said.

My tower was a small stone piece in the middle of my side, with nine pawns around it. His pieces had been closing in for an hour, in dream time. I had held the east road with three pawns. I had lost the west to a trick I saw coming and walked into anyway.

"I see it."

"You saw it four moves ago. You moved toward the noise."

"The noise was a knight."

"The noise was a knight that could not reach you." He put a thin black piece behind my tower. "You guard a place as if the place is the point. It never is. Again."

A long, grumpy sigh came from behind me. It came from a door I never look at straight on. It sounded as if a big old church were settling in the night.

"Some of us," said the Dragon, "are *trying* to sleep."

"You live in me," I said. "Pay rent."

"I *am* the rent. Every morning you wake up breathing, that is the rent." Its voice was deep enough to shake my back teeth. It was also fussy, like a rich old lady. "And while I have you. That was *good linen*. You gave it to a crying stranger like old paper."

"She was using her armband."

"Whatever that means."

The Tutor did not look up. The two of them never talk to each other. They talk past each other, through me, like neighbors who share a wall and complain about the noise.

I set the pieces up again. That is when I saw the doors.

They stand along the edge of the board, where it meets the nothing. I don't know how many. The number changes, and I stopped counting. Most are plain. Tonight one was locked, with a hairpin stuck in the keyhole. The pin was silver and thin, with a small flat head shaped like a seed. It looked like someone had started to pick the lock and then got called away.

I stood up to look closer.

The floor under my left foot went soft.

There is no other way to say it. The edge of the board had moved while I was not looking. The white under my heel was getting thin, like ice in spring. Past it was the nothing, and the nothing pulled.

It did not feel like falling. It felt like being rubbed out, starting from the feet. A cold began at my ankle and reached my knee before I could turn. I threw my weight back. My hand shot out and caught the doorframe next to the hairpin. The silver head was an inch from my knuckles, and I could not look at anything else.

"Not that one," said the Tutor.

He had not moved. He put a white pawn on the board, and the floor under me was hard again.

"Not yet," he said. "Again."

I woke with my left leg asleep up to the hip. A sheet of paper lay on the floor by the door.

* * *

She knocked at half past the fifth bell. Three knocks, evenly spaced.

I opened the door. The hall of my tower was gray and cold, and it smelled of wet stone. Lysette Hollen stood in it with a clean armband on her sleeve. Her hair was pinned a little tighter than yesterday.

"I put a timetable under your door," she said. "Did you read it?"

"Twice."

"I brought another one. In case you lost yours in the last forty minutes."

She held it out. Then she looked past me at my room. There was a bed, a desk, a chair, and nothing on the walls.

"You're dressed," she said, as if I had cheated.

"I sleep dressed."

"That's—" She let it go. "South tables. Walk."

We walked. She stayed half a step ahead, so she had to look back every eleven steps to check I was still there. Two second-years saw us coming and pressed themselves against the wall.

"Do they always do that?" I asked.

"Do what?"

"Never mind."

She did not mention the handkerchief. Neither did I. Her left jacket pocket stayed buttoned.

* * *

The dining hall at the sixth bell is loud as a mill. Plates bang, benches scrape, and the whole room smells of hot porridge and burnt toast.

Lysette brought me to the south tables. She looked at me once more, as if she wanted to remember where she had put me. Then she went to the Committee bench by the windows. From there, she could see me clearly.

Felix Ashgrove sat at the end of our usual table. He was a round-faced boy with brown hair that never lay flat. He had porridge in front of him and a book against the salt. He did not look up when I sat down. Felix never does, for the first minute. That is how he says hello.

"Eaten today?" he said.

"Technically."

He turned a page. "What does technically mean today?"

"A peppermint."

"That's not food."

"It's in the food family."

He pushed the bread basket two inches toward me. His eyes stayed on the book. Then he looked up, over my shoulder, at the Committee bench.

"Ah," he said.

"Yes."

"She's watching you eat."

"She's making sure I eat."

"Eden. What did you do?"

"I offered someone a handkerchief."

Felix put his book down. He only does that for fires and test results. He has sat next to me since our first week at school. He walked up to the only empty seat in the hall, the one beside me, and asked if it was taken. Then he sat down before I answered.

In five years, he has never asked where I go at night. Some mornings I am sure he knows I went somewhere. He just pushes the bread over.

His tea had gone cold while he read. I took the pot and filled his cup to one finger below the top. I added half a spoon of honey and pushed it back. He drinks it too sweet for a boy his size, and he is shy about it.

"Thanks," he said. He picked up his book again. He did not ask about the handkerchief.

"You're eating," Felix said, after a while.

"She's watching."

"Keep going, then."

That was the whole talk. I ate two rolls.

* * *

Spell Grammar is taught in a steep half-bowl of benches that face a big slate wall. Professor Brandmeier stands at the bottom. She is short, with gray hair in a tight knot, and she looks as if she fell into a pit and is waiting for you to climb down.

I had planned to sit in the back third. But Lysette had a seat ready in the front half, exactly as written. Her bag was on it. She moved the bag when I came. She did not look at me.

Brandmeier called the names. When she got to mine, I said, "Here." The whole room went quiet in a way I did not like.

"*Who?*" someone said behind me. Too loud.

Brandmeier took off her glasses. She looked at me over the place where they had been. She put them back on and made a mark.

"How nice," she said. "A sighting."

The lesson was about siege wards. A ward is a spell that guards a place. It is a lock made of words. She wrote a sentence on the slate as she talked. It was a guard-ward, the kind carved into gate towers. It had a subject, a verb, and a long part that named what the ward protects.

Her chalk was mixed with stone dust. When she finished each line, the letters pulled a little mana out of the air and lifted off the slate with a faint blue glow. They were alive.

"Listen," she said. "Wards are sentences. "A sentence with a mistake is a lie. A gatehouse built on a lie falls on the people inside." She drew the last line. "This one has a mistake. A small one."

The letters began to hum.

It was a low sound, almost too low to hear. You felt it in your back teeth. A thin curl of smoke rose from the third line. The front row leaned back together. Someone's inkpot slid an inch across the bench by itself.

Brandmeier did not turn. She looked up into the benches, all the way to the top, the way a hawk checks a field. She found me in the front half, where I had never sat.

"Mr. Wren," she said. "You have been away so often this year that the office asked if you had died. So tell me. Where is the mistake?"

"Mr. Wren," she said again. "The smoke is moving. Quickly, please."

She knew what she was doing. She would let it run until I panicked. Then she would fix it with one stroke and let the room enjoy me.

I could have panicked. It would have been easy. I was eighth on the Ladder, and a nice middle answer was ready. *Maybe the verb tense, Professor.* No one remembers a middle answer.

Next to me, Lysette had gone very still. Her pencil hung above her page.

I looked at the ward again. The sentence said *keep*, and *guard*, and *hold*. It never said what. A ward like that has nothing to point at, so it points at itself. I did not want to say so. But if I stayed quiet, the smoke would reach the second line and the front row would be hurt.

"The ward has no subject, Professor."

Six words. And they were true.

Brandmeier stared at me. It felt long.

Then, without turning, she reached back and drew one short word into the gap at the front of the sentence. The hum stopped. The smoke got thin and was gone. The inkpot stayed where it was.

"Yes," she said. She sounded annoyed. "Write that down, all of you. Mr. Wren will not say it again."

You can feel a room decide it dislikes you. The air changes. For five years, I had never been the boy in class who knew the answer. In four seconds, I became him. No one near me looked my way for the rest of the hour.

Lysette underlined something with her ruler. She did it so hard that the page tore.

"You knew that," she whispered.

"I guessed."

"You do not guess. You *sleep*."

"Both," I said.

* * *

After Strategy, the hall outside the west classrooms was full. It always is at half past the ninth bell. My bag hung off one shoulder. Lysette walked half a step ahead and a little to the left, and she pulled me toward the stairs.

Valerius Aurenmark stood at the top of them.

He was not waiting for me. He was talking to two officers of the school council, with a stack of papers in his hand. He was tall, with fair hair and a straight back. A small bronze pin shone on his collar. Nothing else on him said *Crown Prince*, except the empty space people kept around him without being told.

He had heard about Spell Grammar. I saw it hit him when he turned his head and found me. He sent the officers away with a nod, and they went.

"Hollen," he said to Lysette. He was polite.

"Your Highness."

Then he looked at me. Valerius looks at a person as if he is reading a contract and just found the part he feared.

"Brandmeier," he said. "A ward with no subject."

"It was smoking."

"I know it was smoking. Half the year is talking about it." He stepped closer. It was not a threat. He does not make threats. "Six words. In front of a hundred and twenty people."

I could feel Lysette looking from him to me and back. She was doing a sum, and she did not have all the numbers yet.

"So," Valerius said, quietly, so only the three of us could hear. "You have finally stopped hiding?"

I had a few honest answers. Each one cost something I would not pay in a crowded hall, with the Committee vice-chair at my elbow and her pencil already out.

"I'm on a timetable," I said.

Something closed in his face. He looked tired. He held my eyes one more second.

"Pity," he said.

He stepped aside to let us pass. He was back to his papers before we reached the second step.

"What was that about?" Lysette asked, on the stairs.

"He's a prince. He talks to everyone."

"He doesn't talk to *everyone*."

I said nothing. At the bottom, she stopped and opened her black book. She wrote one line. She tilted the book so I could not read it. Not quite enough.

*Why does the Prince know him?*
