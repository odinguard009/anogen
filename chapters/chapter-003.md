# Short Shadows

The tax clerk bought the same thing every night. One candied pear on a stick, from the same stall, from the same old woman. He paid with a silver quarter. Then he waited for change he did not need.

For three nights, I had watched him do it. For three nights, the old woman's cat got up and left when he came close. It was a fat, striped cat that slept in her coin box.

On the fourth night, Ysolde Lisle put the file in front of me. "Well?"

"The cat knows," I said.

"I can't file a cat."

We sat in the back room of a bookbinder's shop on Quillwright Lane, in Solmarch. The shop closed at sundown. On paper, it belonged to a widow in the south who had never been north of the Brennoch line. The room smelled of glue and cold tea.

Ysolde kept her coat on, as always. She is always about to leave. She is the King's secretary. She is also the person who slides envelopes under bookbinders' doors. Then she wants paperwork for what happens next.

The file said: *Emeric Fauconier. Thirty-four. Junior assessor, Crown Revenue Office, Lantern Ward. Unmarried. No debts. Clean.*

A list was pinned to the back. It showed the homes he had checked this month. Three were Hallowcrest families. One was the Belmonts.

"He changed four weeks ago," Ysolde said. "His landlady says he stopped humming. The man at the next desk says he got better at sums."

"People do that."

"Not at thirty-four." She turned her teacup a quarter turn on the saucer. That is what she does instead of sighing. "I want this quiet. The Golden Seal has six investigators in Lantern Ward this week. Each of them would give a finger to see your mask."

"Which finger?"

"Eden."

"I only want to know which one they would miss least."

She looked at me. Ysolde's face, like mine, does not do much. On her it looks planned.

"And your curfew," she said. "I hear you have a supervisor now."

I did not ask how she knew. "She sleeps at half past the tenth bell. She checks my door at the tenth."

"Then be back by the sixth. And the Bell Assessment is in nine days. Three tones, like always. Not three and a half."

Last year, I put my palm on the big bronze bell in the atrium. I pushed just enough for three clear tones. Then I lifted my hand. In the quiet after, so faint I thought it was my pulse, the bell rang a fourth time.

No one in the atrium turned their head. I decided I had imagined it.

"Three," I said.

She stood and buttoned the top button of a coat she never unbuttoned. "Form 13-B on my desk by the noon bell. All sections."

* * *

The lantern market runs every autumn night, from dusk until the watch clears it out. It has two streets of stalls. Paper lanterns hang over them, crossing and crossing again, so thick that the sky is hard to see. Some are red, some are yellow, and some are a cheap green that the dye-sellers swear is lucky. People come for hot wine and fried dough. They also come to be seen with hot wine and fried dough.

It is a terrible place for me to work.

Light from above and light from every side makes short, soft shadows that run into each other. A door needs an edge. It needs a hard line where light stops and dark starts. Under a thousand lanterns, there are almost no edges. There are only faint smudges under every boot.

I had spent three nights finding the few I could use. There was a rain barrel by the barrel-maker's. There was a deep slot between the chestnut stall and a wall. And there was the dye-seller's awning. It was a long canvas sheet, stiff with old color. It threw one strip of honest black across the stones. That strip lay right over the iron hatch to his cellar.

I had checked the hatch. It was rusted shut. Under it, nine feet down, was packed earth and many dye barrels.

At the ninth bell, I was on the roof tiles above the chestnut stall. I had put the Mantle on.

The Mantle rises out of my shadow like water filling a footprint. It is matte black plate from boots to throat. It is light as felt and almost silent. The helm closes last. It is smooth and has no face, only a slit for the eyes, sewn shut with one white thread. The cold comes with it. Not in my hands. Deeper. While I wear it, things that should matter a little matter even less.

A small voice spoke from the dark under the chimney beside me.

"He's late," said Vesk.

I could not see him. You almost never can. Vesk is the smallest of my three. He is a quick shape with knife-hands. He lives in other people's shadows and comes back with opinions.

"He's never late."

"Well, he is tonight. Also, the pear woman's grandson is chasing the girl from the fried dough stall. Badly. He brought her a pear. *Her competitor's pear.*" He paused. "Thought you'd want to know."

"I didn't."

"You'll want it next week. When there's a fight between the stalls."

"Is he coming?"

"Yes. Here he—oh, he bought *two* pears."

I looked down. Emeric Fauconier came up the street through the crowd. He wore a neat brown coat. He had ink on his right cuff. He had a nice, forgettable face that some woman once found handsome. He held a pear in each hand.

A change in routine. I did not like it.

He still stopped at the pear stall. He paid. He waited for his change. The cat climbed out of the coin box and left. Under the red lanterns, he stood smiling at the old woman. And I watched his shadow on the stones.

Shadows breathe. You do not notice, because you do not look. They grow a hair bigger and settle again with the chest. Bigger and smaller.

His lay on the stones like paint.

Then he turned his head and looked straight up at my roof.

He should not have been able to. The Mantle sits in shadow as a stone sits in a pond. But he looked. His friendly face did not change at all. He dropped one pear and ran.

* * *

He was fast for a man with a clerk's knees. He ran through the crowd the wrong way, against the flow of people. That was smart. It made anyone who chased him easy to see. But no one in the lantern market looks up at the roofs.

I ran along the barrel-maker's roof. I jumped the alley to the hat shop. I landed on a balcony and knocked over a flowerpot. Below me, he pushed through a family of five and cut left toward the river stairs.

A fire basket hangs at the top of the river stairs. If he reached it, I would have light everywhere and no edges at all. Then he would be in the dock lanes, where the Golden Seal had its six men.

"Left," said Vesk, from somewhere. "He's going left. He's—no, *right*, he's—"

"I see him."

"Do you? Because I'm not sure *he* does."

"He knows exactly where he's going."

"Then why's he—"

"Vesk."

"Quiet. Got it."

He was not going left or right. He was running straight down the middle of the street. It was the shortest way to the stairs. And the shortest way passed the dye-seller's stall. I had spent three nights making sure of that.

I knocked once on the air, the way you knock on a door you know is already open.

In the black strip under the awning, a door opened in the shadow. You would have had to stand on it to see it. It was a rectangle of deeper dark, frame and all, set into the stones where the shade was hard. Fauconier's left foot came down on it.

The iron hatch stayed rusted shut. He went through the shadow instead. He dropped nine feet into the cellar like a sack of flour through a trapdoor. The door closed over him. Up on the street, a man selling green dye looked at his own feet, frowned, and went back to shouting about luck.

I climbed off the balcony and crossed the street at a walk. Then I stepped into the awning's shade after him.

* * *

The dark in the cellar was total. It was not like a room when someone puts the lamp out. It was part of the room, like the floor. There was no lantern. There was no crack of light from the street.

I landed beside a dye barrel and stood very still.

"Eden?" Vesk whispered, from far above. "Eden, I can't follow you in there. It's too dark. Even for me."

"I know."

"Is that bad?"

"Yes."

I understood my mistake at the same moment the Veil did.

No light, no edges. No edges, no doors.

A Veil is a monster that wears a dead person's face and life. Somewhere ahead, this one had stopped breathing out loud. Veils do not need to breathe. They do it for the neighbors. Then I heard him take the lamp off its hook by the stairs. A tin clink. He smashed it on the floor, so I would have nothing.

He knew what I was. Or he knew enough.

He came at me in the dark. I mostly heard it. His feet hit the packed earth wrong, with a stride too long for a clerk's legs. Then came the sound a coat makes when the arms inside grow longer than the sleeves.

I brought up the lance, then closed it into the shield. His blow hit the door-shield with a weight no clerk ever had. I let it push me back one step into the barrels. I wanted to learn how strong he was.

Strong. Not strong enough.

He circled. I turned with him by ear. He struck low. I caught it on the bottom edge of the shield. Then a grey finger slid over the rim and touched my wrist. It was cold, and the joints bent the wrong way.

That was when Candor hummed.

I had not called it yet. It was still inside, behind the Gate, where it waits. From in there, it made one note. It was low and clean, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass. It had never done that. It is a sword. Swords do not hum.

The Veil jerked back as if the note had burned him.

I had a tinderbox in my left glove, next to the peppermint. I struck it.

One spark. Then a flame the size of a fingernail. That was all I needed. Light on one side, dark on the other. An edge. I reached past the Door to the other place, the Gate. Candor came into my right hand as if it had been waiting there all along.

It does not shine. People expect it to. What it does is make any light tell the truth. In the light of that one match, the clerk's brown coat hung off something grey and thin with too many elbows. It looked as if it were a heron made of ash. Emeric Fauconier's face sat on the front of it like a mask that had slipped.

I always confirm before I end one. It is my rule. I made it at thirteen. I have never broken it.

"Your middle name," I said.

The face opened its mouth. Nothing came out. It tried again, like a stuck drawer. Still nothing.

Close, open, light. I caught its next swing on the shield. I opened its guard with the lance, at the shoulder joint. Then I put Candor through the place where a heart should be.

It sank slowly between the dye barrels. The grey turned to dust from the edges inward, the way paper burns. Only the face was left, lying in the dirt, with its eyes on me.

"You," it said, in Emeric Fauconier's voice, clear as a bell, "smell like the bottom of the world."

Then the face was dust too.

The match burned my fingers. I shook it out and stood in the dark. Through the stone above, very faint, the lantern market went on. Someone laughed. A dog barked. A man shouted about lucky green.

* * *

Ysolde was waiting on Quillwright Lane with her coat on. She did not ask how it went. She looked at the soot on the Mantle's glove. Then she looked at the coat under my arm.

"His," I said.

"Form 13-B," she said. "Write about the cat."

"You want the cat in the report?"

"I want everything in the report. Was it a Veil?"

"Yes."

"Did it say anything?"

"It said I smell like the bottom of the world."

"Put that in too. Quote it."

The Mantle had already sunk back into my shadow. I could still feel its cold, sitting behind my ribs. She took the coat. Before I handed it over, I took the ledger out of the inside pocket. She would have done the same.

"Anything else?" Ysolde asked.

"Give me a second."

It was a tax book. It had names, homes and sums. But the last six pages were not in the clerk's neat columns. They were a list in a different hand, rounder. Someone else's. Forty names. I knew every one.

Year Five, Hallowcrest. Mine was on the second page, between two girls from the riding club. Nothing was written beside it.

Rowan Tallis was on the third page. Someone had circled his name twice, so hard that the paper was dented. Next to it, in the same round hand, was a note.

"Ysolde," I said. "Whose handwriting is this?"

She took the page and read it. If she had held a teacup, it would have turned a quarter turn.

"Not his," she said.

"I know it's not his."

"Then I'll find out." She looked up. "Go to bed, Eden. Before your supervisor checks the door."

*Lantern Ball. Before the last dance.*
