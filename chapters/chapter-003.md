# Short Shadows

The tax clerk bought the same thing every night. One candied pear on a stick, from the same stall, from the same old woman. He paid with a silver quarter. Then he waited for change he didn't need.

For three nights, I'd watched him do it. For three nights, the old woman's cat got up and left when he came close. It was a fat brindled thing that slept in her coin box.

On the fourth night, Ysolde Lisle put the file in front of me. "Well?"

"The cat knows," I said.

"I can't file a cat."

We sat in the back room of a bookbinder's shop on Quillwright Lane, in Solmarch. It closed at sundown. On paper, it belonged to a widow in the south who'd never been north of the Brennoch line. The room smelled like glue and cold tea.

Ysolde kept her coat on, like always. She's always about to leave. She's the King's secretary. She's also the person who slides envelopes under bookbinders' doors. Then she wants paperwork for what happens next.

The file said: *Emeric Fauconier. Thirty-four. Junior assessor, Crown Revenue Office, Lantern Ward. Unmarried. No debts. Clean.*

Pinned to the back was a list of homes he'd assessed this month. Three were Hallowcrest families. One was the Belmonts.

"He changed four weeks ago," Ysolde said. "His landlady says he stopped humming. His desk mate says he got better at sums."

"People do that."

"Not at thirty-four." She turned her teacup a quarter turn on the saucer. That's what she does instead of sighing. "I want this quiet. The Golden Seal has six investigators in Lantern Ward this week. Every one of them would give a finger to see your mask."

"Which finger?"

"Eden."

"I'm only asking which one they'd miss least."

She looked at me. Ysolde's face, like mine, doesn't do much. On her it looks planned.

"And curfew," she said. "I hear you have a supervisor now."

I didn't ask how she knew. "She sleeps at half past the tenth bell. She checks my door at the tenth."

"Then be back by the sixth. And the Bell Assessment is in nine days. Three tones, like always. Not three and a half."

Last year, I'd put my palm on the big bronze bell in the atrium. I pushed exactly enough for three clear tones. Then I lifted my hand. In the quiet after, so faint I thought it was my pulse, the bell rang a fourth time.

Nobody in the atrium turned their head. I decided I'd imagined it.

"Three," I said.

She stood and buttoned the top button of a coat she'd never unbuttoned. "Form 13-B on my desk by the noon bell. All sections."

* * *

The lantern market runs every autumn night from dusk until the watch clears it out. Two streets of stalls. Paper lanterns hang over them, crossing and recrossing, so thick that the sky is just a rumor. Red ones, yellow ones, and a cheap green the dye-sellers swear is lucky. People come for hot wine and fried dough. They also come to be seen holding hot wine and fried dough.

It's a terrible place for me to work.

Light from above and light from every side means short, soft shadows, smeared across each other. A door needs an edge. A hard line where light stops and dark starts. Under a thousand lanterns, there are almost no edges. Just faint smudges under every boot.

I'd spent three nights finding the few I could use. A rain barrel by the cooper's. A deep slot between the chestnut stall and a wall. And the dye-seller's awning. It was a long canvas sheet, stiff with old color, and it threw one strip of honest black across the cobbles. Right over the iron hatch to his cellar.

I'd checked the hatch. It was rusted shut. Under it, nine feet down, was packed earth and a lot of dye barrels.

At the ninth bell, I was on the slates above the chestnut stall. I'd put the Mantle on.

It rises out of my shadow like water filling a footprint. Matte black plate from boots to throat. Light as felt, almost silent. The helm closes last. It's smooth and faceless, with a slit for the eyes sewn shut with one white thread. The cold comes with it. Not in my hands. Deeper. While I wear it, things that should matter a little matter even less.

A small voice spoke from the dark under the chimney beside me.

"He's late," said Vesk.

I couldn't see him. You almost never can. Vesk is the smallest of my three. He's a quick, knife-handed shape that lives in other people's shadows and comes back with opinions.

"He's never late."

"Well, he is tonight. Also, the pear woman's grandson is chasing the girl from the fried dough. Badly. He brought her a pear. *Her competitor's pear.*" A pause. "Thought you'd want to know."

"I didn't."

"You'll want it next week. When there's a feud."

"Is he coming?"

"Yes. Here he—oh, he bought *two* pears."

I looked down. Emeric Fauconier came up the street through the crowd. Neat brown coat. Ink on his right cuff. A nice, forgettable face that some woman once found handsome. A pear in each hand.

A change in routine. I didn't like it.

He still stopped at the pear stall. He paid. He waited for his change. The cat climbed out of the coin box and left. Under the red lanterns, he stood smiling at the old woman. And I watched his shadow on the cobbles.

Shadows breathe. You don't notice, because you're not looking. They swell a hair and settle with the chest. Swell and settle.

His lay on the stones like paint.

Then he turned his head and looked straight up at my roof.

He shouldn't have been able to. The Mantle sits in shadow like a stone in a pond. But he looked. His friendly face didn't change at all. He dropped one pear and ran.

* * *

He was fast for a man with a clerk's knees. He ran through the crowd the wrong way, against the flow. That was smart. It made anyone chasing him easy to see. But nobody in the lantern market looks up at the roofs.

I ran along the cooper's roof. I jumped the alley to the haberdasher's. I landed on a balcony and killed a flowerpot. Below me, he shoved through a family of five and cut left toward the river stairs.

A brazier hangs at the top of the river stairs. If he reached it, I'd have light everywhere and no edges at all. Then he'd be in the dock lanes, where the Golden Seal had its six men.

"Left," said Vesk, from somewhere. "He's going left. He's—no, *right*, he's—"

"I see him."

"Do you? Because I'm not sure *he* does."

"He knows exactly where he's going."

"Then why's he—"

"Vesk."

"Quiet. Got it."

He wasn't going left or right. He was running straight down the middle of the street. It was the shortest way to the stairs. And the shortest way passed the dye-seller's stall. I'd spent three nights making sure of that.

I knocked once on the air, like knocking on a door you know is already open.

In the black strip under the awning, a door opened in the shadow. You'd have had to be standing on it to see it. A rectangle of deeper dark, frame and all, set into the cobbles where the shade was hard. Fauconier's left foot came down on it.

The iron hatch stayed rusted shut. He went through the shadow instead. He dropped nine feet into the cellar like a sack of flour through a trapdoor. The door closed over him. Up on the street, a man selling green dye looked at his own feet, frowned, and went back to shouting about luck.

I climbed off the balcony and crossed the street at a walk. Then I stepped into the awning's shade after him.

* * *

The dark in the cellar was total. It wasn't like a room with the lamp out. It was part of the room, like the floor. No lantern. No crack of light from the street.

I landed beside a dye barrel and stood very still.

"Eden?" Vesk whispered, from somewhere far above. "Eden, I can't follow you in there. It's too dark. Even for me."

"I know."

"Is that bad?"

"Yes."

I understood my mistake at the same moment the Veil did.

No light, no edges. No edges, no doors.

Somewhere ahead, he'd stopped breathing out loud. Veils don't need to. They do it for the neighbors. Then I heard him take the lamp off its hook by the stairs. A clink of tin. He smashed it on the floor, so I'd have nothing.

He knew what I was. Or he knew enough.

He came at me in the dark. I mostly heard it. His feet on packed earth, wrong, with a stride too long for a clerk's legs. Then the sound a coat makes when the arms inside get longer than the sleeves.

I brought up the lance, then closed it into the shield. His hit landed on the door-shield with a weight no clerk ever had. I let it push me back a step into the barrels. I wanted to learn how strong he was.

Very. Not enough.

He circled. I turned with him by ear. He struck low. I caught it on the shield's bottom edge. Then a grey finger slid over the rim and touched my wrist. It was cold, and the joints bent the wrong way.

That was when Candor hummed.

I hadn't called it yet. It was still inside, behind the Gate, where it waits. From in there, it sounded one note. Low and clean, like a finger around the rim of a glass. It had never done that. It's a sword. Swords don't hum.

The Veil jerked back like the note had burned him.

I had a tinderbox in my left glove, next to the peppermint. I struck it.

One spark. Then a flame the size of a fingernail. That was all I needed. Light on one side, dark on the other. An edge. I reached past the Door, to the other place, the Gate. Candor came into my right hand like it had been waiting there all along.

It doesn't shine. People expect it to. What it does is make any light tell the truth. In that one match flame, the clerk's brown coat hung off something grey and narrow with too many elbows. It looked like a heron made of ash. Emeric Fauconier's face sat on the front of it like a mask that had slipped.

I always confirm before I end one. It's my rule. I made it at thirteen. I've never broken it.

"Your middle name," I said.

The face opened its mouth. Nothing came out. It tried again, like a stuck drawer. Still nothing.

Close, open, light. I caught its next swing on the shield. I opened its guard with the lance, at the shoulder joint. Then I put Candor through the place where a heart should be.

It sank slowly between the dye barrels. The grey turned to dust from the edges inward, the way paper burns. Only the face was left, lying in the dirt, eyes on me.

"You," it said, in Emeric Fauconier's voice, clear as a bell, "smell like the bottom of the world."

Then the face was dust too.

The match burned my fingers. I shook it out and stood in the dark. Through the stone above, very faint, the lantern market went on. Someone laughing. A dog. A man shouting about lucky green.

* * *

Ysolde was waiting on Quillwright Lane with her coat on. She didn't ask how it went. She looked at the soot on the Mantle's glove. Then at the coat under my arm.

"His," I said.

"Form 13-B," she said. "Note the cat."

"You want the cat in the report?"

"I want everything in the report. Was it a Veil?"

"Yes."

"Did it say anything?"

"It said I smell like the bottom of the world."

"Include that too. Quote it."

The Mantle had already sunk back into my shadow. I could still feel its cold, sitting behind my ribs. She took the coat. Before I handed it over, I took the ledger out of the inside pocket. She'd have done the same.

"Anything else?" Ysolde asked.

"Give me a second."

It was a revenue book. Names, homes, sums assessed. But the last six pages weren't in the clerk's neat columns. They were a list in a different hand, rounder. Someone else's. Forty names. I knew every one.

Year Five, Hallowcrest. Mine was on the second page, between two girls from the riding club. Nothing was written beside it.

Rowan Tallis was on the third page. Someone had circled his name twice, so hard it dented the paper. Next to it, in the same round hand, was a note.

"Ysolde," I said. "Whose handwriting is this?"

She took the page and read it. Her teacup, if she'd had one, would have turned a quarter turn.

"Not his," she said.

"I know it's not his."

"Then I'll find out." She looked up. "Go to bed, Eden. Before your supervisor checks the door."

*Lantern Ball. Before the last dance.*
