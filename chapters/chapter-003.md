# Short Shadows

The tax clerk bought the same thing every night: one candied pear on a stick, from the same stall, from the same old woman, and every night he paid with a silver quarter and waited for change he didn't need. For three nights I'd watched him do it. For three nights the old woman's cat, a fat brindled thing that slept in her coin box, had got up and walked away when he came near.

On the fourth night, Ysolde Lisle put the file in front of me and said, "Well?"

"The cat knows," I said.

"I can't file a cat."

We were in the back room of a bookbinder's on Quillwright Lane in Solmarch, which shut at sundown and belonged, on paper, to a widow in the south who'd never been north of the Brennoch line. It smelled of glue and cold tea. Ysolde sat with her coat on, as she always does, because she's always about to leave. She's the King's secretary. She is also, for my purposes, the person who slides envelopes under bookbinders' doors and then wants paperwork for what happens next.

The file said: *Emeric Fauconier. Thirty-four. Junior assessor, Crown Revenue Office, Lantern Ward. Unmarried. No debts. Clean.* Pinned to the back was a list of the households he'd assessed in the last month. Three were Hallowcrest families. One was the Belmonts.

"He changed four weeks ago," Ysolde said. "His landlady says he stopped humming. His desk mate says he got better at sums."

"People do."

"Not at thirty-four." She turned her teacup a quarter turn on its saucer, which is what she does instead of sighing. "I want it quiet. The Golden Seal has six investigators in Lantern Ward this week, and every one of them would give a finger to see your mask in person."

"Which finger?"

"Eden."

"I'm just asking which one they'd value least."

She looked at me. Ysolde's face, like mine, doesn't do much. On hers it looks deliberate.

"And curfew," she said. "I understand you now have a supervisor."

I didn't ask how she knew. "She sleeps at the tenth bell and a half. She checks my door at the tenth."

"Then be back by the sixth," Ysolde said. "And the Bell Assessment is in nine days. Three tones, as usual. Not three and a half."

Last year I'd laid my palm on the great bronze bell in the atrium and pushed exactly enough for three clear tones, and lifted my hand, and in the quiet after, so faint I'd thought it was my own pulse, it had rung a fourth. Nobody in the atrium had turned their head. I'd decided, at the time, that I'd imagined it.

"Three," I said.

She stood, and buttoned the top button of a coat she'd never unbuttoned. "Form 13-B on my desk by the noon bell. All sections."

* * *

The lantern market in Lantern Ward runs every night of autumn from dusk until the watch clears it, two streets of stalls under strings of paper lanterns that cross and re-cross overhead so thickly the sky is just a rumor. Thousands of lanterns. Red, yellow, a cheap green that the dye-sellers swear is lucky. People come for hot wine and fried dough and to be seen holding hot wine and fried dough.

It is a terrible place for me to work.

Light from above and light from every side means short shadows, soft ones, smeared across each other. A door needs an edge, a hard line where light stops and dark begins. Under a thousand lanterns there are almost no edges. Just a hundred faint, overlapping smudges of not-quite-light under every boot.

I'd spent three nights finding the ones I could use. A rain barrel by the cooper's. The deep slot between the chestnut stall and a wall. And the dye-seller's awning, a long canvas sheet stiff with old color, which threw one strip of honest black across the cobbles, right over the iron hatch to his cellar.

I'd checked the hatch. It was rusted shut. Under it, nine feet down, was packed earth and a lot of dye barrels.

At the ninth bell I was on the slates above the chestnut stall, and I'd put the Mantle on.

It comes up out of my shadow the way water fills a footprint. Matte black plate from boots to throat, light as felt and almost silent, then the helm closing last: smooth, faceless, with a slit for the eyes stitched across with one white thread. The cold arrives with it. Not in the hands. Further in. While I wear it, things that would usually matter a little matter a little less.

A small voice spoke from the dark under the chimney beside me.

"He's late," said Vesk.

I couldn't see him. You almost never can. Vesk is the smallest of my three, a quick, knife-handed shape that lives in other people's shadows and comes back with opinions.

"He's never late."

"Well, he is tonight. Also, the pear woman's grandson is courting the girl from the fried dough. Badly. He's brought her a pear. *Her own competitor's pear.*" A pause. "I thought you'd want to know."

"I didn't."

"You'll want to know next week, when there's a feud."

"Is he coming?"

"Yes. Here he—oh, he's bought *two* pears."

I looked down. Emeric Fauconier came up the street through the crowd: neat brown coat, ink on his right cuff, a pleasant forgettable face that some woman had once found handsome. He had a pear in each hand.

Change in routine. I didn't like it.

He stopped at the pear stall anyway. Paid. Waited for his change. The cat got out of the coin box and left. And under the red lanterns, while he stood there smiling at the old woman, I watched his shadow on the cobbles.

People's shadows breathe. You don't notice, because you're not looking, but they do: they swell a hair and settle, swell and settle, with the chest. His lay on the stones like something painted there.

Then he turned his head and looked straight up at the roof where I was.

He shouldn't have been able to. The Mantle sits in shadow like a stone in a pond. But he looked, and his pleasant face didn't change at all, and he dropped one pear and ran.

* * *

He was fast for a man with a clerk's knees. He went through the crowd the wrong way, against the flow, which is clever, because it's what makes pursuit visible. Nobody in the lantern market looks up at the roofs.

I ran the ridge of the cooper's roof, jumped the alley to the haberdasher's, came down onto a balcony with a flowerpot that didn't survive it. Below me, he put his shoulder through a family of five and cut left toward the river stair.

The river stair is lit at the top by a hanging brazier. If he reached it, I'd have light everywhere and no edges at all, and he'd be into the dock lanes, where the Golden Seal has its six men.

"Left," said Vesk, from somewhere. "Left, he's going left, he's—no, *right*, he's—"

"I see him."

He wasn't going left or right. He was going straight down the middle of the street, the shortest way to the stair, and the shortest way ran past the dye-seller's stall. I'd spent three nights making sure of that.

I knocked once on the air, the way you'd knock on a door you already know is open.

In the black strip under the awning, a door opened in the shadow. You'd have had to be standing on it to see it: a rectangle of deeper dark, frame and all, set into the cobbles where the shade was hard. Fauconier's left foot came down on it.

The iron hatch was still rusted shut. He went through the shadow instead and dropped nine feet into the cellar like a sack of meal through a trapdoor, and the door closed over him, and up on the street a man selling green dye looked down at his own feet and frowned and went back to shouting about luck.

I came down off the balcony, crossed the street at a walk, and stepped into the awning's shade after him.

* * *

The dark in the cellar was complete: the kind of black that's a fact of the room, like the floor, with no lantern and no crack of street-light. I landed in it beside a dye barrel and stood very still, and understood my mistake at about the same time he did.

No light, no edges. No edges, no doors.

Somewhere ahead of me he'd stopped breathing out loud. Veils don't need to; they do it for the neighbors. Then I heard him take the lamp off the hook by the stair, a clink of tin, and smash it on the floor so that I'd have nothing.

He knew what I was. Or he knew enough.

He came at me in the dark. I heard it more than anything: his feet on packed earth, wrong, too long a stride for a clerk's legs, and then the sound a coat makes when the arms inside it get longer than the sleeves. I brought the lance up and closed it and his hit landed on the door-shield with a weight no clerk has ever had, and I let it push me a step back into the barrels to learn how much he had.

A lot. Not enough.

He circled. I turned with him by ear. He hit again, low, and I caught it on the shield's lower edge and felt a grey finger, cold and jointed wrong, slide over the rim and touch my wrist.

That was when Candor hummed.

I hadn't called it yet. It was still inside, behind the Gate, where it waits. And from in there, in the black, it sounded one note: low, clean, like a finger run around the rim of a glass. It has never once done that. It is a sword. Swords don't hum.

The Veil jerked back as if the note had burned him.

I had a tinderbox in my left glove, next to the peppermint. I struck it.

One spark, then a flame the size of a fingernail, and that was all I needed: light on one side, dark on the other, an edge. I reached past the Door, to the other place, the Gate, and Candor came into my right hand as if it had been waiting there all along.

It doesn't shine. People expect it to. What it does is make whatever light there is tell the truth, and in that one match-flame the clerk's pleasant brown coat was hanging off something grey and narrow and many-elbowed, a shape like a heron made of ash, with Emeric Fauconier's face set on the front of it like a mask that had slipped.

Before I end one, I confirm. It's my rule. I made it when I was thirteen and I've broken it never.

"Your middle name," I said.

The face opened its mouth. Nothing came out. It tried again, the way you try a stuck drawer, and still nothing.

Close, open, light. I caught its next reach on the shield, opened its guard with the lance to the shoulder joint, and put Candor through the place where a heart would have been.

It came down slowly between the dye barrels. The grey went to dust from the edges in, the way paper burns, until there was only the face, lying in the dirt with its eyes on me.

"You," it said, in Emeric Fauconier's voice, quite clearly, "smell like the bottom of the world."

Then the face was dust too.

The match burned my fingers. I shook it out and stood in the dark, and through the stone above, very faint, I could hear the lantern market still going on: somebody laughing, a dog, a man shouting about lucky green.

* * *

Ysolde was waiting on Quillwright Lane with her coat on. She didn't ask how it had gone. She looked at the soot on the Mantle's gauntlet, and then at the coat I was carrying under one arm.

"His," I said.

"Form 13-B," she said. "Note the cat."

The Mantle had gone back into my shadow by then. I still felt it, the cold of it, sitting somewhere behind my ribs where things usually were. She took the coat and I took the ledger out of its inside pocket before I handed it over, because she'd have done the same.

It was a revenue book: names, households, sums assessed. The last six pages weren't in the clerk's neat columns. They were a list in a different hand, rounder, someone else's. Forty names. I knew all of them.

Year Five, Hallowcrest. Mine was on the second page, between two girls from the riding club, with nothing next to it.

Rowan Tallis was on the third. Someone had drawn a circle around his name, twice, pressing hard enough to dent the paper. Beside it, in that same rounder hand, someone had written an appointment.

*Lantern Ball. Before the last dance.*
