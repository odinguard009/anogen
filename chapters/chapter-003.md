# Short Shadows

The tax clerk bought the same thing every night. One candied pear on a stick, from the same stall, from the same old woman. He paid with a silver quarter. Then he waited for change he did not need.

For three nights, I had watched him do it. For three nights, the old woman's cat got up and left when he came close. It was a fat, striped cat that slept in her coin box.

On the fourth night, Ysolde put the file in front of me. "Well?"

"The cat knows," I said.

"I can't file a cat."

We sat in the back room of a bookbinder's shop in Solmarch, the big market town below our school. It was after sundown, and the shop was closed. The room smelled of glue and cold tea. A single lamp stood on the table, and the window had a dark cloth over it.

Ysolde kept her coat on, as always. She is a thin woman with sharp eyes and short gray hair, and she is always about to leave. She is the King's secretary. She is also the person who slides envelopes under bookbinders' doors. Then she wants paperwork for what happens next.

The file said: *Emeric Fauconier. Thirty-four. Tax clerk. Unmarried. No debts. Clean.*

"He changed four weeks ago," Ysolde said. "His landlady says he stopped humming. The man at the next desk says he got better at sums."

"People do that."

"Not at thirty-four. Men that age don't change. They only get tired." She turned her teacup a quarter turn on the saucer. That is what she does instead of sighing. "I want this quiet. The Golden Seal has six investigators in town this week. They are the King's police, and they hate secrets they did not find first. If they see your mask, they will want it."

"They can have the mask."

"Eden."

"I'm only saying it is not a good mask."

She looked at me. Her face, like mine, does not do much. On her it looks planned.

"And your curfew," she said. "I hear you have a supervisor now."

I did not ask how she knew. "She goes to bed at half past the tenth bell. She checks my door at the tenth."

"Then be back by the sixth. And the Bell Assessment is in nine days. Three tones, like always. Not three and a half."

Last year, I put my hand on the big bronze bell in the school hall. I pushed just enough for three clear tones, and I lifted my hand. In the quiet after, so faint I thought it was my pulse, the bell rang a fourth time.

Nobody in the hall turned their head. I decided I had imagined it.

"Three," I said.

She stood and buttoned the top button of a coat she never unbuttoned. "Form 13-B on my desk by noon. All sections."

* * *

The lantern market runs every autumn night, from dusk until the guards clear it out. It has two streets of stalls. Paper lanterns hang over them, crossing and crossing again, so thick that you can hardly see the sky. Some are red, some are yellow, and some are a cheap green that the dye-sellers say is lucky. The air smells of hot wine and fried dough.

It is a terrible place for me to work.

My power is doors. A door needs an edge, a hard line where light stops and dark starts. Under a thousand lanterns, the shadows come out short and soft, and they run into each other. There are almost no edges.

I had spent three nights finding the few I could use. The best one belonged to the dye-seller's stall. Its canvas roof threw one strip of honest black across the stones, right over an iron hatch that led down to his cellar. The hatch was rusted shut. Under it, nine feet down, was packed earth and a lot of dye barrels.

At the ninth bell, I was on the roof tiles above the chestnut stall. The tiles were still warm from the day, and the smoke from the roasting pans stung my nose. I let the Mantle come.

The Mantle is black armor, and it comes out of my shadow like water filling a footprint. First the boots, cold on my feet, then the legs and the chest, plate by plate, closing from the ground up. Each piece is cold for one heartbeat. Then it is warm like my own skin. The last piece is the helm. It is smooth and has no face, only a thin slit for the eyes, sewn shut with one white thread.

The armor is matte black and light as felt, and it makes almost no sound. But the cold stays, deep behind my ribs.

"Cold again?" said a small voice from the dark under the chimney beside me. "Your teeth say so."

"My teeth are quiet."

"Not to me."

It was Vesk.

I could not see him. You almost never can. Vesk is one of my three shadow friends, the smallest one, quick, with hands like knives. He lives in other people's shadows and comes back with opinions.

"He's late," Vesk added. "He's never late."

"Maybe he's busy."

"Maybe. Also, the pear woman's grandson is chasing the girl from the fried dough stall. Badly. He brought her a pear. Her *competitor's* pear." He paused. "Thought you'd want to know."

"I didn't."

"You'll want it next week. When the stalls fight."

"Is he coming?"

"Yes. Here he—oh. He bought *two* pears."

I looked down. The clerk came up the street through the crowd in a neat brown coat, with ink on his right cuff. He had a nice, forgettable face. He held a pear in each hand.

"Two pears," Vesk whispered. "Who is the second one for?"

"Me, maybe."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke."

A change in routine. I did not like it.

He still stopped at the pear stall. He paid. He waited for his change. The cat climbed out of the coin box and walked away. Under the red lanterns, he stood smiling at the old woman, and I watched his shadow on the stones.

"Eden," Vesk whispered. "Look at his shadow."

"I am."

"It isn't breathing."

"I know."

Shadows breathe. You do not notice, because you do not look. They grow a hair bigger and smaller with the chest. His lay on the stones like paint.

"He's looking up," Vesk breathed. "Eden. He's looking *up*."

"I see."

Then he turned his head and looked straight up at my roof.

He should not have been able to see me. The Mantle sits in the dark as a stone sits in a pond. But he looked. His friendly face did not change at all. He dropped one pear and ran.

* * *

He was fast for a man with a clerk's knees. He pushed through the crowd the wrong way, against the people. That was smart. But nobody in a lantern market looks up at the roofs.

I ran along the barrel-maker's roof and jumped the alley to the hat shop. I landed on a balcony and knocked over a flowerpot. Below me, he shoved through a family of five and cut left toward the river stairs.

A big fire basket hangs at the top of those stairs. If he reached it, I would have light everywhere and no edges at all. Then he would be in the dock lanes, where the Golden Seal had its men.

"Left," said Vesk, from somewhere. "He's going left. He's—no, *right*, he's—"

"I see him."

"Do you? Because I'm not sure *he* does."

"He knows where he's going. He is running straight down the middle."

"Then why's he—"

"Vesk."

"Quiet. Got it."

The shortest way to the stairs went right past the dye-seller's stall. That was why I had spent three nights on it. I wanted him to run over the black strip. "Plan?" said Vesk.

"The dye-seller's shadow. A door. He falls, I follow."

"Alone?"

"No Seal man sees a thing."

I knocked once on the air, the way you knock on a door you know is already open.

In the black strip under the awning, a door opened in the shadow. You would have had to stand on it to see it. It was a rectangle of deeper dark, frame and all, set into the stones. The clerk's left foot came down on it.

The iron hatch stayed rusted shut. He went through the shadow instead. He fell nine feet into the cellar, fast and quiet. The door closed over him. A man selling green dye looked at his own feet, frowned, and went back to shouting about luck. "Lucky green! Get your lucky green!"

I climbed off the balcony and crossed the street at a walk. Then I stepped into the shade after him.

* * *

The dark in the cellar was total. It was not the dark of a room when someone puts the lamp out. It was part of the room, as the floor is. The air was cold, and it smelled of wet earth and old paint. There was no lantern and no crack of light from the street.

I landed beside a dye barrel and stood very still.

"Eden?" Vesk whispered, from far above. "Eden, I can't follow you in there. It's too dark. Even for me."

"I know."

"Is that bad?"

"Yes."

I saw my mistake at the same moment the Veil did.

No light, no edges. No edges, no doors.

A Veil is a monster that wears a dead person's face and life. This one had stopped pretending to breathe. Veils do not need air. They breathe only for the neighbors. Then I heard a tin clink. He had taken the lamp off its hook by the stairs, and he smashed it on the floor so I would have nothing.

He knew what I was. Or he knew enough.

He came at me in the dark, and I only heard it. His feet hit the packed earth with a stride too long for a clerk. Then came the sound a coat makes when the arms inside grow longer than the sleeves.

I pulled out my lance, then closed it into the shield. It is a door-shield, the size of a small door, with an iron ring-knocker in the middle. His first blow hit the shield with a weight no clerk ever had. I let it push me back one step into the barrels. I wanted to learn how strong he was.

Strong. Not strong enough.

He circled. I turned with him, by ear. He struck low, and I caught it on the bottom edge of the shield. Then a gray finger slid over the rim and touched my wrist. It was cold, and the joints bent the wrong way.

That was when my sword hummed.

My sword is called Candor. I had not called it yet. It waited inside, behind the Gate, the place where I keep it. From in there, it made one low, clean note, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass. It had never done that before. Swords do not hum.

The Veil jerked back as if the note had burned him.

I had a tinderbox in my left glove, next to the peppermint. I struck it.

One spark, then a flame the size of a fingernail. That was all I needed. Light on one side, dark on the other. An edge. I reached into the Gate, and Candor came into my right hand as if it had been waiting there all along.

Candor does not shine. People expect it to. What it does is make any light tell the truth. In the light of one match, the clerk's brown coat hung off something gray and thin, as tall as a door. It had long legs and too many elbows, like a heron made of ash. The clerk's face sat on the front of it like a mask that had slipped.

I always confirm before I end one. It is my rule. I made it at thirteen, and I have never broken it.

"Your middle name," I said.

The face opened its mouth. Nothing came out. It tried again. Still nothing.

That was my answer. The match shook in my fingers, and I used the last of its light. I caught the next swing on the shield. I opened its guard with the lance, at the shoulder. Then I put Candor through the place where a heart should be.

It sank slowly between the dye barrels. The gray turned to dust from the edges inward, like burning paper. Only the face was left, lying in the dirt, with its eyes on me.

"You," it said, in the clerk's voice, clear as a bell, "smell like the bottom of the world."

Then the face was dust too.

The match burned my fingers. I shook it out and stood in the dark. Through the stone above, very faint, the market went on. Someone laughed. A dog barked. A man shouted about lucky green.

* * *

Ysolde was waiting in the lane with her coat on. She looked at the soot on my glove, then at the coat under my arm.

"Well?" she said.

"It's done."

"Quiet?"

"Quiet enough."

"His," I said.

"Form 13-B," she said. "Write about the cat."

"You want the cat in the report?"

"I want everything in the report. Was it a Veil?"

"Yes."

"Did it say anything?"

"It said I smell like the bottom of the world."

"Put that in too. Quote it."

The Mantle had already sunk back into my shadow. I could still feel its cold, sitting behind my ribs. She took the coat. Before I handed it over, I took the clerk's book out of the inside pocket. She would have done the same.

"Anything else?" Ysolde asked.

"Give me a second."

It was a tax book, full of names, homes and sums. But the last six pages were not in the clerk's neat columns.

"A list," I said. "Forty names. Different handwriting. Rounder."

"Whose names?"

"Mine too."

They were all students from my year at school. Mine was on the second page, between two girls from the riding club. Nothing was written beside it.

Rowan Tallis was on the third page. Someone had circled his name twice, so hard that the paper was dented. Next to it, in the same round hand, was a note.

"Ysolde," I said. "Whose handwriting is this?"

She took the page and read it. If she had held a teacup, it would have turned a quarter turn.

"Not his," she said.

"I know it's not his."

"Then I will find out." She looked up. "Go to bed, Eden. Before your supervisor checks the door."

*Lantern Ball. Before the last dance.*
