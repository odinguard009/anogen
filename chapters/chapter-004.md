# Eleven Blocks

The notice board in the atrium is as old as the academy. In a hundred and three years, nobody thought to replace the cork. It was black with old pinholes. In some places they were so thick that new notices hung by one tack and spun whenever the doors opened.

On the first morning of the week, a clean sheet went up in the middle. It was pinned at all four corners by someone who meant it.

*BELL ASSESSMENT, YEAR FIVE. Order of candidates.*

I read it twice. Then I took the clerk's ledger from my inside pocket. I opened it to the last six pages and held it next to the sheet. I looked like I was checking my own time slot. To be fair, I also was.

Same names. Same order. A boy called Grimwald first. Then Felix. Then Clara Belmont. Then a run of riding-club girls. It wasn't alphabetical. The Bell Office sorts by dormitory floor, then by height. The reasons died with whoever started it.

The ledger matched the board name for name. It even matched the typo in Bastien Ardenne's surname, with two n's in the middle.

I'd spent two nights on that list. I had a theory. The list was written in a careful, round hand. The order matched no roll I knew. So I decided it came from somewhere private. A registrar's office. A desk with a lock. Someone on the inside.

Someone on the inside had pinned it to a public board. Anyone who could walk into the atrium could have copied it. And two thousand four hundred people walk into the atrium.

Two nights wasted. I'd be ashamed if I had the energy.

The only thing the board didn't have was the circle around Rowan Tallis. I put the ledger away.

* * *

Lunch at Hallowcrest is served in the long refectory at the noon bell. It's the one hour when the whole academy sits in one room and can see who you're sitting with.

I'd had a good system for five years. I sat with Felix. People saw Felix, and next to him a shape that might be his coat.

The system ended when Lysette Hollen put her tray down across from me.

"Committee," she said, before I could talk. "You're a flight risk at lunch. You were one all last week."

"Last week I was in the library."

"You were on the roof."

"The library has a roof."

Felix turned a page. He had the look of a man who plans to see nothing.

"Felix," Lysette said. "Is he always like this?"

"Like what?"

"Slippery."

"Only when he's awake," said Felix, and went back to his book.

Lysette ate the way she did everything. Fast and neat. She cut her potato into identical squares. Twice I saw her look down the hall. Rowan Tallis sat at the Year Five center table. Clara Belmont was on one side of him. Three people tried to squeeze in on the other. He was telling a story with both hands.

Clara was laughing. Each time Lysette looked, her left hand went to her collar. To a thin gold chain that vanished under her shirt. She touched it. Then her hand dropped.

I watched him too, for a different reason. He got to the part where something fell on somebody. He acted it out. The whole table roared. Clara grabbed his arm to steady herself. Someone threw a bread roll.

In my pocket was a page with his name circled twice in someone else's ink. I was the only person in the room who knew.

That's the job. I've never minded it. It's just that the refectory is very loud at noon. You can hear every word of every story, and none of them tell you anything.

So I filed the chain instead. A girl in love with a boy who's laughing with someone else. A chain she touches when it hurts. I thought I knew what hung on the end. A token. A dried flower. A button off his coat. People keep strange things.

"Oh, *look*," said a voice above my head. "My savior slept through breakfast again."

Princess Seraphine Aurenmark sat down next to Lysette without asking. She had her brother's coloring and none of his posture. She had a pastry in each hand. She put one on my plate.

"I was at breakfast," I said.

"You were *present* at breakfast. That's different. Hollen, he was present at breakfast, wasn't he? You'd know. I hear you're his keeper now."

"I'm his *supervisor*," Lysette said. "By Committee assignment."

"Self-assigned."

"Committee assignment," Lysette said, with the patience of someone talking to a judge, "can be started by any officer."

Seraphine smiled at her. It was a very pleasant smile. Lysette's ears went red.

Seraphine and I have known each other since we were twelve. She likes to poke at people to see what's under the surface. Mostly it's kind. Lysette didn't know that yet. It wasn't my place to explain.

"He saved my life once," Seraphine told her. She leaned in. "Did he mention it?"

"No."

"He never does. It was very dashing. Then he went to sleep in a hedge."

"It was a ditch," I said.

"See? Modest."

Felix turned another page. Lysette cut a potato square in half. Then in half again. Then again, until it couldn't be cut anymore.

* * *

Arms class at the ninth bell is in the Long Yard. It's a strip of packed sand under the east wall. The teacher is Tesfane Okoru. He has one arm, and he needs about a quarter of it to put any student in the sand.

He paired us by rank. That gave me Bastien Ardenne, rank eleven. For three years he'd waited to be ranked above me. Today he saw no reason to wait longer.

"Batons," said Okoru. "Points. Three touches. Out of the ring counts as a touch."

I have one rule for class. I show the lance, and I only block with it. Nothing else. Rank eight blocks well, attacks without heart, and loses a third of his bouts to people ranked below him. That's how he stays rank eight.

Bastien came in hard. A straight thrust. A high feint. A cut to my ribs from the left. I caught all three on the shaft and gave up half a step each time.

He was quick, and he knew it. Before every real attack, he dropped his left shoulder, like some people clear their throat before they lie. His weight sat on his front foot. After each cut, his guard came back a hair late on the right. If I'd been trying, it would've taken three exchanges. The third would've put him on his back, wondering where the sky went.

He came again. I blocked. Again. Block.

Eleven times I blocked. By the eleventh, I could've told you where he'd be standing a minute later. He hadn't touched me once. That was a problem. Rank eight doesn't do that. People were starting to watch the ring.

On the twelfth, I let his feint pull my lance high. His baton came in low and hit my ribs. Left side, under the arm.

It was a good hit. Better than I'd planned for. The air left me with a noise I didn't approve of. I stepped back to make room, and my heel crossed the chalk.

"Touch," said Okoru. "And out. Two."

A moment later, Bastien took the third on my forearm, because I let him. The bout was over. He grinned at me with real joy, the simple kind. He held out his hand. I shook it.

"Tell me that wasn't amazing," he said.

"It was fine."

"*Fine?* I beat the eighth rank!"

"Congratulations."

He'd tell people for a week. Good. I'd rather be someone Bastien Ardenne beat than someone people talk about.

My ribs disagreed, but nobody asked them.

Okoru didn't say "next." He stood at the edge of the ring with his one hand tucked in his belt. He looked at me. It went on longer than a teacher's look should. His face is lined and calm. He has the eyes of a man who once had to count how many came back.

"Wren," he said.

"Sir."

"You watched his left shoulder eleven times."

I didn't answer. Anything I said would've been worse.

"I like his left shoulder," I said. "It's honest."

"Honest shoulders are a rare thing."

"Is that a compliment, sir?"

"It's an observation. Next pair."

He turned away.

I went and sat on the bench with my ribs. I didn't look at him again. For the rest of the hour, I felt him not looking at me.

* * *

Lysette was paired with a girl from the second floor. Clara's floor. And Lysette was very, very good. Two short batons, Golden Seal style, held close. All wrists and angles. She watched her opponent in a way that made you feel cross-examined. She took the first two touches cleanly.

On the third, the other girl's baton glanced off Lysette's guard. It flicked up across her collarbone. The thin gold chain snapped.

It fell into the sand. Whatever hung on it fell too, and popped open.

Lysette stopped like she'd been hit for real. The bout was over. The other girl was already saying sorry. Lysette didn't hear. She dropped to one knee in the sand. I was closest, so I picked it up.

A locket. Plain gold, a little dented. Inside, on the left, a tiny painted portrait the size of a thumbnail.

It wasn't Rowan.

It was a young man, maybe sixteen or seventeen when it was painted. He had her coloring and her stubborn chin. But his mouth was easier. He had the face of someone who'd been laughing a second before the painter told him to hold still.

Something in my stomach turned over, slow, like a fish in cold water.

I didn't know why. I looked at that face and found nothing I could name. Just that slow turn. And the feeling that, somewhere deep in a house, a door was closing very quietly.

She held out her hand. I gave it to her.

"My brother," she said. She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the portrait. "Corentin."

Behind us, someone in the yard whispered the name. I heard it pass from person to person. Then it stopped.

Everyone at Hallowcrest knows that name. Two years ago, the Black Order executed Corentin Hollen, the Justiciar's son, for treason against the crown. There was no trial. There was never a body. There was only a notice, one sheet, pinned on the gates of House Hollen and on the board in the atrium. It hung there for a week, between the club lists and the lost gloves.

Lysette closed the locket. Her thumb stayed on the clasp.

At lunch, I thought I knew what she kept reaching for every time she looked at Rowan. I'd had a whole theory.

I looked away first. I didn't choose to. I just stared at the sand, at the scuff where she'd knelt. My ribs hurt. I didn't look up again until she spoke.

She'd stood. The chain hung from her fist in two pieces. She wasn't talking to me. Or to the girl still apologizing. Or to Okoru, who had walked to the edge of the ring and stopped.

"Lysette," the girl said, very small. "I'm so sorry. I didn't mean—"

"It's fine. It's not your fault." Lysette didn't look at her. "It's old. It was bound to break."

She straightened.

"I will prove," Lysette said, to the yard, to the east wall, to the sky above it, "that they are monsters."

The girl stopped apologizing. Nobody said a word.

"Wren," Okoru said quietly, from the edge of the ring. "Sit."

"I was about to, sir."

"Good."

I sat back down on the bench. After a while, I noticed my right hand was pressed flat against my ribs. Hard. Like something under there needed holding in.
