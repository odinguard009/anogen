# Eleven Blocks

The notice board in the atrium had been there since the academy was founded, and in a hundred and three years nobody had thought to replace the cork. It was black with old pinholes, so dense in places that new notices hung by a single tack and turned slowly whenever the doors opened. On the first morning of the week a clean sheet went up in the middle of it, pinned at all four corners by someone who meant it.

*BELL ASSESSMENT, YEAR FIVE. Order of candidates.*

I read it twice. Then I took the clerk's ledger out of my inside pocket, opened it to the last six pages, and held it beside the sheet as if I were checking my own time slot, which, to be fair, I also was.

Same names. Same order: a boy called Grimwald first, then Felix, then Clara Belmont, then a run of riding-club girls, none of it alphabetical, because the Bell Office sorts by dormitory floor and then by height, for reasons that died with whoever started doing it. The ledger matched the board name for name, including the misspelling of Bastien Ardenne's surname with two n's in the middle.

I'd spent two nights on that list. I'd had a theory. The list was in a careful, rounded hand, and the order matched no roll I knew, and so I'd decided it had come from somewhere private: an office, a registrar, a desk with a lock. Someone inside.

Someone inside had pinned it to a public board. Anyone who could walk into the atrium could have copied it, and two thousand four hundred people walk into the atrium.

Two nights. I'd be ashamed of myself if I had the energy.

The only thing the board didn't have was the circle round Rowan Tallis. I put the ledger away.

* * *

Lunch at Hallowcrest is served in the long refectory at the noon bell, and it's the one hour of the day when the whole academy is in one room and can see who you're sitting with. I've had a good arrangement for five years. I sit with Felix. People see Felix, and they see a shape next to Felix that might be his coat.

That arrangement lasted until Lysette Hollen put her tray down across from me.

"Committee," she said, before I could say anything. "You're a flight risk at lunch. You were a flight risk at lunch all last week."

"Last week I was in the library."

"You were on the roof."

"The library has a roof."

Felix, beside me, turned a page of his book with the air of a man who intends to see none of this.

She ate the way she did everything: quickly, efficiently, cutting the potato into identical squares. Twice I saw her look down the hall to where Rowan Tallis sat at the Year Five center table with Clara Belmont on one side and three people trying to sit on the other. He was telling a story with both hands. Clara was laughing. Each time she looked, Lysette's left hand went to her collar, to a thin gold chain that disappeared under her shirt, and touched it, and came away.

I watched him too, for a different reason. He'd got to the part of the story where something fell on someone, and he mimed it, and the whole center table went up in a roar. Clara put her hand on his arm to steady herself. Somebody threw a bread roll. In my pocket, against my ribs, was a page with his name circled twice in another person's ink, and six words after it, and I was the only one in a room of four hundred people who knew it was there.

That's the job. I've never minded it. It's only that the refectory is very loud at noon, and from the south tables you can hear every word of every story and none of them are about anything you can tell.

I filed the chain instead. A girl in love with a boy who's laughing with someone else, and a chain round her neck she reaches for when it hurts. I thought I knew what was on the end of it. A token, a dried flower, a button off his coat. People keep strange things.

"Oh, *look*," said a voice above my head. "My saviour slept through breakfast again."

Princess Seraphine Aurenmark sat down next to Lysette without asking. She had her brother's coloring and none of his posture, and a pastry in each hand, one of which she put on my plate.

"I was at breakfast," I said.

"You were *present* at breakfast. That's different. Hollen, he was present at breakfast, wasn't he? You'd know. I hear you're his keeper now."

"I'm his *supervisor*," Lysette said. "By Committee assignment."

"Self-assigned."

"Committee assignment," Lysette said, with the dangerous patience of a person repeating herself to a magistrate, "may be initiated by any officer."

Seraphine smiled at her. It was a very pleasant smile and Lysette's ears went red under it. Seraphine and I have known each other since we were twelve; she has a way of poking at people to see what's underneath that is mostly kind. Lysette didn't know that yet, and I didn't feel it was my place to explain.

"He saved my life once," Seraphine told her, leaning in. "Did he mention?"

"No."

"He never does. It was very dashing. Then he went to sleep in a hedge."

"It was a ditch," I said.

"See? Modest."

Felix turned another page. Lysette cut a potato square in half, and then in half again, until it couldn't reasonably be cut any more.

* * *

Arms class at the ninth bell is held in the Long Yard, a strip of packed sand under the east wall, and taught by Tesfane Okoru, who has one arm and needs about a quarter of it to put any student in the sand.

He paired us off by rank. That gave me Bastien Ardenne, eleventh, who'd spent three years waiting to be ranked above me and saw no reason, today, to wait any longer.

"Batons," said Okoru. "Points. Three touches. Out of the ring is a touch."

I have a rule for class. I show the lance and I block with it. Nothing else. Rank eight blocks well and attacks without conviction and loses about a third of his bouts to people ranked below him, which is how he stays rank eight.

Bastien came in hard. A good straight thrust, a feint high, a cut to the ribs from the left. I caught all three on the shaft and gave ground a half-step each time. He was quick and he knew it and he dropped his left shoulder before every real attack, the way some people clear their throat before they lie. His weight lived on his front foot. His guard came back a hair late on the right after every cut. If I'd been trying, it would've been three exchanges, and the third would've put him on his back in the sand wondering where the sky had gone.

He came again. I blocked. Again. Block.

Eleven exchanges. I blocked eleven times, and by the eleventh I could have told you where he'd be standing a minute from now, and he hadn't touched me once. That was a problem. Rank eight doesn't do that. People were starting to watch the ring.

On the twelfth I let his feint pull my lance high, and his baton came in underneath and took me in the ribs, the left side, below the arm.

It was a decent hit. Better than I'd budgeted for. The breath went out of me in a noise I didn't approve of, and I stepped back to make room for the next one, and my heel went over the chalk.

"Touch," said Okoru. "And out. Two."

Bastien took the third on my forearm a moment later because I let him, and the bout was done, and he grinned at me with real pleasure, the uncomplicated kind, and offered his hand. I took it. He'll tell people about it for a week. Good. I'd rather be someone Bastien Ardenne beat than someone anyone talks about.

My ribs disagreed, but they're not consulted.

Okoru didn't say "next." He stood at the edge of the ring with his one hand tucked into his belt, and looked at me. It went on longer than a teacher's look usually goes on. His face is lined and very calm, and he has the eyes of a man who once had to count how many people came back.

"Wren," he said.

"Sir."

"You watched his left shoulder eleven times."

I didn't answer that. There was nothing to say to it that wouldn't be worse.

"Next pair," said Okoru, and turned away. I went and sat on the bench with my ribs, and didn't look at him again, and could feel him not looking at me for the rest of the hour.

* * *

Lysette was paired with a girl from the second floor, Clara's floor, and she was very, very good. Two short batons, Golden Seal style, held close, all wrists and angles, and a way of watching her opponent that made you feel cross-examined. She took the first two touches cleanly.

On the third, the other girl's baton glanced off Lysette's guard and up across her collarbone, and the thin gold chain at her neck snapped.

It fell into the sand. Whatever was on it fell with it and came open.

Lysette stopped as if she'd been struck properly. The bout was over; the other girl was already apologizing. Lysette didn't hear her. She was on one knee in the sand, and I was nearer than anyone, so I picked it up.

A locket. Plain gold, a little dented. Inside, on the left, a tiny painted portrait the size of a thumbnail.

Not Rowan.

A young man, sixteen or seventeen when it was painted. Her coloring, her stubborn chin, but easier around the mouth, the kind of face that had been laughing a second before the painter told it to hold still.

Something in my stomach turned over, slowly, like a fish in cold water.

I didn't know why. I looked at the face and there was nothing there I could name, just that slow turn, and the feeling of a door somewhere far back in the house closing very quietly.

She held out her hand. I gave it to her.

"My brother," she said. She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the portrait. "Corentin."

Behind us, someone in the yard said the name under their breath. I heard it pass from person to person and stop.

Everyone at Hallowcrest knows the name. Two years ago the Black Order had executed Corentin Hollen, the Justiciar's son, for treason against the crown. There had been no trial. There had never been a body. There had been a notice, a single sheet, posted on the gates of House Hollen and on the board in the atrium, among the club rosters and the lost gloves, and it had hung there for a week.

Lysette closed the locket. Her thumb stayed on the clasp.

I'd thought I knew what she was reaching for at lunch, every time she looked at Rowan. I'd had a whole theory about it.

I looked away first. I didn't decide to. I was just looking at the sand, at the scuff where she'd knelt, and my ribs hurt, and I didn't look up again until she spoke.

She'd stood. The chain hung from her fist in two pieces. She wasn't talking to me, or to the girl who was still apologizing, or to Okoru, who had come to the edge of the ring and stopped there.

"I will prove," Lysette said, to the yard, to the east wall, to the sky over it, "that they are monsters."

The girl stopped apologizing. Nobody said anything.

I sat back down on the bench. After a while I realized my right hand was pressed flat to my ribs, hard, as if something there needed holding in.
