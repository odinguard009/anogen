# Eleven Blocks

The notice board in the front hall is as old as the school. In a hundred and three years, nobody changed the cork. It was black with old pinholes. In some places the holes were so close together that new notices hung by one tack and spun every time the doors opened.

On the first morning of the week, a clean sheet went up in the middle. It was pinned at all four corners by someone who meant it.

*BELL ASSESSMENT, YEAR FIVE. Order of candidates.*

I read it twice. The hall was cold and smelled of floor wax. I took the clerk's ledger from my inside pocket and held its last six pages next to the sheet, like a boy checking his own time.

"Looking for yourself?" said Felix, behind me. He had a roll in his hand.

"I'm eighth. I know where I am."

"Then why are you comparing?"

"Habit."

Same names. Same order. It was not in alphabet order. The school office sorts by floor, then by height, and the reason died with whoever started it.

The ledger matched the board, name for name. It even matched the spelling mistake in Bastien Ardenne's last name.

I had spent two nights on that list. It was written in a careful, round hand, and the order matched no list I knew. So I decided it came from somewhere private. A locked desk. Someone on the inside.

But someone on the inside had pinned it to a public board. Anyone who could walk into the front hall could have copied it. And two thousand people walk into the front hall.

"Two nights wasted," I said.

"On what?" Felix asked.

"Nothing. A theory."

"Was it a good one?"

"It was very proud of itself."

The only thing the board did not have was the circle around Rowan Tallis. I put the ledger away.

* * *

Lunch is at the noon bell, in the long dining hall. It is the one hour when the whole school sits in one room and can see who you sit with.

For five years, I had a good system. I sat with Felix. People saw Felix, and next to him a shape that might be his coat.

The system ended when Lysette Hollen put her tray down across from me.

"Committee," she said, before I could speak. "You're a flight risk at lunch. You were one all last week."

"Last week I was in the library."

"You were on the roof."

"The library has a roof."

Felix turned a page. He looked like a man who planned to see nothing.

"Eat," Lysette told me. "Then we talk about your timetable."

"We talked about it yesterday."

"We talked about it badly."

"Felix," Lysette said. "Is he always like this?"

"Like what?"

"Slippery."

"Only when he's awake," said Felix, and went back to his book.

Lysette ate the way she did everything, fast and neat. Twice I saw her look down the hall. Rowan Tallis sat at the big center table, with Clara Belmont on one side of him. Clara was a small girl with curly brown hair and a loud laugh. Three people tried to squeeze in on his other side. He was telling a story with both hands.

Each time Lysette looked, her left hand went to her collar. A thin gold chain went down under her shirt. She touched it. Then her hand dropped.

I watched him too, for a different reason.

"He's very loud," I said.

"He is *happy*," said Lysette. "It's different." She stabbed a potato.

In my pocket was a page with his name circled twice in someone else's ink. I was the only person in the room who knew.

That is the job. I have never minded it. It is only that the dining hall is loud at noon. You can hear every word of every story, and none of them tell you anything.

So I filed the chain instead. A girl in love with a boy who laughs with someone else, and a chain she touches when it hurts. I thought I knew what hung on the end. A token. A dried flower. A button from his coat. People keep strange things.

"Oh, *look*," said a voice above my head. "My savior slept through breakfast again."

Princess Seraphine sat down next to Lysette without asking. She had her brother's fair hair and none of his straight back. She had a pastry in each hand, and she put one on my plate.

"I was at breakfast," I said.

"You were *present* at breakfast. That's different. Hollen, he was present, wasn't he? You'd know. I hear you're his keeper now."

"I'm his *supervisor*," Lysette said. "By Committee order."

"You gave it to yourself."

"Any officer," Lysette said, in the voice you use with a judge, "can start a Committee order."

Seraphine smiled at her. It was a very pleasant smile. Lysette's ears went pink.

Seraphine and I have known each other since we were twelve. Most of the time she is kind. Lysette did not know that yet.

"He saved my life once," Seraphine told her. She leaned in. "Did he say so?"

"No."

"He never does. It was very dashing. Then he went to sleep in a hedge."

"It was a ditch," I said.

"See? Modest." She pointed her pastry at Lysette. "Do you know what he said afterward? 'Are you hurt?' Nothing else. Then he left."

"I was tired."

"He is always tired," Seraphine told Lysette. "Fix that, and you are a genius."

Felix turned another page. Lysette cut a potato square in half. Then in half again. Then again, until it could not be cut anymore.

* * *

Arms class is at the ninth bell, in the Long Yard. It is a strip of packed sand under the east wall, and in the afternoon it smells of hot dust and old leather. The teacher is Okoru. He has one arm, and he needs about a quarter of it to put any student in the sand.

He paired us by rank. That gave me Bastien Ardenne, a tall boy with a thick neck and a quick laugh. He was rank eleven. For three years he had waited to be ranked above me, and today he saw no reason to wait longer.

"Batons," said Okoru. "Three touches. Out of the ring counts as a touch."

I have one rule for class. I show my lance, and I only block with it. A rank eight boy blocks well, attacks without heart, and loses a third of his fights. That is how he stays rank eight.

"Ready, Wren?" Bastien called.

"No."

"Good!"

The ring was ten steps across, drawn in chalk. Bastien stood three steps from me, with his back to the sun. I stood near the east wall, so I had no room to run. That was fine. I did not plan to run.

Bastien came in hard. A straight thrust. A high fake. A cut to my ribs from the left. I caught all three on the shaft and gave up half a step each time.

He was quick, and he knew it. Before every real attack, he dropped his left shoulder, the way some people clear their throat before they lie. His weight sat on his front foot. After each cut, his guard came back a little late on the right. If I had been trying, it would have taken three exchanges. The third would have put him on his back, wondering where the sky went.

He came again. I blocked. Again. Block.

I blocked eleven times. By the eleventh, I knew where he would stand a minute later. He had not touched me once. That was a problem. A rank eight boy does not do that, and people were starting to watch the ring.

So on the twelfth, I let his fake pull my lance high. His baton came in low and hit my ribs, on the left side, under the arm.

It was a good hit. Better than I had planned for. The air left me with a noise I did not like. I stepped back to make room, and my heel crossed the chalk.

"Touch," said Okoru. "And out. Two."

A moment later, Bastien took the third on my forearm, because I let him. The fight was over. He grinned at me with real joy, the simple kind, and held out his hand. I shook it.

"Tell me that wasn't amazing," he said.

"It was fine."

"*Fine?* I beat the eighth rank!"

"Congratulations."

"Tell everyone," I said.

"I will!"

He would tell people for a week. Good. I would rather be someone Bastien Ardenne beat than someone people talk about.

My ribs disagreed, but nobody asked them.

Okoru did not say "next." He stood at the edge of the ring with his one hand in his belt, and he looked at me for longer than a teacher should. His face is lined and calm. He has the eyes of a man who once had to count how many came back.

"Wren," he said.

"Sir."

"You watched his left shoulder eleven times."

I did not answer. Anything I said would have been worse.

"I like his left shoulder," I said. "It's honest."

"Honest shoulders are rare."

"Is that a compliment, sir?"

"It's something I noticed. Next pair."

He turned away.

I went and sat on the bench with my ribs. For the rest of the hour, I felt him not looking at me.

* * *

Lysette was paired with a girl from the second floor. The two of them stood at the far end of the sand, near the shade of the wall, and the other girl was bigger by a head. Lysette did not seem to mind.

She used two short batons, held close. It was all wrists and angles. She watched her opponent in a way that made you feel questioned. She took the first two touches cleanly.

On the third, the other girl's baton slid off Lysette's guard. It flicked up across her collarbone. The thin gold chain snapped.

It fell into the sand. Whatever hung on it fell too, and popped open.

Lysette stopped as if she had been hit for real. The fight was over. The other girl was already saying sorry. Lysette did not hear. She dropped to one knee in the sand. I was closest, so I picked it up.

It was a locket, plain gold and a little dented. Inside, on the left, was a tiny painted face the size of a thumbnail.

It was not Rowan.

It was a young man, maybe sixteen or seventeen when the picture was painted. He had her coloring and her stubborn chin. But his mouth was softer. He had the face of someone who had been laughing a second before the painter told him to hold still.

Something in my stomach turned over, slowly, like a fish in cold water.

I did not know why. I looked at that face and found nothing I could name. Only that slow turn. And the feeling that, somewhere deep in a house, a door was closing very quietly.

She held out her hand. I gave it to her.

"My brother," she said. She was not looking at me. She was looking at the picture. "Corentin."

Behind us, someone in the yard whispered the name. I heard it pass from person to person. Then it stopped.

Everyone in the school knows that name. Two years ago, the Black Order killed Corentin Hollen for treason against the crown. There was no trial and there was no body. There was only a notice, one sheet, pinned on the school gate and on the board in the front hall. It hung there for a week, between the club lists and the lost gloves.

Lysette closed the locket. Her thumb stayed on the clasp.

At lunch, I thought I knew what she kept reaching for every time she looked at Rowan. I had a whole theory. It was wrong.

"Is he—" I began.

"Don't," she said.

I looked away first. I did not choose to. I stared at the sand, at the mark where she had knelt, and my ribs hurt.

She had stood. The chain hung from her fist in two pieces. She was not talking to me, or to the girl who still said sorry, or to Okoru, who had walked to the edge of the ring and stopped.

"Lysette," the girl said, very small. "I'm so sorry. I didn't mean—"

"It's fine. It's not your fault." Lysette did not look at her. "It's old. It was going to break."

She stood straight.

"I will prove," Lysette said, to the yard, to the east wall, to the sky above it, "that they are monsters."

The girl stopped saying sorry. Nobody said a word.

"Wren," Okoru said quietly, from the edge of the ring. "Sit."

"I was about to, sir."

"Good."

I sat back down on the bench. After a while, I saw that my right hand was pressed flat against my ribs. Hard. As if something under there needed holding in.
