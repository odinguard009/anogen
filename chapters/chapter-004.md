# Eleven Blocks

The notice board in the atrium is as old as the academy. In a hundred and three years, no one thought to change the cork. It was black with old pinholes. In some places the holes were so close together that new notices hung by one tack and spun whenever the doors opened.

On the first morning of the week, a clean sheet went up in the middle. It was pinned at all four corners by someone who meant it.

*BELL ASSESSMENT, YEAR FIVE. Order of candidates.*

I read it twice. Then I took the clerk's ledger from my inside pocket. I opened it to the last six pages and held it next to the sheet. I looked like I was checking my own time. To be fair, I also was.

Same names. Same order. A boy called Grimwald was first. Then Felix. Then Clara Belmont. Then a run of riding-club girls. It was not in alphabet order. The Bell Office sorts by room floor, then by height. The reason died with whoever started it.

The ledger matched the board name for name. It even matched the spelling mistake in Bastien Ardenne's last name, with two n's in the middle.

I had spent two nights on that list. I had a theory. The list was written in a careful, round hand. The order matched no list I knew. So I decided it came from somewhere private. A registrar's office. A locked desk. Someone on the inside.

Someone on the inside had pinned it to a public board. Anyone who could walk into the atrium could have copied it. And two thousand four hundred people walk into the atrium.

Two nights wasted. I would feel ashamed if I had the energy.

The only thing the board did not have was the circle around Rowan Tallis. I put the ledger away.

* * *

Lunch at Hallowcrest is served in the long refectory at the noon bell. It is the one hour when the whole academy sits in one room and can see who you sit with.

For five years, I had a good system. I sat with Felix. People saw Felix, and next to him a shape that might be his coat.

The system ended when Lysette Hollen put her tray down across from me.

"Committee," she said, before I could speak. "You're a flight risk at lunch. You were one all last week."

"Last week I was in the library."

"You were on the roof."

"The library has a roof."

Felix turned a page. He had the look of a man who plans to see nothing.

"Felix," Lysette said. "Is he always like this?"

"Like what?"

"Slippery."

"Only when he's awake," said Felix, and went back to his book.

Lysette ate the way she did everything. Fast and neat. She cut her potato into equal squares. Twice I saw her look down the hall. Rowan Tallis sat at the Year Five center table. Clara Belmont sat on one side of him. Three people tried to squeeze in on the other side. He was telling a story with both hands.

Clara was laughing. Each time Lysette looked, her left hand went to her collar. A thin gold chain went down under her shirt. She touched it. Then her hand dropped.

I watched him too, for a different reason. He got to the part where something fell on somebody. He acted it out. The whole table roared. Clara grabbed his arm to stay in her seat. Someone threw a bread roll.

In my pocket was a page with his name circled twice in someone else's ink. I was the only person in the room who knew.

That is the job. I have never minded it. It is only that the refectory is very loud at noon. You can hear every word of every story, and none of them tell you anything.

So I filed the chain instead. A girl in love with a boy who laughs with someone else. A chain she touches when it hurts. I thought I knew what hung on the end. A token. A dried flower. A button from his coat. People keep strange things.

"Oh, *look*," said a voice above my head. "My savior slept through breakfast again."

Princess Seraphine Aurenmark sat down next to Lysette without asking. She had her brother's coloring and none of his straight back. She had a pastry in each hand. She put one on my plate.

"I was at breakfast," I said.

"You were *present* at breakfast. That's different. Hollen, he was present at breakfast, wasn't he? You'd know. I hear you're his keeper now."

"I'm his *supervisor*," Lysette said. "By Committee order."

"You gave it to yourself."

"A Committee order," Lysette said, in the voice you use with a judge, "can be started by any officer."

Seraphine smiled at her. It was a very pleasant smile. Lysette's ears went red.

Seraphine and I have known each other since we were twelve. She likes to poke at people to see what is under the surface. Most of the time she is kind. Lysette did not know that yet. It was not my place to tell her.

"He saved my life once," Seraphine told her. She leaned in. "Did he say so?"

"No."

"He never does. It was very dashing. Then he went to sleep in a hedge."

"It was a ditch," I said.

"See? Modest."

Felix turned another page. Lysette cut a potato square in half. Then in half again. Then again, until it could not be cut anymore.

* * *

Arms class at the ninth bell is in the Long Yard. It is a strip of packed sand under the east wall. The teacher is Tesfane Okoru. He has one arm, and he needs about a quarter of it to put any student in the sand.

He paired us by rank. That gave me Bastien Ardenne, rank eleven. For three years he had waited to be ranked above me. Today he saw no reason to wait longer.

"Batons," said Okoru. "Points. Three touches. Out of the ring counts as a touch."

I have one rule for class. I show the lance, and I only block with it. Nothing else. A rank eight boy blocks well, attacks without heart, and loses a third of his fights to people ranked below him. That is how he stays rank eight.

Bastien came in hard. A straight thrust. A high fake. A cut to my ribs from the left. I caught all three on the shaft and gave up half a step each time.

He was quick, and he knew it. Before every real attack, he dropped his left shoulder, as some people clear their throat before they lie. His weight sat on his front foot. After each cut, his guard came back a little late on the right. If I had been trying, it would have taken three exchanges. The third would have put him on his back, wondering where the sky went.

He came again. I blocked. Again. Block.

I blocked eleven times. By the eleventh, I could have told you where he would stand a minute later. He had not touched me once. That was a problem. A rank eight boy does not do that. People were starting to watch the ring.

On the twelfth, I let his fake pull my lance high. His baton came in low and hit my ribs. It hit the left side, under the arm.

It was a good hit. Better than I had planned for. The air left me with a noise I did not like. I stepped back to make room, and my heel crossed the chalk.

"Touch," said Okoru. "And out. Two."

A moment later, Bastien took the third on my forearm, because I let him. The fight was over. He grinned at me with real joy, the simple kind. He held out his hand. I shook it.

"Tell me that wasn't amazing," he said.

"It was fine."

"*Fine?* I beat the eighth rank!"

"Congratulations."

He would tell people for a week. Good. I would rather be someone Bastien Ardenne beat than someone people talk about.

My ribs disagreed, but no one asked them.

Okoru did not say "next." He stood at the edge of the ring with his one hand in his belt. He looked at me. It went on longer than a teacher's look should. His face is lined and calm. He has the eyes of a man who once had to count how many came back.

"Wren," he said.

"Sir."

"You watched his left shoulder eleven times."

I did not answer. Anything I said would have been worse.

"I like his left shoulder," I said. "It's honest."

"Honest shoulders are rare."

"Is that a compliment, sir?"

"It's something I noticed. Next pair."

He turned away.

I went and sat on the bench with my ribs. I did not look at him again. For the rest of the hour, I felt him not looking at me.

* * *

Lysette was paired with a girl from the second floor. It was Clara's floor. And Lysette was very, very good. She used two short batons, Golden Seal style, held close. It was all wrists and angles. She watched her opponent in a way that made you feel questioned. She took the first two touches cleanly.

On the third, the other girl's baton slid off Lysette's guard. It flicked up across her collarbone. The thin gold chain snapped.

It fell into the sand. Whatever hung on it fell too, and popped open.

Lysette stopped as if she had been hit for real. The fight was over. The other girl was already saying sorry. Lysette did not hear. She dropped to one knee in the sand. I was closest, so I picked it up.

It was a locket. Plain gold, a little dented. Inside, on the left, was a tiny painted face the size of a thumbnail.

It was not Rowan.

It was a young man, maybe sixteen or seventeen when it was painted. He had her coloring and her stubborn chin. But his mouth was softer. He had the face of someone who had been laughing a second before the painter told him to hold still.

Something in my stomach turned over, slowly, like a fish in cold water.

I did not know why. I looked at that face and found nothing I could name. Only that slow turn. And the feeling that, somewhere deep in a house, a door was closing very quietly.

She held out her hand. I gave it to her.

"My brother," she said. She was not looking at me. She was looking at the picture. "Corentin."

Behind us, someone in the yard whispered the name. I heard it pass from person to person. Then it stopped.

Everyone at Hallowcrest knows that name. Two years ago, the Black Order killed Corentin Hollen, the Justiciar's son, for treason against the crown. There was no trial. There was never a body. There was only a notice, one sheet, pinned on the gates of House Hollen and on the board in the atrium. It hung there for a week, between the club lists and the lost gloves.

Lysette closed the locket. Her thumb stayed on the clasp.

At lunch, I thought I knew what she kept reaching for every time she looked at Rowan. I had a whole theory.

I looked away first. I did not choose to. I stared at the sand, at the mark where she had knelt. My ribs hurt. I did not look up until she spoke.

She had stood. The chain hung from her fist in two pieces. She was not talking to me. Or to the girl who still said sorry. Or to Okoru, who had walked to the edge of the ring and stopped.

"Lysette," the girl said, very small. "I'm so sorry. I didn't mean—"

"It's fine. It's not your fault." Lysette did not look at her. "It's old. It was going to break."

She stood straight.

"I will prove," Lysette said, to the yard, to the east wall, to the sky above it, "that they are monsters."

The girl stopped saying sorry. No one said a word.

"Wren," Okoru said quietly, from the edge of the ring. "Sit."

"I was about to, sir."

"Good."

I sat back down on the bench. After a while, I saw that my right hand was pressed flat against my ribs. Hard. As if something under there needed holding in.
