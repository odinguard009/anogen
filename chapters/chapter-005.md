# Latch

Felix's arrow flew forty paces and pinned the painted wooden Husk to the wall by its third eye. Chalk dust was still drifting when he spoke. He didn't lower the bow.

"Eaten today?"

"We're in a maze."

"That's not an answer."

"Half a roll."

"Better." He nocked another arrow. "Left or right?"

The passage split ahead of us under a hanging glowstone. Both ways looked the same. Rough stone, wet in the seams, a gutter down the middle that smelled like old rain. The Undercroft runs under the whole plateau. Years One to Four learn it in pieces. Year Five is supposed to know it well enough to run it in teams of three. Against the clock. With targets to hit and wardens to dodge.

"Left," said Lysette, from behind us. "Right loops back. I've seen the map."

"There's a map?" said Felix.

"There's always a map. You just ask the right clerk."

She'd put herself on our team. Committee assignment, started by an officer. Felix had looked at me when she announced it. Then at her. Then at his bow. He said nothing at all. From Felix, that's a whole speech.

"Is she always going to do that?" Felix murmured to me.

"What?"

"Know things."

"Probably."

We went left.

* * *

The drill had started a quarter hour earlier, at the bottom of the Long Stair. Okoru read out the rules in the voice he uses for things he's said two hundred times.

Three per team. Hit nine targets. Avoid the drill-wardens. Those were old Year Three constructs, bronze and stone. Slow. About as dangerous as a wardrobe that doesn't like you. A warden's touch put you out. No Tenets above Spark. No blades.

"Shouting," Okoru had added, "is allowed."

Somebody laughed.

"Was that a joke, sir?" a boy asked.

"No," said Okoru. Nobody laughed after that.

* * *

Six targets in, the left passage opened on one side into a long iron grille. It ran from waist height to the ceiling. Through it, we could see the next run over, parallel to ours.

Rowan Tallis's team was in it. Him, Clara Belmont, and a broad, quiet boy from the north towers. Rowan was in front, of course. His Tenet was lit at Spark like everyone's. But on him, even Spark looked like a lamp turned up. A warm gold edge all around him. Like he'd been cut out of a sunrise and pasted on the gray.

"Come on, slowpoke!" he called to Clara. She shoved him. He laughed. The laugh came through the grille and bounced down our passage.

Lysette stopped.

Only a second. Just long enough to look. Her hand started toward her collar, where the chain used to be. It found nothing. It dropped.

"Hollen," I said. "Eyes front."

"I am looking front."

"You're looking left."

"I'm *checking the flank*."

I didn't argue. I was looking at the far end of our passage. There was a low side door there, the kind used for repairs. It was open.

It had been shut when I checked the map. I'd also asked the right clerk.

* * *

The warden came out at a run.

That was the first wrong thing. Drill-wardens don't run. They don't have the joints. This one did. It came through the low door bent double. Then it unfolded. Nine feet of stacked stone and bronze bands. A head like an anvil. A dull crystal in the chest, where a heart would be.

On its right arm, where the drill models have a padded club, it had a blade. Training steel, not sharp, a yard long. But that much stone swinging it would break bones like a dropped rock breaks a plate.

It didn't go for Felix, who was closest. It didn't go for me.

It charged straight down the passage at Lysette. She was looking through a grille at a boy laughing. She turned her head half a second late.

"Hollen!" Felix yelled. "*Left!*"

"I can see it!"

"Then *move*!"

* * *

When I'm about to choose, something happens. Everything slows down and gets quiet. I can hear my options lining up like men at a door.

Felix was drawing his bow. His arrow would do nothing. A blunt shaft against a stone chest.

Lysette had her batons out. Good ones, Golden Seal style. She'd block once, maybe twice. The third swing would go through her guard like wind through a window.

I could call Gorrath. Nine feet of shield-knight out of the shadow behind the grille. In front of Felix and Lysette Hollen. And, through the bars, the Dawn Boy's whole team. That would end rank eight, and a lot more.

I could put Candor through the crystal. That would end rank eight just as fast.

What I was allowed to use, in front of witnesses, was a short dark lance for blocking.

"Felix," I said. "Shoot the crystal. Keep it looking at you."

"It's looking at *her*!"

"Then shoot anyway."

I went.

* * *

The passage was bad for fighting something big. It was good for fighting something long. Seven feet wide. Glowstones every twenty paces on the left wall and none on the right. So every lit stretch had a hard line of shadow down one side. Edge after edge after edge.

The thing's blade was a yard of steel on a nine-foot frame. It needed room to swing. The grille gave it none on one side. The wall gave it none on the other.

It had to come straight. Overhead, or a thrust.

It chose overhead. Lysette got a baton up. The baton snapped in half. The shock ran down her arm and dropped her on one knee. The construct pulled its blade back for another swing.

I came in from her left, lance level, thrusting at its elbow. It saw me. It turned the blade to block.

I knocked.

One beat, like rapping on a door as you walk by. The lance folded in my hands, mid-thrust. It opened out into a door. Tall. Narrow. Black. A panel of dark wood with an iron ring in the middle. It stood between the construct and Lysette Hollen. The training blade came down on it.

The door slammed.

That's the only word for the sound. Like a house slamming its front door in a temper. The blade hit the wood. The panel snapped shut on the steel, the way a door shuts on a careless hand. I twisted my wrists. The flat of the blade slid along the wood, up against the iron ring, and stuck. Wedged tight between the ring and the panel.

Latch. My mother called it that, when the kitchen door caught its own lock and she had to shove it with her shoulder. I don't know why that came to mind.

The construct pulled. I leaned. It was much heavier than me. I stood on wet stone, and my bruised ribs had opinions. For a second, it was pure weight. Its weight going back, mine going forward, the blade grinding in the ring.

"The chest," I said. "The crystal. Now."

"I know where it is!" Lysette snapped.

"Then go!"

I wasn't talking to Felix.

Lysette came up off her knee. She had one baton left. She didn't freeze and she didn't swing wild. She stepped left, inside the line of the trapped arm, where it couldn't reach her. Then she drove the butt of the baton into the crystal with her whole weight. Like hammering a nail.

The crystal cracked across.

The construct stopped pulling. It stood there, blade stuck in my door. A long, dry sound came out of it, like sand poured down a pipe. It went down on one knee and stayed there. Its anvil head tipped forward until it rested on top of the door.

I let the door fold back into a lance. The training blade dropped and rang on the stones.

* * *

Felix still had an arrow on the string. He lowered it slowly.

"That," he said, "was a warden."

"An old one," I said.

"They don't run."

"This one was keen."

"Keen."

"Very."

"Eden."

"Yes?"

"Nothing." He sighed.

He looked at me the way he does. Not suspicious. Just very patient, like a man ready to wait years for an answer. Then he went to check the side passage, bow up, in case anything else was keen.

Lysette stood in front of the construct with half a baton in her hand.

Her hands were shaking. Not much. Enough that the broken end ticked against her ring. She looked at them like they belonged to someone she'd have to write up.

I half expected her to call up her Tenet right there. To make the passage replay itself in ghost-light, the last half minute, step by step. If she had, she'd have seen my lance fold. She'd have seen a door. She didn't. She just stared at her hands.

"Are you all right?" I asked.

"I'm fine."

"Your hands—"

"I *said* I'm fine."

"I didn't need that," she said.

"I know."

She glanced up, sharp, ready to be angry about how I said it. I don't think she found anything. I didn't mean it the way people mean it. She'd stepped inside the arm. She'd hit the right spot. I'd only held a door.

The shaking got worse. She moved the broken baton to her other hand and closed the first one tight. That didn't help either.

My handkerchief was in her pocket. I knew because a corner showed. A bit of silver stitching against dark wool. I could've pointed. That would've been strange. I stood there with nothing in my hands and nothing useful to say.

Then I picked up the half of her baton that had snapped off. I held it out to her, like she'd want it.

She took it. She looked at it. "This is no use to anyone," she said.

"No."

She put it in her pocket anyway. With the handkerchief.

* * *

Okoru came down the passage at a walk. That was somehow worse than a run. Behind him, through the grille, Rowan's whole team had stopped, pressed up against the bars. Rowan's gold glow had gone out completely. He was looking at Lysette.

"Hollen?" he called. "Lysette, are you hurt? Did it get you? It looked like it got you, your arm went all—"

"I'm perfectly fine, thank you, Tallis."

"You don't *sound* fine."

"Then you're not listening properly."

She didn't look at him when she said it. I noticed that. Clara put a hand on Rowan's sleeve. He closed his mouth. He kept looking.

Okoru looked at the construct. At the blade on the floor. At the low door, still open. Last, and longest, he looked at me. At the lance held low in my left hand. At the scuff marks on the stone where my heels had slid.

Then he crouched by the construct. He put his one hand flat on its chest, beside the cracked crystal, and pressed.

The crystal fell out of its socket in two pieces. Behind it, there should have been the usual tangle of copper wire and charged stone. Instead there was a hollow the size of a fist.

It was full of dust.

Gray. Fine as flour. Packed so tight it kept the shape of the hollow even after the crystal was gone. A little of it sifted onto the flagstones. In the glowstone light, it looked like nothing. Like what you'd sweep up after a fire.

Okoru didn't touch it. He took a folded cloth from his belt with one hand. He laid it over the hollow and tucked in the edges, carefully, the way you'd cover a face.

"Wren," he said. "You blocked it."

"Yes, sir."

"With a lance."

"It was all I had."

"Mm." He pulled the cloth tight. "Hollen. You hit the crystal. Why the crystal?"

"It was glowing, sir."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

He nodded slowly, like both answers were fine and neither was true.

"Who," he said, to nobody, very quietly, "had the keys to the warden store this week?"

Nobody answered. At the edge of the cloth, a thin trail of dust had already reached the gutter. The old rainwater carried it away downhill, into the parts of the Undercroft that aren't on anybody's map.
