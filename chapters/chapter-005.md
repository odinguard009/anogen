# Latch

Felix's arrow flew forty steps and pinned the painted wooden Husk to the wall by its third eye. Chalk dust was still floating in the air when he spoke. He did not lower the bow.

"Eaten today?"

"We're in a maze."

"That's not an answer."

"Half a roll."

"Better." He put another arrow on the string. "Left or right?"

The passage split ahead of us under a hanging glowstone. Both ways looked the same. The stone was rough and wet in the cracks. A gutter ran down the middle, and it smelled of old rain. The Undercroft runs under the whole hill. Years One to Four learn it in pieces. Year Five must know it well enough to run it in teams of three. Against the clock. With targets to hit and wardens to dodge.

"Left," said Lysette, from behind us. "Right goes in a circle. I've seen the map."

"There's a map?" said Felix.

"There's always a map. You just ask the right clerk."

She had put herself on our team. Committee order, started by an officer. Felix had looked at me when she said it. Then he looked at her. Then he looked at his bow. He said nothing at all. From Felix, that is a whole speech.

"Is she always going to do that?" Felix murmured to me.

"What?"

"Know things."

"Probably."

We went left.

* * *

The drill had started a quarter hour earlier, at the bottom of the Long Stair. Okoru read out the rules in the voice he uses for things he has said two hundred times.

Three people per team. Hit nine targets. Avoid the drill-wardens. Those were old Year Three constructs, made of bronze and stone. A construct is a moving statue. They were slow. They were about as dangerous as a wardrobe that does not like you. A warden's touch put you out. No Tenets above Spark. No blades.

"Shouting," Okoru had added, "is allowed."

Someone laughed.

"Was that a joke, sir?" a boy asked.

"No," said Okoru. No one laughed after that.

* * *

Six targets in, the left passage opened on one side into a long iron grille. It ran from waist height to the ceiling. Through it, we could see the next passage over, running next to ours.

Rowan Tallis's team was in it. It was him, Clara Belmont, and a broad, quiet boy from the north towers. Rowan was in front, of course. His Tenet was lit at Spark like everyone's. But on him, even Spark looked like a lamp turned up high. A warm gold edge shone all around him. He looked like he had been cut out of a sunrise and pasted on the gray.

"Come on, slowpoke!" he called to Clara. She shoved him. He laughed. The laugh came through the grille and bounced down our passage.

Lysette stopped.

Only for a second. Just long enough to look. Her hand started toward her collar, where the chain used to be. It found nothing. It dropped.

"Hollen," I said. "Eyes front."

"I am looking front."

"You're looking left."

"I'm *checking the side*."

I did not argue. I was looking at the far end of our passage. There was a low side door there, the kind used for repairs. It was open.

It had been shut when I checked the map. I had asked the right clerk too.

* * *

The warden came out at a run.

That was the first wrong thing. Drill-wardens do not run. They do not have the joints for it. This one did. It came through the low door bent over. Then it stood up. It was nine feet tall, made of stacked stone and bronze bands. Its head was shaped like an anvil. A dull crystal sat in its chest, where a heart would be.

On its right arm, where the drill models have a padded club, it had a blade. It was training steel, not sharp, a yard long. But that much stone swinging it would break bones as a dropped rock breaks a plate.

It did not go for Felix, who was closest. It did not go for me.

It charged straight down the passage at Lysette. She was looking through a grille at a boy laughing. She turned her head half a second late.

"Hollen!" Felix yelled. "*Left!*"

"I can see it!"

"Then *move*!"

* * *

When I am about to choose, everything slows down and gets quiet. I can hear my choices line up like men at a door.

Felix was drawing his bow. His arrow would do nothing. It was a blunt shaft against a stone chest.

Lysette had her batons out. They were good ones, Golden Seal style. She could block once, maybe twice. The third swing would go through her guard like wind through a window.

I could call Gorrath. He is nine feet of shield-knight who comes out of the shadow. I would have to do it in front of Felix and Lysette Hollen. And, through the bars, in front of the Dawn Boy's whole team. That would end rank eight, and much more.

I could put Candor through the crystal. That would end rank eight just as fast.

What I was allowed to use, in front of people, was a short dark lance for blocking.

"Felix," I said. "Shoot the crystal. Keep it looking at you."

"It's looking at *her*!"

"Then shoot anyway."

I went.

* * *

The passage was bad for fighting something big. It was good for fighting something long. It was seven feet wide. Glowstones hung every twenty steps on the left wall and none on the right. So every lit part had a hard line of shadow down one side. Edge after edge after edge.

The thing's blade was a yard of steel on a nine-foot body. It needed room to swing. The grille gave it none on one side. The wall gave it none on the other.

It had to come straight. Overhead, or a thrust.

It chose overhead. Lysette got a baton up. The baton snapped in half. The shock ran down her arm and dropped her on one knee. The construct pulled its blade back for another swing.

I came in from her left, with the lance level, thrusting at its elbow. It saw me. It turned the blade to block.

I knocked.

One beat, like a rap on a door as you walk by. The lance folded in my hands, in the middle of the thrust. It opened into a door. Tall. Narrow. Black. It was a panel of dark wood with an iron ring in the middle. It stood between the construct and Lysette Hollen. The training blade came down on it.

The door slammed.

That is the only word for the sound. It was a house slamming its front door in anger. The blade hit the wood. The panel snapped shut on the steel, the way a door shuts on a careless hand. I twisted my wrists. The flat of the blade slid along the wood, up against the iron ring, and stuck. It was wedged tight between the ring and the panel.

Latch. My mother called it that, when the kitchen door caught its own lock and she had to push it with her shoulder. I don't know why that came to mind.

The construct pulled. I leaned. It was much heavier than me. I stood on wet stone, and my bruised ribs had opinions. For a second, it was only weight. Its weight went back, mine went forward, and the blade ground in the ring.

"The chest," I said. "The crystal. Now."

"I know where it is!" Lysette snapped.

"Then go!"

I was not talking to Felix.

Lysette came up off her knee. She had one baton left. She did not freeze and she did not swing wild. She stepped left, inside the line of the trapped arm, where it could not reach her. Then she drove the end of the baton into the crystal with her whole weight. It was like hammering a nail.

The crystal cracked across.

The construct stopped pulling. It stood there, with its blade stuck in my door. A long, dry sound came out of it, like sand poured down a pipe. It went down on one knee and stayed there. Its anvil head tipped forward until it rested on top of the door.

I let the door fold back into a lance. The training blade dropped and rang on the stones.

* * *

Felix still had an arrow on the string. He lowered it slowly.

"That," he said, "was a warden."

"An old one," I said.

"They don't run."

"This one was keen."

"Keen."

"Very."

"Eden."

"Yes?"

"Nothing." He sighed.

He looked at me the way he does. He was not suspicious. He was patient, like a man who can wait years for an answer. Then he went to check the side passage, bow up, in case anything else was keen.

Lysette stood in front of the construct with half a baton in her hand.

Her hands were shaking. Not much. It was enough that the broken end ticked against her ring. She looked at them as if they belonged to someone she would have to report.

I half expected her to call up her Tenet right there. She could make the passage replay itself in ghost-light, the last half minute, step by step. If she had, she would have seen my lance fold. She would have seen a door. She did not. She only stared at her hands.

"Are you all right?" I asked.

"I'm fine."

"Your hands—"

"I *said* I'm fine."

"I didn't need that," she said.

"I know."

She looked up, sharp, ready to be angry about how I said it. I don't think she found anything. I did not mean it the way people mean it. She had stepped inside the arm. She had hit the right spot. I had only held a door.

The shaking got worse. She moved the broken baton to her other hand and closed the first one tight. That did not help either.

My handkerchief was in her pocket. I knew because a corner showed. A bit of silver stitching against dark wool. I could have pointed at it. That would have been strange. I stood there with nothing in my hands and nothing useful to say.

Then I picked up the half of her baton that had snapped off. I held it out to her, as if she would want it.

She took it. She looked at it. "This is no use to anyone," she said.

"No."

She put it in her pocket anyway. With the handkerchief.

* * *

Okoru came down the passage at a walk. That was somehow worse than a run. Behind him, through the grille, Rowan's whole team had stopped, pressed against the bars. Rowan's gold glow had gone out completely. He was looking at Lysette.

"Hollen?" he called. "Lysette, are you hurt? Did it get you? It looked like it got you, your arm went all—"

"I'm perfectly fine, thank you, Tallis."

"You don't *sound* fine."

"Then you're not listening properly."

She did not look at him when she said it. I noticed that. Clara put a hand on Rowan's sleeve. He closed his mouth. He kept looking.

Okoru looked at the construct. He looked at the blade on the floor. He looked at the low door, still open. Last, and longest, he looked at me. He looked at the lance held low in my left hand. He looked at the scuff marks on the stone where my heels had slid.

Then he crouched by the construct. He put his one hand flat on its chest, beside the cracked crystal, and pressed.

The crystal fell out of its socket in two pieces. Behind it, there should have been the usual tangle of copper wire and charged stone. Instead there was a hollow the size of a fist.

It was full of dust.

It was gray and fine as flour. It was packed so tight that it kept the shape of the hollow even after the crystal was gone. A little of it fell onto the stone floor. In the glowstone light, it looked like nothing. It looked like what you sweep up after a fire.

Okoru did not touch it. He took a folded cloth from his belt with one hand. He laid it over the hollow and tucked in the edges, carefully, the way you cover a face.

"Wren," he said. "You blocked it."

"Yes, sir."

"With a lance."

"It was all I had."

"Mm." He pulled the cloth tight. "Hollen. You hit the crystal. Why the crystal?"

"It was glowing, sir."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

He nodded slowly, as if both answers were fine and neither was true.

"Who," he said, to no one, very quietly, "had the keys to the warden store this week?"

No one answered. At the edge of the cloth, a thin line of dust had already reached the gutter. The old rainwater carried it away downhill, into the parts of the Undercroft that are not on anyone's map.
