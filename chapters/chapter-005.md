# Latch

Felix's arrow went through the painted wooden Husk at forty paces and pinned it to the wall by its third eye, and the chalk-dust from the impact was still drifting when he said, without lowering the bow, "Eaten today?"

"We're in a maze."

"That isn't an answer."

"Half a roll."

"Better." He nocked another arrow. "Left or right?"

The passage split ahead of us under a hanging glowstone, both ways the same: rough-cut stone, wet in the seams, a gutter running down the middle that smelled of old rain. The Undercroft goes on under the whole plateau. Years One to Four learn it in pieces; Year Five is supposed to know it well enough to run it in Lances of three against the clock, with targets to hit and wardens to avoid.

"Left," said Lysette, from behind us, before I could say anything. "Right loops back. I've seen the map."

"There's a map?" said Felix.

"There's always a map. You just have to ask the right clerk."

She'd assigned herself to our three. Committee assignment, initiated by an officer. Felix had looked at me when she announced it, then at her, then at his bow, and said nothing at all, which from Felix is a whole speech.

We went left.

* * *

The drill had started a quarter of an hour earlier at the bottom of the Long Stair, with Okoru reading out the rules in the voice he uses for things he's said two hundred times. Three per team. Hit nine targets. Avoid the drill-wardens, which were old Year Three constructs, bronze-and-stone, slow, about as dangerous as a wardrobe that disliked you. A warden that touched you put you out. No Tenets above Spark. No blades.

"Shouting," Okoru had added, "is permitted."

Somebody had laughed. Nobody had laughed when he looked at them.

* * *

Six targets in, the left passage opened on one side into a long iron grille, waist-high to the ceiling, and through it you could see the next run over, parallel to ours.

Rowan Tallis's team was in it. Him, Clara Belmont and a broad quiet boy from the north towers. Rowan was in front, of course. His Tenet was lit at Spark like everyone else's, and on him even Spark looked like a lamp turned up: a warm gold edge all the way round, as if he'd been cut out of a sunrise and pasted onto the gray. He said something to Clara. She shoved him. He laughed, and the laugh came through the grille and bounced down our passage.

Lysette stopped.

Not long. A second. Long enough to look. Her hand didn't go to her collar, because the chain was in a drawer somewhere in two pieces, but it went up anyway and found nothing and came down again.

I was looking at the far end of our passage. There was a side door there, low, the kind they put in for maintenance. It was open.

It had been shut when I'd checked the map. I'd also asked the right clerk.

* * *

The warden came out of it at a run.

That was the first wrong thing. Drill-wardens don't run; they don't have the joints for it. This one did. It came through the low door bent nearly double and unfolded on the far side, nine feet of stacked stone and bronze bands, a head like an anvil with a single dull crystal set in the chest where a heart would be, and on its right arm, where the drill models had a padded club, it had a blade. Training steel, unsharpened, a yard long. Unsharpened steel swung by that much stone breaks bones the way a dropped stone breaks a plate.

It didn't go for Felix, who was nearest. It didn't go for me.

It went straight down the passage at Lysette, who was looking through a grille at a boy laughing, and who turned her head a half-second late.

* * *

There's a thing that happens when I'm about to have to choose. Everything gets slower and much quieter and I can hear the options lining up, like men at a door.

Felix was drawing. He'd put an arrow in it, and the arrow would do nothing; a blunt shaft against a stone chest.

Lysette had her batons out. Good ones, Golden Seal style. She'd get one block in, maybe two, and the third swing would come through her guard like weather through a window.

I could wake Gorrath. Nine feet of shield-knight out of the shadow behind the grille, in front of Felix and Lysette Hollen and, through the bars, the Dawn Boy's whole team. That would be the end of rank eight, and of a great deal else.

I could put Candor through the crystal. That would be the end of rank eight about as fast.

What I was allowed, in front of witnesses, was a short dark lance I used for blocking.

I went.

* * *

The passage was a bad place to fight something big and a good place to fight something long. Seven feet wide. Glowstones every twenty paces on the left wall, none on the right, so every lit stretch had a hard line of shadow down one side, edge after edge after edge. The thing's blade was a yard of steel on a nine-foot frame. It needed room to swing. The grille gave it none on that side; the wall gave it none on the other.

It had to come straight. Overhead, or a thrust.

It chose the overhead. Lysette got a baton up into it. The baton snapped in half, and the shock went down her arm and put her on one knee, and the construct drew the blade back for a second stroke.

I came in from her left with the lance level, thrusting at its elbow. It saw me. It turned the blade to meet the thrust.

I knocked.

One beat, the way you'd rap a door on your way past. The lance folded in my hands mid-thrust, frame and all, and opened out into a door: tall, narrow, black, a panel of dark wood with an iron ring in its center, standing between the construct and Lysette Hollen. The training blade came down on it.

The door slammed.

That's the only word for the sound. A front door shut by a house in a temper. The blade hit it and the panel closed on the steel the way a door closes on a careless hand, and I turned my wrists, and the flat of the blade slid along the wood and up against the iron ring and stuck there, wedged hard between the ring and the panel.

Latch. My mother used to call it that, when the kitchen door caught on its own lock and she had to shoulder it. I don't know why that was in my head.

The construct pulled. I leaned. It was much heavier than me, and I was standing on wet stone, and my bruised ribs had opinions. For a second it was nothing but weight, its weight going back, mine going forward, the blade grinding in the ring.

"The chest," I said. "The crystal. Now."

I wasn't speaking to Felix.

Lysette came up off her knee. She had one baton left. She didn't hesitate and she didn't swing wild; she stepped once to the left, inside the line of the trapped arm, where it couldn't reach her, and drove the butt of the baton into the crystal with her whole weight behind it, the way you'd drive a nail.

The crystal cracked across.

The construct stopped pulling. It stood there with its blade caught in my door, and a long, dry sound came out of it, like sand poured down a pipe, and then it went down on one knee and stayed there, and its anvil head tipped forward until it rested on the top of the door.

I let the door fold back into a lance. The training blade dropped and rang on the stones.

* * *

Felix had an arrow on the string still. He lowered it slowly.

"That," he said, "was a warden."

"An old one," I said.

"They don't run."

"This one was keen."

He looked at me in the way he has, which is not suspicious, only very patient, like a man who's prepared to wait several years for an answer. Then he went to look down the side passage, bow up, to see if anything else was keen.

Lysette was still standing in front of the construct with half a baton in her hand.

Her hands were shaking. Not much. Enough that the broken end of the baton was making small ticking noises against her ring. She looked down at them as if they belonged to someone she'd have to write up.

I'd half expected her to lift one and call up her Tenet right there, to have the passage play itself back to her in ghost-light, the last half-minute, every step. If she had, she'd have seen my lance fold. She'd have seen a door. She didn't. She just stood and looked at her hands.

"I didn't need that," she said.

"I know."

She glanced up then, sharp, ready to be angry about the way I'd said it. I don't think she found anything to be angry about. I hadn't meant it the way people mean it. She'd stepped inside the arm. She'd hit the right place. I'd just held a door.

The shaking got worse instead of better. She put the broken baton in her other hand and closed the first one hard, and that didn't help either.

My handkerchief was in her pocket. I knew because the corner of it was showing, a little silver stitching against the dark wool. I could have pointed. That would have been strange. I stood there with nothing in my hands and nothing useful to say, and after a moment I picked up the half of her baton that had broken off and held it out to her, as if it were something she'd want.

She took it. She looked at it. "This is no use to anyone," she said.

"No."

She put it in her pocket anyway, in with the handkerchief.

* * *

Okoru came down the passage at a walk, which was somehow worse than a run. Behind him, through the grille, Rowan Tallis's whole team had stopped and were pressed up against the bars. Rowan's gold edge had gone out completely. He was looking at Lysette.

"Hollen?" he called through the bars. "Lysette, are you hurt? Did it get you? It looked like it got you, your arm went all—"

"I'm perfectly well, thank you, Tallis."

"You don't *sound* perfectly well."

"Then you're not listening properly."

She didn't look at him while she said it. I noticed that. Clara put a hand on Rowan's sleeve, and he closed his mouth, and kept looking.

Okoru looked at the construct. At the blade on the floor. At the low door, still open. Last, and longest, he looked at me, at the lance held low in my left hand and the scuff marks on the stone where my heels had slid.

Then he crouched by the construct, put his one hand flat on its chest beside the cracked crystal, and pressed.

The crystal came away from its setting in two pieces. Behind it, where there should have been the usual clockwork of copper wire and charged stone, was a hollow the size of a fist.

It was full of dust.

Gray, fine as flour, packed in so tight it held the shape of the hollow even after the crystal came away. A little of it sifted down onto the flagstones. In the glowstone light it looked like nothing. Like something you'd sweep up after a fire.

Okoru didn't touch it. He took a folded cloth from his belt, one-handed, and laid it over the hollow, and tucked the edges in, carefully, the way you'd cover a face.

"Who," he said, to nobody, very quietly, "had the keys to the warden store this week?"

Nobody answered. At the edge of the cloth, a thin trail of the dust had already found the gutter, and the old rainwater was carrying it away downhill, into the parts of the Undercroft that aren't on anybody's map.
