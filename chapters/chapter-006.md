# Lance Nine

I had picked my Lance three days before anyone told us we would be picked.

Lance Fourteen. That was the plan, if the Ladder numbers worked as they did last year. A Lance is a group of five students who train and work together. Fourteen had three star-readers who spent every break on the observatory steps. They argued about a comet no one else could see. It also had a boy from the river towers who carved spoons.

Together, those four had said maybe forty words in public since summer. A Lance like that gets the back of the line. It gets the cold end of the Wall in winter and the job of counting crates. No one watches a Lance like that. I could have slept through the whole year and come out the other side with a spoon.

Okoru read the lists in the lowchapel. That is the old stone chapel under the hill. He says no one falls asleep there. He is right. It is the oldest room on the hill, a stone box half sunk in the ground, cold in every season. The pews have names cut into the ends. They are not names of people who gave money. They are Year Sixes who went out on the Crossing and never came back from the Teeth.

The newest names were still pale in the cuts. Along the side aisle stood a few jars of lilies from the summer remembrance. No one had cleared them. They had gone the color of tea.

"Lance One," said Okoru. He stood on the step under the bell-rope. Someone had cut the rope off at head height a hundred years ago. He stood under the frayed end as if it had been put there for him. "Aurenmark. Aurenmark."

Seraphine turned in her pew and wiggled her fingers at me. "Good luck, sleepy," she mouthed. Her brother did not turn around. He does not need to. He has a way of not looking at you that you can feel in your shoulder blades.

Lances Two to Eight went by. I did not listen. I watched the star-readers at the back, already whispering to each other.

"Perfect," I whispered, to no one.

"Nine," said Okoru. "Ashgrove."

Felix, beside me, did not move.

"Wren."

That was fine. Nine was a low number.

"Tallis. Belmont."

That was less fine.

A room makes a certain sound when it adds up a sum it likes. The Dawn Boy was rank two. Clara Belmont was rank three. They would be in one Lance with the year's biggest truant and Felix Ashgrove, who is nineteenth and does not care. Someone behind me said, "Oh, *bad* luck." He said it warmly, like a man talking about a funeral he does not have to go to.

"And Hollen," said Okoru.

Lysette stood up. She does not usually stand to speak. She did it now to be taller than the pew in front.

"Instructor, with respect. I'm on the Committee schedule all autumn, and the schedule assumes—"

"Wren has sixty-one absences." He did not look at his slate for the number. "The Committee already gave itself to him in the Undercroft. I watched. Now I'm making it official. You will supervise."

"Supervise," she said.

"Find a better word and I'll use it."

She sat down. Two pews ahead, Rowan Tallis had turned all the way around, with his elbow on the seat back. He grinned at her like a dog who has just heard the walk is still on. She stared at the frayed rope.

"Wonderful," she said, in a voice cold enough to keep butter in.

Felix leaned an inch toward me without turning his head.

"Fourteen?" he murmured.

I had not told him about Fourteen. I had not told anyone.

"Spoons," I said.

"You would have hated the spoons."

* * *

When the lists were done, Okoru said, "Nine stays." The rest of the year walked out past us. Many of them looked at me on the way, which they do not usually do.

I used to be the boy who slept. Now I was the boy who slept in Lance Nine. That is worse, because people like to know where everything is in a room with a prince in it.

Rowan came down the aisle before Okoru could say more. He stepped around the lily jars and stuck out his hand.

"You're the door one," he said.

I looked at his hand. It was as big as a shovel blade. It had a burn scar across the knuckles in the shape of a pan handle.

"The warden," he said. "In the drill. Clara said you were really calm. I said, I bet he's scared inside, he just has one of those faces. Were you scared inside?"

"No."

"See, that's exactly what a scared person would say."

He laughed. It is a big laugh, the kind that never asks if it is allowed. It went up into the roof and came back sounding like three people. Then he dug in his coat and pulled out half a honey-cake wrapped in a page of someone's notes on the Breach.

"Here. I always bring two. I never eat the second one. No idea why I bring it."

"Eaten today?" Felix asked me, very softly.

"He's about to," said Rowan, happy. He pressed the cake into my glove.

It is hard to dislike someone who feeds you and wants nothing back. I had been trying since the market, since I saw his name circled twice in a dead man's ledger. It was easier when he was only a gold edge behind a grille.

Clara had sat down on the end of a side pew to wait, next to a dead lily jar. She is small and brown-haired, and she sits so still that people near her talk less. Her hand rested on the end of the pew, over the names, and she did not seem to know it. Lysette sat as far from her as the pew allowed, which was the width of two people. She wrote in her Committee book as if it were the most interesting book ever made.

"Hollen," Rowan said, turning. "You were brilliant in the drill. You hit it dead center. I saw through the grille. Clara saw too. We both said."

"Thank you, Tallis."

"Were *you* scared?"

"I was busy."

"That's a yes," Rowan told me, in a loud whisper. Lysette's pencil went right through the page.

"Nine," Okoru called. "Corridor. I'll take you one at a time." He jerked his chin at the vestry door, went through it, and left it open.

Clara got up last. "Coming, Wren?" she said, over her shoulder. I said yes and stayed one step behind her. That is how I saw the jar she had been sitting beside. The lilies in it were standing up.

They were not fresh. The edges of the petals were still brown. But the flowers stood straight and white underneath, as if someone had watered them an hour ago instead of three months ago. None of the other jars had changed.

She did not look back. I don't think she knew.

* * *

The corridor between the lowchapel and the vestry is where the academy keeps whatever it has not decided to throw away. Mostly paintings. In the time of the Iron King, someone had gone down it with a pot of white lime paint. He covered a row of wall paintings, one for each old kingdom the king had taken. Later, someone else started scraping the paint off, then gave up a third of the way along.

So it is a long white wall with patches of color showing through. Every few steps there is a face.

Felix and Rowan sat on the stone bench under the windows. Clara took the far end. Lysette stood. I leaned on the wall by the one painting that had been scraped almost clean. It showed a woman in a long red coat. She stood on a hill with her back to us, holding up something that must have been the sun. Her whole outline was gold leaf, going brown at the corners. Under her, the plaster had a word half eaten by damp. LIREN. The last letters were gone.

"Wren," said Lysette.

"Hollen."

"For the record, I didn't ask for this."

"I know."

"You keep saying that."

"You keep telling me things I know."

She opened her mouth, shut it, and wrote something down. I was fairly sure it was my name.

On the bench, Rowan was showing Felix something. "No, look. If I keep it at Spark, I can make it go round in a ring. Like a coin on a table. Clara thinks it's stupid."

"I think it's very stupid," said Clara, without opening her eyes.

"It's a great ring!"

"It's a ring," said Felix, with polite pain.

Rowan held up his hand. His Tenet came on the way it always does, with no fuss. A warm gold line ran along his fingers. Then it lifted off his skin and spun around his palm in a slow circle. Everyone watched, because you do.

I was not watching the ring. The light had reached the wall behind Rowan's shoulder, and the woman's gold edge had caught it.

Gold leaf takes on any light. That is what it is for. Candlelight makes it candle-colored, and glowstone makes it green. This went exactly the color of Rowan's ring. Not close. The same, down to the faint pink at the outer edge, as if the painter had mixed the paint while looking at him.

As long as he held up the ring, the painted woman on the hill glowed with the same light as the boy on the bench. Then he closed his hand, and both lights went out.

"Did you see that?" I almost said. No one else had looked. Lysette was reading her book, and Clara's eyes were still shut.

I put it away in the place where I keep things I cannot use yet. That place is getting full.

"Tallis," Okoru called from inside.

Rowan went in, still talking. I heard Okoru's low voice, then Rowan's, not low at all. About two minutes later he came out saying, "No, that's fair, that's totally fair," and sat back down.

Then Clara went in. Then Felix, who came out exactly as he went in.

Then Okoru said, "Wren." I went.

* * *

The vestry was made for storing robes, though it had not held a robe in fifty years. Okoru had turned it into an office for the Lances. It had a table, two chairs, a brazier, and a ledger bound in green cloth with the warden store's stamp on the corner. The book lay open. He sat on the far side with his pinned sleeve toward the fire.

Next to the ledger lay the cloth he had laid over the warden's chest. Someone had folded it into a neat square, after shaking it out badly. The folds were gray.

"Sit," he said.

"I'm fine standing."

"Then stand. Ask."

"I'd like to move to another Lance."

"Which one?"

"Fourteen needs one more."

"Fourteen has five."

"Fifteen, then."

"Fifteen has five. They all have five, Wren. That is what a Lance is." He did not smile. "Try again."

So I tried the truth. It sometimes works on people who expect a lie. "Nine has too many top ranks. Rank two, three and four. No one can grade that fairly. Put me with people my size and you'll get a better spread."

"Your size," said Okoru.

The door behind me was still open. Out in the corridor, Rowan was telling Felix about a horse he had at the fort, and Lysette's pencil was scratching. The stone carried every word of it into this room, and every word of ours out there.

"I'll shut that," I said.

"Leave it."

He let the quiet sit. Then he spoke, not loud and not quiet. It was the voice he uses to tell a whole drill yard what they did wrong.

"Your lance closed."

Out in the corridor, the horse story kept going. The pencil stopped.

"It's a blocking lance, sir," I said. "It's made to close."

"I've watched blocking lances close for thirty-one years. They clack. They don't slam." He turned the green ledger a quarter turn toward me. "And you hold a shield like a man holding a door shut against the weather. I saw that once before. He was not a student."

"Then it's an accident."

"Probably," said Okoru, who did not think so.

I watched him decide not to push. That was why he had left the door open. He did not want to expose me. He wanted to show me he could, and that he had not. Fine.

I looked down at the ledger, because he clearly wanted me to.

It was the store's list of things given out, one line per item, written in a dozen hands. Bolts, padding, a wheel for the funicular. Halfway down the left page, next to the warden we had broken, it said: *Core, one. Charged. For refit.* Below that was a signature in brown ink, upright and old-fashioned. The last letter had a long tail that ran under the date. *C. Daubert.*

The date was the eleventh day of the sixth month.

"Who's Daubert?" I asked.

Okoru did not answer. He picked up the folded cloth and shook it out with one hand over the brazier. The last gray dust fell into the coals without a sound. Then he folded it again, in halves, then quarters, pinning each fold against his chest with his chin. He must have learned to do everything that way after the arm.

"Clement Daubert taught construct-work here for twenty-six years," he said. "He built that warden. Half the wardens down there, in fact. He liked them slow. He used to say a slow thing teaches better. You have time to feel ashamed of your mistake while it is still coming at you."

He set the cloth on the table, right on top of the signature. He smoothed it once with his palm.

"He died in the spring," said Okoru. "Fourth month. I carried the front of his coffin down the Long Stair."

He gave me the long look from the drill. He did not mention the date, and he did not have to. Fourth month. Sixth month. Out in the corridor, Rowan laughed at something, and the stone carried that in too.
