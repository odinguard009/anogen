# What the Moon Allows

The moon would leave the workshop window in thirty-four minutes.

I had timed it the night before. I lay on my back on a bench in the cloister. My watch was on my chest. I watched a bright square creep across the stones toward the wall. The light came down a shaft that was cut for air. It passed through an iron grate and fell into the west workshops of the Undercroft. It would last thirty-four more minutes. Then it would be gone.

After that, the workshops would be black. I do not mean dark. I mean black. You hold up your hand and you have to believe it is there.

In black, there are no edges. Without edges, I cannot open a door. I learned that in a dye-seller's cellar. I did not want to learn it twice.

"Thirty-three," said Vesk. His voice came from the shadow under my elbow.

"I know."

"I'm just saying it out loud. Some of us like to feel the moment."

* * *

The west workshops were where the Academy kept its constructs between drills. The room was long and low. Workbenches ran down the middle. Racks stood along both walls. In the racks, in neat rows, stood the wardens. There were twenty-two of them. They had bronze bands, stone bodies, and heads like anvils. Each one bent its head down. In the moonlight they looked like monks. Someone had told them to wait, and they had waited a very long time.

Each rack had a slot. Each slot had a small brass plate above it. Someone had cut a name into every plate. They were not numbers. They were names. *Margery. The Bishop. Old Biscuit. Hedwig. Uncle.* One slot was empty. It was the slot of the warden that had gone after Lysette. Its plate said *Tortoise*.

"He named them," said Gorrath.

He came out of the long shadow under the benches. It was the only shadow big enough to hold him. He walked with care so he would not knock anything over. He was nine feet tall and covered in armor. He held his tower shield against his chest like a waiter holding a tray of glasses. He bent down to read the plates, one by one.

"Tortoise," he said again. He sounded sad. "That's a good name for a slow one."

"Then someone made it fast," said Vesk. He was still mostly in my shadow. All I could see was two eyes the color of lamps. His voice sounded like gossip over a fence. "That's rude to the tortoise. Thirty-one."

Mirelle did not come out. She never did, until she had a reason. I could feel her somewhere in the dark under the grate. She was like a held breath with a blade in it.

"Tell the plan again," Vesk said.

"The daybook," I said, and I kept my voice low. "The real one. The green ledger is copied from it. I want the sixth month. I want to see Daubert's name where he first wrote it, not where someone copied it. Then we leave before the moon does."

"And if someone sees us?"

"No one sees us."

That was the one rule Ysolde Lisle had given me, in plain words, in five years. *Never be seen by staff at night.* Students did not matter. Students saw things at night all the time, and no one believed them. Staff wrote things down.

"Twenty-nine," said Vesk. "The daybook is on the foreman's desk at the far end. It's under a cup. Someone left tea in the cup. Someone took three sugars."

I walked down the middle of the room, between the benches. The square of moonlight lay halfway down the floor. The bars of the grate cut it into small squares. It looked like fifty bright boxes with black lines between them.

I was three steps past the lines when Margery lifted her head.

* * *

The sound came first. It sounded like sand running down a pipe. It was the same dry sound I had heard when Tortoise broke. This time it ran the other way. It was filling, not emptying. The warden in the third slot on the left stood up straight. Bronze scraped on stone. It stepped off its rack.

Then the one next to it moved. *Uncle.*

Two wardens. Both were between me and the grate.

"Oh, that's *not* fair," said Vesk.

They moved the way Tortoise had moved. They were fast, and it looked wrong. They bent low. They had no blades this time. They had only long stone arms. They did not go for the door. They went to the middle of the room, into the moonlight. They stood on both sides of the bright squares. Their bodies covered half of them at once.

It was clever. Or someone clever had made them that way. Every square of shadow they covered was an edge I did not have.

"Gorrath, take the left one," I said. "Hold it. Don't break it. Mirelle, the crystal in the right one, when I open. Vesk."

"Yes, yes. I know my job."

He was already gone from my shadow. He slid into the shadow of a bench. Then he slid into the black behind Uncle's heels. He hit the bronze band on its ankle twice with the handle of a knife. It sounded sharp, like a man knocking on a door to ask for money.

Constructs are not smart. Uncle spun toward the sound. It swung one arm. The swing could have broken a man's back. It hit only air, and the weight of the arm pulled Uncle halfway around.

Gorrath had gone left. He did not run. Gorrath never ran. He just arrived. Margery came at him with both arms. He put the bottom of his shield on the stones and let her hit it. The crash rolled down the room and came back, like a bell that fell down a well. The shield did not move an inch.

"Please stop that," he told her politely.

"She can't hear you," Vesk called from the dark.

"It's only good manners."

Uncle was still turning. It hunted the knocking, but the knocking had moved to its other heel. For one second it stood with its back to the grate and its chest to me. It covered the left half of the moonlight. The right half lay open on the floor beside it. I could see the small bright squares and the black bars between them.

The bars were edges. Each one was an inch wide. Each square was as big as a hand.

I had never opened a door that small. Until now, I had never had a reason.

I knelt. I put two fingers on the nearest black bar.

"Too small," said Vesk. "You can't open a door that small."

"Watch me," I said. I knocked.

The door that opened was the size of a book. It was a small black panel in the floor, with a hinge as small as a fingernail. From far below, and yet very close, a long thin blade came up through it. It went straight up into Uncle's chest from underneath. It hit the crystal. It made no sound. That was Mirelle. Most blades make a noise when they go through stone. Hers did not think they needed to.

The blade slid back down. The little door shut. Uncle stood still for a moment. Then it bent at the knees, slowly. It knelt in the moonlight with its head down. It was back in the pose it had kept for years.

Margery hit Gorrath's shield once more. Then she stopped. She looked unsure, like someone who had been given the wrong address.

"May I?" said Gorrath.

"Hold her. Don't dent her."

"I would never."

He leaned the shield in and pushed her gently against her own rack. She ground against it. I slipped around behind her. I opened a second tiny door in the moonlight, by her heel. Mirelle did it again.

* * *

"Twenty-two," said Vesk. "Well done. No one died. No one even yelled."

"Mirelle?" I whispered.

Something tapped twice under the floor. She was fine. She was dusty, probably.

Two wardens knelt in the moonlight with their chests cracked. I held a match in my glove and looked inside. Each hollow was full of gray dust. It was packed down tight and neat.

Gorrath lifted Margery back into her slot. He held her by the hips, like a piece of furniture. Then he straightened her head. He did the same for Uncle.

"There," he said. "Good as new. Nearly."

"Gorrath. They will know someone was here."

"I'll tell them it wasn't us."

"You can't lie."

"I can," he said. He sounded hurt. "I'm just not good at it."

I did not stop him.

"No one will believe two broken wardens walked home alone anyway," I said.

"Then why worry?" said Vesk.

"I don't. Gorrath does."

"I do," said Gorrath.

The daybook was under the cup, where Vesk said. It was a fat book in a canvas cover. It was stained with rings from cups. I turned it in the moonlight to the sixth month, the eleventh day. *Core, one, charged, for refit, Tortoise.* Then I saw the name. It was in brown ink, upright, with the long tail under the date. *C. Daubert.*

"Look at the tail," I said.

"It looks fine to me," said Vesk.

"That's the problem." I turned back a few pages. "Before the fourth month, Daubert was alive. His tail shook where the pen lifted. It was an old man's hook, with a shake in it. The sixth-month name is clean all the way down."

"So someone learned to copy him."

"Someone practiced."

I found the proof under the daybook. A sheet of blotting paper lay on the desk. It was old and dirty. People had used it for months. I tilted it toward the grate. A name was written on it again and again, backward, one on top of another. It was the same name, thirty or forty times. Each one was a little steadier than the one before. Someone had sat here, in a dead man's place. Someone had taught their hand to stop shaking the way his hand had.

"Fourteen," said Vesk. Then he spoke in a different voice. "Someone is in the drawing room."

* * *

The drawing room was at the end of the workshop, up three steps. The foremen used to lay out their plans there. It had its own air shaft and its own grate. The moon was in that one too.

I sent my three friends back. All of them went into my shadow. Gorrath went last and slowest.

"Eden," he whispered. "Is that a bad man?"

"I don't know yet."

"He hums nicely."

"That's what bothers me."


"Stay quiet," I told him.

"I am always quiet," he whispered. It was not true.

I walked up the three steps. I walked like a boy who came down to the workshops at night for a stupid reason. That was the best story I had. It was the only one that would still work if someone looked at it closely.

A man sat at the drafting table. He had a pile of papers and a red pencil. He had no lamp. Moonlight came down the shaft and fell on the table in a square. He had put the papers inside the square. He sat just outside it, in the dark. His hands were lit. His face was not.

He was humming.

I knew the tune before I knew that I knew it. Four notes went up. The fifth did not come. It hung there. Then the four notes came again, lower. That was all of it. It had no words. My mother said it did not need words. Words would only get in the way of the part where you fall asleep. She made it up on the night I was born, she said, as she sat in a window. She said it was the only thing she ever made that was no use to anyone.

I stopped on the top step. My hand was on the rail. It stayed there.

I do not freeze. I had not frozen since I was ten years old. I had stood in front of Veils with knives in my coat and counted their breaths. I had stood on a roof with a crying girl and known what to do with my hands.

Now I stood on the top step. I could not make my hand leave the rail.

The humming stopped. The man looked up. He leaned into the square of moonlight. It was Vice-headmaster Strent. He had gray hair and half-moon glasses. His cardigan was buttoned wrong. It was a face that everyone on the plateau liked at first sight. I had liked it too, until four seconds ago.

"Oh dear," he said. "Oh, I'm so sorry. Did I scare you? I come down here to grade papers. Lamps give me terrible headaches, you see. Clement never minded. This was his room." He set the red pencil down next to the papers, very neatly. "It's an old habit. I suppose I should stop."

"Sir," I said. It came out calm. I want that noted.

"It's late for you to be down here."

"I couldn't sleep."

"No," he said kindly. "No, I don't suppose you could." He looked at me over his glasses. His hands lay folded on someone's essay. He took his time. "Wren, isn't it? Year Five. Thornmere name."

The moon was sliding off the edge of the table. I could see it go. It moved a quarter of an inch.

"Your mother must have sung that to you too," said Strent.

I had never told anyone that she sang.
