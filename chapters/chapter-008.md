# What the Moon Allows

The moon would be off the workshop grate in thirty-four minutes.

I'd timed it the night before. I lay on my back on a cloister bench with my watch on my chest and watched the bright square on the flagstones creep toward the wall. Thirty-four minutes of moonlight came down a shaft cut for air, through an iron grate, into the Undercroft's west workshops. Then nothing.

After that, the workshops would be what they are at night. Black. Not dark. Black, the kind where you hold up your hand and have to take its word that it's there.

In black there are no edges. Without edges, I can't open a door. I learned that in a dye-seller's cellar, and I'd rather not learn it twice.

"Thirty-three," said Vesk, from the shadow of my own elbow.

"I know."

"Just saying it out loud. Some of us like a sense of occasion."

* * *

The west workshops are where the academy keeps its constructs between drills. It's a long, low vault with workbenches down the middle and racks along both walls. In the racks, in rows with their heads bowed, stood the wardens. Twenty-two of them. Bronze bands, stone bodies, anvil heads. In the moonlight they looked like monks who'd been told to wait and had been waiting a very long time.

Each rack had a slot. Each slot had a little brass plate above it, and somebody had engraved names on the plates. Not numbers. Names. *Margery. The Bishop. Old Biscuit. Hedwig. Uncle.* The slot for the warden that went after Lysette was empty. Its plate said *Tortoise*.

"He named them," said Gorrath.

He'd come out of the long shadow under the benches, the only shadow big enough to hold him. He stood very carefully so he wouldn't knock anything over. He's nine feet of armor, and he held his tower shield against his chest the way other people hold a tray of glasses. He bent to read the plates one by one.

"Tortoise," he said again, sadly. "That's a good name for a slow one."

"Then somebody made it fast," said Vesk. He was still mostly in my shadow, just a pair of lamp-colored eyes and a voice like gossip over a fence. "Which is rude to the tortoise. Thirty-one."

Mirelle hadn't come out. She doesn't, until there's a reason. I could feel her somewhere in the dark under the grate, like a held breath with a blade in it.

"Plan again?" Vesk asked.

"Daybook. The real one, the one the green ledger gets copied from." I kept my voice low. "I want the sixth month. I want Daubert's signature where it was first written, not copied. Then we leave before the moon does."

"And if someone sees us?"

"Nobody sees us."

That was the one promise I'd made to Ysolde Lisle in five years that she'd asked for in actual words. *Never be seen by staff at night.* Not students. Students see things at night all the time and nobody believes them. Staff write things down.

"Twenty-nine," said Vesk. "The daybook's on the foreman's desk at the far end. Under a cup. Somebody left tea in it. Somebody," he added, "takes three sugars."

I went down the middle of the vault between the benches. The square of moonlight lay halfway down the floor, cut into a lattice by the grate bars. It was fifty small bright squares with a net of hard black lines between them.

I was three steps past the net when Margery lifted her head.

* * *

The sound came first. Sand down a pipe. It was the same dry pouring I'd heard from Tortoise when the crystal broke, only running the other way, filling instead of emptying. The warden in the third slot on the left straightened out of its bow with a grind of bronze on stone and stepped off its rack.

Then the one beside it. *Uncle.*

Two. Both between me and the grate.

"Oh, that's *not* fair," said Vesk.

They came the way Tortoise had, fast and wrong, bent low. No blades this time, only long stone arms. They didn't go for the door. They went to the middle of the vault, into the moonlight, and stood on either side of the lattice. Their bodies blotted out half the squares at once.

Clever. Or made by someone clever. Every square of shadow they covered was an edge I didn't have.

"Gorrath, left one," I said. "Hold, don't break. Mirelle, the crystal in the right one, when I open. Vesk."

"Yes, yes, I know my job."

He was already gone from my shadow. He slid into the shadow of a bench, then into the black behind Uncle's heels. From there he rapped a knife-hilt twice on the bronze band around its ankle, sharp, like a creditor at a door.

Constructs aren't bright. Uncle spun toward the noise with a swing that would have broken a man's back. It hit only air, and its own weight carried it halfway around.

Gorrath had gone left. He didn't run. Gorrath never runs. He arrives. Margery came at him with both arms, and he planted the bottom of his tower shield on the flagstones and let her hit it. The crash went down the vault and back, like a bell dropped down a well. The shield didn't move an inch.

"Please stop that," he told her, politely.

"She can't hear you," Vesk called from the dark.

"It's only manners."

Uncle was still turning, hunting the knocking, which had moved to its other heel. For one second it stood with its back to the grate and its chest to me. It covered the left half of the moonlight. The right half lay open on the floor beside it, all those small bright squares and the black bars between them.

The bars were edges. Each was an inch wide. Each square was a hand across.

I'd never opened a door that small. There had never been a reason to.

I knelt, put two fingers on the nearest bar of shadow, and knocked.

The door that opened was the size of a book. It was a little black panel in the floor with a hinge no bigger than a fingernail. Through it, from somewhere very far down and very close at once, came one long thin blade. It went straight up through Uncle's chest from underneath, right into the crystal. It made no sound. That's Mirelle. Most blades make some noise going through stone. Hers don't think they need to.

The blade slid back down. The little door shut. Uncle stood still for a moment. Then it folded slowly at the knees until it knelt in the moonlight with its head bowed, back in the pose it had held for years.

Margery hit Gorrath's shield once more and stopped, unsure, like someone given the wrong address.

"May I?" said Gorrath.

"Hold her. Don't dent her."

"I would never."

He leaned the shield in and pinned her against her own rack, gently. She ground against it. I slipped around behind, opened a second tiny door in the moonlight by her heel, and Mirelle did it again.

* * *

"Twenty-two," said Vesk. "Nicely done. Nobody died. Nobody even yelled."

"Mirelle?" I whispered.

Something tapped twice under the floor. Fine. Dusty, probably.

Two wardens knelt in the lattice of moonlight with their chests cracked. I cupped a match in my glove and looked inside. The hollows were full of gray dust, packed down neat.

Gorrath lifted Margery back into her slot, carefully, by the hips, like furniture. Then he straightened her head and did the same for Uncle.

"There," he said. "Good as new. Nearly."

"Gorrath. They'll know someone was here."

"I'll tell them it wasn't us."

"You can't lie."

"I can," he said, wounded. "I'm just not good at it."

I didn't stop him. Nobody was going to believe two broken wardens had walked home alone anyway.

The daybook was under the cup, right where Vesk said. It's a fat thing in a canvas cover, stained in rings. I turned it in the moonlight to the sixth month, the eleventh day. *Core, one, charged, for refit, Tortoise.* And the signature. Brown ink, upright, the long tail under the date. *C. Daubert.*

"Look at the tail," I said.

"Looks fine to me," said Vesk.

"That's the problem." I flipped back a few pages. "Before the fourth month, when Daubert was alive, his tail wobbled where the pen lifted. An old man's hook, with a tremor in it. The sixth-month one is clean all the way down."

"So somebody learned to copy him."

"Somebody practiced."

I found the proof under the daybook. A sheet of blotting paper lay on the desk, old and dirty and used for months. I tilted it toward the grate. Mirror-written in the blot, over and over, overlapping, was one name. Thirty or forty times. Each was a little steadier than the one before. Someone had sat here, in a dead man's place, and trained their hand to stop shaking the way his had.

"Fourteen," said Vesk. Then, in a different voice: "Somebody's in the drawing room."

* * *

The drawing room is at the end of the vault, up three steps, where the workshop foremen used to lay out plans. It has its own air shaft and its own grate. The moon was in that one too.

I sent them back. All three, into my shadow. Gorrath went last and slowest. He looked at me as he went, with a face that on anyone smaller I'd have called worried.

"Stay quiet," I told him.

"I am always quiet," he whispered. It wasn't true.

Then I walked up the three steps like a boy who'd come down to the workshops at night for some stupid reason. That was the best story I had, and the only one that would survive being looked at.

A man sat at the drafting table. He had a stack of papers in front of him and a red pencil. He had no lamp. The moonlight came down the shaft onto the table in a square, and he'd set the papers inside the square and himself just outside it, in the dark. His hands were lit. His face wasn't.

He was humming.

I knew the tune before I knew I knew it. Four notes going up, the fifth one not coming, hanging there. Then the four again, lower. That's all it is. It has no words. My mother said it didn't need any, because words would only get in the way of the part where you fall asleep. She made it up the night I was born, she said, sitting in a window. She said it was the only thing she'd ever made that was no use to anybody.

I stopped on the top step. My hand was on the rail. It stayed there.

I don't freeze. I haven't frozen since I was ten. I've stood in front of Veils with knives in my coat and counted their breaths. I've stood on a roof with a crying girl and known what to do with my hands.

I stood on the top step and couldn't make my hand leave the rail.

The humming stopped. The man looked up and leaned into the square of moonlight. It was Vice-headmaster Strent. Gray hair, half-moon spectacles, cardigan buttoned wrong. It's a face everybody on the plateau likes on sight. I'd liked it too, until about four seconds ago.

"Oh dear," he said. "Oh, I'm so sorry. Did I frighten you? I come down here to grade. Lamps give me terrible headaches, you see, and Clement never minded. This was his room." He set the red pencil down very neatly beside the papers. "An old habit. I suppose I should stop."

"Sir," I said. It came out level. I'd like that noted.

"It's late for you to be down here."

"I couldn't sleep."

"No," he said kindly. "No, I don't suppose you could." He looked at me over the half-moons, his hands folded on someone's essay, and took his time. "Wren, isn't it? Year Five. Thornmere name."

The moon was sliding off the edge of the table. I could see it go. A quarter of an inch.

"Your mother must have sung that to you too," said Strent.

I had never told anyone that she sang.
