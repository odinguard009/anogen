# What the Moon Allows

The moon would be off the workshop grate in thirty-four minutes.

I'd timed it the night before from the cloister, lying on my back on a bench with my watch on my chest, watching the bright square it threw on the flagstones slide toward the wall. Thirty-four minutes of moonlight down a shaft cut for air, falling through an iron grate into the Undercroft's west workshops, and then nothing. After that the workshops would be what they are at night without it: black. Not dark. Black, the kind where you hold up your hand and have to take its word for being there.

In black there are no edges. Without edges I can't open a door. I learned that in a dye-seller's cellar and I'd rather not learn it again.

"Thirty-three," said Vesk, from the shadow of my own elbow.

"I know."

"Just saying it aloud. Some of us like a sense of occasion."

* * *

The west workshops are where the academy keeps its constructs between drills. A long vault, low, with workbenches down the middle and racks along both walls, and in the racks, standing in rows with their heads bowed, the wardens. Twenty-two of them. Bronze bands, stone bodies, anvil heads. In the moonlight they looked like monks who'd been told to wait and had been waiting a very long time.

Each rack had a slot, and each slot had a little brass plate screwed above it, and someone had engraved names on the plates. Not numbers. Names. *Margery. The Bishop. Old Biscuit. Hedwig. Uncle.* The slot for the warden that had come for Lysette was empty, and its plate said *Tortoise*.

"He named them," said Gorrath.

He'd come out of the long shadow under the benches, which was the only shadow in the room big enough to hold him, and he was standing very carefully so as not to knock anything over, nine feet of armor holding a tower shield against his chest the way other people hold a tray of glasses. He read the plates one by one, bending to do it.

"Tortoise," he said again, sadly. "That's a good name for a slow one."

"Somebody made it fast," said Vesk. He was in my shadow still, mostly, a pair of lamp-colored eyes and a voice like gossip over a fence. "Which is rude to the tortoise. Thirty-one."

Mirelle hadn't come out. She doesn't, until there's a reason. I could feel her somewhere in the dark under the grate. A held breath, with a blade in it.

The plan was simple, which is how I like plans before they meet anything. Find the store's issue book, the real one, the daybook the green ledger gets copied from. Look at the sixth month. Look at Daubert's signature in the place where it was first written, not copied. Leave before the moon did.

I had made exactly one promise to Ysolde Lisle in five years that she'd asked for in words, and it was this: *Never be seen by staff at night.* Not students. Students see things at night all the time and nobody believes them. Staff write things down.

"Twenty-nine," said Vesk. "Daybook's on the foreman's desk at the far end. Under a cup. Somebody left tea in it. Somebody," he added, "takes three sugars, from the ring."

I went down the middle of the vault, between the benches. The square of moonlight was on the floor halfway down, cut into a lattice by the grate's bars: forty or fifty small bright squares, and between them a net of hard black lines.

I was three steps past the net when Margery lifted her head.

* * *

It was the sound first. Sand down a pipe. The same dry pouring I'd heard come out of Tortoise when the crystal broke, only running the other way, filling instead of emptying, and the warden in the third slot on the left straightened out of its bow with a grinding of bronze on stone and stepped down off its rack.

Then the one beside it. *Uncle.*

Two. Both between me and the grate.

"Oh, that's *not* fair," said Vesk.

They came the way Tortoise had come, fast and wrong, bent low, no blades this time, only the long stone arms. They didn't make for the door. They made for the middle of the vault, for the moonlight, and stood in it, one on either side of the lattice, so that their bodies blotted out half the squares at once.

Clever. Or made by someone clever. Every square of shadow they covered was an edge I didn't have.

"Gorrath," I said. "Left one. Hold, don't break. Mirelle, crystal, right one, when I open. Vesk."

"Yes, yes, I know my job." He was already gone out of my shadow, into the shadow of a bench, and then into the black at Uncle's heels, and from there he rapped a knife-hilt twice on the bronze band round its ankle, smartly, like a creditor. Constructs aren't bright. Uncle swung round at the noise with a blow that would have broken a man's back, and hit air, and its own weight carried it half round.

Gorrath had gone left. He didn't run. Gorrath never runs; he arrives. Margery came at him with both arms and he set the bottom edge of the tower shield on the flagstones and let her hit it, and the sound went down the vault and back like a bell dropped down a well, and the shield didn't move an inch.

"Please stop that," he told her, politely.

Uncle was still turning, hunting the knocking, which had moved to its other heel. For a second it stood with its back to the grate and its chest to me and its body covering the left half of the moonlight, and the right half lay open on the floor beside it, all those small bright squares and the black bars between them.

The bars were the edges. Each one an inch wide. Each square a hand across.

I'd never opened a door that small. There'd never been a reason to.

I knelt and put two fingers on the nearest bar of shadow, and knocked.

The door that opened was the size of a book. A little black panel in the floor, a hinge no bigger than a fingernail. Through it, from somewhere very far down and very close at once, came one long thin blade, straight up, and through Uncle's chest from underneath, exactly through the crystal. It went in without a sound. That's Mirelle. Most blades make some noise going through stone. Hers don't seem to think they need to.

The blade went back down through the book-sized door, and the door shut, and Uncle stood still, and then folded slowly at the knees until it was kneeling in the moonlight with its head bowed, back in the shape it had stood in for years.

Margery hit Gorrath's shield once more and stopped, uncertain, as if she'd been told the wrong address.

"May I?" said Gorrath.

"Hold her."

He held her. He leaned the shield in and pinned her against her own rack, gently, and she ground against it, and I went round behind and opened a second little door in the moonlight by her heel, and Mirelle did the same thing again.

* * *

"Twenty-two," said Vesk.

Two wardens knelt in the lattice of moonlight, cracked through the chest. Their hollows, when I looked with a match cupped in my glove, were full of gray dust. Packed. Neat.

Gorrath put Margery back in her slot. He didn't have to; nobody would believe two broken wardens had walked back into their racks; I didn't stop him. He lifted her like furniture, carefully, by the hips, and stood her up under her plate and straightened her head. Then he did the same for Uncle.

"There," he said.

"They'll know someone was here," I said.

"I'll tell them it wasn't us."

"Gorrath, you can't lie."

"I can," he said, wounded. "I'm just not good at it."

The daybook was under the cup, where Vesk had said. It's a fat thing in a canvas cover, stained in rings. I turned it in the moonlight to the sixth month, the eleventh day. *Core, one, charged, for refit, Tortoise.* And the signature. Brown ink, upright, the long tail under the date. *C. Daubert.*

It was a very good signature. That was the trouble with it. The tail was too good. Everywhere else in the book, before the fourth month, where Daubert had actually been alive to sign, his tail wobbled at the end where the pen lifted, an old man's tail, a little tremor in the hook. The sixth-month one was clean all the way down.

Under the daybook, on the foreman's desk, was a sheet of blotting paper. Old, dirty, used for months. I tilted it to the grate.

Mirror-written in the blot, again and again, overlapping, there was a name. Practice. Thirty or forty times. Each one a little steadier than the last, as if someone had sat here, in the place of a dead man, and taught their hand to stop shaking the way his had.

"Fourteen," said Vesk, and then, in a different voice: "Somebody's in the drawing room."

* * *

The drawing room is at the end of the vault, up three steps, where the workshop foremen used to lay out plans. It has its own air shaft, its own grate. The moon was in that one too.

I sent them back. All three, into my shadow, and Gorrath went last and slowest and looked at me as he went with an expression that, on anyone smaller, I'd have called worried.

Then I walked up the three steps like a boy who'd come down to the workshops at night for some stupid reason, because that was the best story I had, and the only one that would survive being looked at.

There was a man at the drafting table. He had a stack of papers in front of him and a red pencil and no lamp. The moonlight came down the shaft onto the table in a square, and he'd set the papers in the square and himself just outside it, in the dark at its edge, so that his hands were lit and his face wasn't.

He was humming.

I knew the tune before I knew I knew it. Four notes going up and the fifth one not coming, hanging there, and then the four again, lower. That's all it is. It doesn't have words. My mother said it didn't need any, that words would only get in the way of the bit where you fall asleep. She made it up when I was born, she said, sitting in a window. She said it was the only thing she'd ever made that wasn't any use to anybody.

I stopped on the top step. My hand was on the rail. It stayed there.

I don't freeze. I haven't frozen since I was ten. I've stood in front of Veils with knives in my coat and counted their breaths; I've stood on a roof with a crying girl and known what to do with my hands.

I stood on the top step and couldn't move my hand off the rail.

The humming stopped. The man looked up, and leaned forward into the square of moonlight, and it was Vice-headmaster Strent: gray hair, half-moon spectacles, cardigan buttoned wrong, a face everybody on the plateau likes on sight, and I'd liked it too, until about four seconds ago.

"Oh dear," he said. "Oh, I'm so sorry. Did I frighten you? I come down here to grade. Lamps give me terrible headaches, you see, and Clement never minded. This was his room." He set the red pencil down very neatly beside the papers. "Old habit. I suppose I should stop."

"Sir," I said. It came out level. I'd like that noted.

"It's late for you to be down here."

"I couldn't sleep."

"No," he said kindly. "No, I don't suppose you could." He looked at me over the half-moons for a long moment, in the moonlight, with his hands folded on someone's essay. "Wren, isn't it? Year Five. Thornmere name."

The moon was sliding off the edge of the table. I could see it go. A quarter of an inch.

"Your mother must have sung that to you too," said Strent.

I had never told anyone that she sang.
