# Not Technically a Veil

"Last car down, son. You riding it or writing it a poem?"

The funicular man had a pipe he never lit and a lantern he did. He held the lantern up to my face like he expected to know it. He didn't. Nobody does. That's what the face is for.

"Riding," I said.

"Curfew's tenth bell."

"I have a pass."

"Course you do." He took it, glanced at it upside down, and gave it back. "They all have passes. The ones without passes take the Long Stair and break their necks. Get in."

The car is a wooden box on iron wheels, with benches on two sides and windows that rattle. It rides down the cliff below Hallowcrest on a cable of braided vein-stone as thick as a man's arm. If you sit on the left, you watch Solmarch rise up to meet you. The river. The palace on its hill across the water. The lower city in a long smear of lights.

Tonight there was a smudge in the smear. In one part of Lantern Ward, the red lanterns had gone out in a ring, as if someone had pressed a thumb on them.

I sat on the left. I watched that ring the whole way down.

* * *

The monthly debrief is the one meeting Ysolde holds at the palace instead of the bookbinder's. The King likes to drop in, and he can't drop into a bookbinder's. You enter by the Gardeners' Gate on the river side and climb a stair that smells of potting soil. Nobody stops you. A clerk at the bottom has been told a student comes on the first of the month to collect a book.

The quick way from the funicular to the Gardeners' Gate goes through Lantern Ward. Past Quillwright Lane. Past the market. Past the stall where an old woman sells candied pears.

The stall was shut. The shutters were down and padlocked, with a Golden Seal notice pasted across them in that special yellow they use. *Closed pending inquiry.* Her cat sat on top of the shutters with its paws tucked under, staring at the notice as if it might read it if it waited long enough.

I stopped. I don't usually.

A revenue clerk had bought one pear from her every night for three weeks. Twelve days ago he vanished from the face of the world, in a dye-seller's cellar, because of me. A missing clerk is exactly the kind of thing the Golden Seal is good at. They ask who saw him last. They ask who saw him every night.

Two streets farther on, I found out where the lanterns had gone.

* * *

There was a chandlery on the corner of Wick Street, an old shop with a bow window and a sign shaped like a candle. The Seal had taken it over as a post. Somebody had hung a yellow pennant on the sign. Somebody else had pulled it down and thrown it in the gutter.

The crowd filled the street wall to wall. Lantern Ward people: dyers, porters, the fried-dough girl from the market, families from the south duchies who came north in the bad years and stayed. It wasn't a mob yet. It was a crowd that hadn't decided. They'd taken the lanterns off their own doors and held them, unlit. I didn't get why until I smelled it. Lamp oil, raw. Every lantern was full.

Somebody at the front kept shouting a name I didn't know. Then the pear woman's name, which I did.

"She's seventy-one!" he yelled. "She's *seventy-one!*"

Through the bow window, by the light of one candle, I could see her. She sat on a stool in the middle of the shop, small and upright, with her hands in her lap. Behind her stood two Seal juniors in yellow armbands, barely older than me. One had his hand on his baton. The other had a hand on the first one's arm.

The door was shut. A chandlery has a good door. Oak, iron-studded, made to stop thieves who know what lamp oil sells for.

A broad man near the front, with a porter's strap across his chest, lit his lantern.

It caught all at once, because he'd slopped oil down the outside of the glass. The crowd made a noise, half pulling back and half leaning in. He didn't hold it like a light. He held it by the bail at arm's length and swung it a little. He was measuring the distance to the door.

Thirty feet. Then the door burns, and the frame burns, and a shop full of tallow and wicks and casks goes up like a struck match. With an old woman on a stool in the middle.

I worked it out in the time it takes to say it. Seventy-one. Two boys with batons. One candle.

I could open a door in the porter's shadow and drop him into whatever's under Wick Street. But twenty people would watch a man fall through the ground, and by morning every Seal investigator in Solmarch would be on Wick Street with a measuring tape. I could put Candor through the lantern from the dark. Twenty people would see a white light, and Ysolde would see a report.

What I was allowed was the short dark lance a scholarship student is known to carry. And my hood.

I put the hood up and walked into the crowd.

It's easier than it sounds. People let you through if you walk like you've been sent. I came out at the front, between the porter and the door, with my back to the oak and my hands empty. I turned around and looked at him.

He was bigger up close. He had sweat in his eyebrows. Not a bad face. It was a face that had been shouting for an hour and was scared of what it would do next.

"Move," he said.

"She's in there."

"*I know she's in there!* That's why." His voice cracked. "They'll hang her for that clerk. They hang us for anything."

"She sold him pears," I said.

It wasn't the answer he wanted. It wasn't really an answer. He drew his arm back.

I knocked.

The lance came into my left hand out of nothing. That's ordinary. Half the Year Fives at Hallowcrest can call a weapon. Then it folded, and the door it folded into stood up in front of me, black and narrow, ring outward. The lantern hit it dead center and burst.

Burning oil ran down the panel like rain down a window. The heat came through the wood onto my hands. Fire spilled to the cobbles at the foot of the door and lay there in a puddle. It went nowhere else. The door was between it and the chandlery, and the door didn't care.

Somebody screamed. Somebody always does.

The next thing to come was a cobblestone. I didn't see who threw it. I saw it over the top of the door for an instant, turning. I could have stepped aside. But stepping meant the bow window, and the candle, and the old woman's face.

I didn't step.

It hit above my left eye. There was a white flash and a noise like a dropped plank. Then I was still standing, which I'd decided beforehand. Something warm ran into my eyebrow.

The crowd went quiet. It's a particular quiet. I've heard it on other streets, and it never lasts long.

The porter stared at the burning puddle. He'd let go of the bail. His hands hung open.

At the far end of the street, whistles started. Seal reinforcements, a whole squad by the sound, running up from the river. The crowd heard them too. It began to come apart at the edges as people remembered they had somewhere else to be.

I folded the door back into a lance, and the lance back into nothing. I kept my head down and went with them.

Behind me, through the bow window, the old woman had turned on her stool to watch me go. Her cat sat on the sill outside, between the glass and the flames, washing one paw.

* * *

"Is that blood?" said Ysolde.

"Some of it."

"Whose?"

"Mine. A man with a cobblestone."

"I didn't ask what he was holding."

She studied the cut. Then she took a clean handkerchief from her desk drawer, folded it into a pad, and handed it over without a word. That's the nearest she comes to fussing. Her office is high in the east wing, narrow and tidy, with three locked cabinets and a fourth that isn't locked because it only holds forms.

"Sit. Wick Street?"

"You heard."

"I hear everything that happens to the Golden Seal. They make sure of it." She sat and uncapped a pen. "The pear woman was released a quarter of an hour ago. No evidence. Somebody upstairs had words." The pen didn't move. "Somebody hooded stood in front of a Seal post with a door-shield and took a stone for it. Description: young, hooded, door-shield. There are maybe two hundred young men in Solmarch who carry a blocking lance."

"Then it's fine."

"Is it." That wasn't a question. She pulled a form from the fourth cabinet. "Moving on. Form 13-B. Your drill-warden."

"It wasn't a Veil."

"It was a hostile construct with Hush residue packed in its core. The form covers any hostile entity with Hush residue. Section four asks whether it was a Veil." She turned it to face me. Section four had two boxes. *Yes. No.* "You'll tick no. Then section four-a asks you to explain why you're filing a Veil form for something that isn't one."

"Because you asked me to."

"That isn't one of the options."

"Then the form's wrong."

"The form," said Ysolde, "has been wrong since the reign of Gerulf. It's still the form."

The door opened without a knock. Ysolde stood. I stood a second after her, still holding the handkerchief to my eyebrow.

King Theodric IV is tall and stoops a little in doorways, whether they need it or not. He was in shirtsleeves, with ink on one cuff and reading glasses pushed up into his gray hair. He looked like what he'd been doing, which was reading something long and dull in a chair by a fire. The sword at his hip was plain gray and plainly old. I've never seen him without it.

"Sit, sit," he said. Then he saw my face. "What happened to you?"

"A cobblestone, Your Majesty."

"I can see it was a cobblestone." He looked at Ysolde. Ysolde looked at her form. He looked back at me, at the handkerchief and the cut. He looked for so long that I started to wonder what he saw. His face changed in a way I don't have a name for. Then it was gone, and he asked, quite gently, "Are you eating?"

"Yes, sir."

"Properly?"

"There's a boy in my Lance who keeps giving me his eggs."

"Good," said the King of Verandel, seriously. "Let him." He touched my shoulder on his way past, once, briefly, as if checking it was there. To Ysolde he said, "Don't keep him long. He's bleeding on your forms." Then he left.

Ysolde sat down. So did I.

"Strent," she said.

My hand stayed on the handkerchief. "What about him?"

"The vice-headmaster filed a note with the bursar this morning. *Student out of bed, west workshops, after midnight. No harm done, lenient view taken.* Your name." She set the note on the desk between us. Small, neat handwriting. A tiny owl sketched in the margin, the way some teachers do to show they aren't angry. "Two of Daubert's wardens were found this morning back in their racks with their chests cracked. I'd like a report on Strent."

"There's nothing to report."

"You were seen."

"By a man who grades essays in the dark because lamps hurt his head."

"Eden."

"He was kind," I said. "He sent me to bed."

All of that was true. I could hear the humming while I said it. Four notes up, the fifth one not coming. I didn't tell her about that, or about the top step, or my hand on the rail. I don't know why. That's not true. I do know. I'm the Black Order. I don't freeze on stairs.

She looked at me for a long time. Then she opened the third cabinet, the locked one. She took something out and set it on the desk, and kept her hand flat on top of it.

It was an envelope. Old. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, and the wax seal was pale gray, one I didn't recognize. She didn't turn it over.

"What's that?" I asked.

"Orders you haven't earned." She slid it back in the cabinet and locked it. "Not tonight."

"Because I didn't write a report."

"Because you lied to me about a teacher, and you're not good at it, and you think you are." She took a fresh sheet, wrote on it, quick and square, and folded it in three. "New work. An Ember cell in the lower city, the tannery streets. Four, maybe five of them. I think they're where the clerk's ledger was going."

I took it.

"And Eden." She didn't let go of the paper right away. "Watch the Tallis boy."
