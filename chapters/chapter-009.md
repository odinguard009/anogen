# Not Technically a Veil

"Last car down, son. Are you riding it or writing it a poem?"

The funicular man had a pipe that he never lit and a lantern that he did. He held the lantern up to my face. He seemed to expect to know me. He did not. No one ever does. That is what my face is for.

"Riding," I said.

"Curfew is the tenth bell."

"I have a pass."

"Of course you do." He took it. He looked at it upside down and gave it back. "They all have passes. The ones without passes take the Long Stair and break their necks. Get in."

The car was a wooden box on iron wheels. It had benches on two sides and windows that rattled. It rode down the cliff below Hallowcrest on a thick cable. The cable was as wide as a man's arm. If you sat on the left, you watched the city of Solmarch rise up to meet you. You saw the river. You saw the palace on its hill across the water. You saw the lower city, a long smear of lights.

Tonight the smear had a dark spot in it. In one part of Lantern Ward, the red lanterns had gone out in a ring. It looked like someone had pressed a thumb on them.

I sat on the left. I watched that ring all the way down.

* * *

Once a month, I met Ysolde at the palace. It was the only meeting she held there and not at the bookbinder's shop. The King liked to drop in, and he could not drop in at a bookbinder's. You went in by the Gardeners' Gate on the river side. You climbed a stair that smelled of dirt and plants. No one stopped you. A clerk at the bottom had been told that a student came on the first of each month to pick up a book.

The fast way from the funicular to the Gardeners' Gate went through Lantern Ward. I passed Quillwright Lane. I passed the market. I passed the stall where an old woman sold candied pears.

The stall was shut. The shutters were down, with a padlock on them. A Golden Seal notice was pasted across them, in the special yellow paper they used. *Closed pending inquiry.* The woman's cat sat on top of the shutters with its paws tucked under. It stared at the notice, as if it would read it if it waited long enough.

I stopped. I did not usually stop.

A revenue clerk had bought one pear from her every night for three weeks. Twelve days ago, he had disappeared. It happened in a dye-seller's cellar, and it was because of me. The Golden Seal was good at missing clerks. They asked who saw him last. They asked who saw him every night.

Two streets farther on, I found out where the lanterns had gone.

* * *

There was a chandlery on the corner of Wick Street. It was an old shop with a bow window and a sign shaped like a candle. The Seal had taken it over as a post. Someone had hung a yellow flag on the sign. Someone else had pulled it down and thrown it in the gutter.

A crowd filled the street from wall to wall. They were Lantern Ward people. There were dyers and porters. There was the fried-dough girl from the market. There were families from the south duchies. They had come north in the bad years and stayed. It was not a mob yet. It was a crowd that had not decided. They had taken the lanterns off their own doors, and they held them unlit. I did not know why until I smelled it. It was raw lamp oil. Every lantern was full.

A man at the front kept shouting a name I did not know. Then he shouted the pear woman's name. I knew that one.

"She's seventy-one!" he yelled. "She's *seventy-one!*"

I could see her through the bow window, by the light of one candle. She sat on a stool in the middle of the shop. She was small and straight, with her hands in her lap. Behind her stood two Seal juniors in yellow armbands. They were barely older than me. One had his hand on his baton. The other had a hand on the first one's arm.

The door was shut. A chandlery has a good door. It was oak with iron studs. It was made to stop thieves who knew what lamp oil sold for.

A broad man near the front wore a porter's strap across his chest. He lit his lantern.

It caught fire all at once. He had spilled oil down the outside of the glass. The crowd made a noise. Some people pulled back and some leaned in. The man did not hold the lantern like a light. He held it out at arm's length by the handle and swung it a little. He was measuring the distance to the door.

It was thirty feet. Then the door would burn. Then the frame would burn. Then a shop full of candle fat and wicks and barrels would go up like a match. And an old woman sat on a stool in the middle of it.

I worked it out in the time it takes to say it. She was seventy-one. There were two boys with batons. There was one candle.

I could open a door in the porter's shadow. I could drop him into whatever was under Wick Street. But twenty people would watch a man fall through the ground. By morning, every Seal investigator in Solmarch would stand on Wick Street with a measuring tape. I could send Candor through the lantern from the dark. But twenty people would see a white light, and Ysolde would see a report.

What I was allowed to use was a short dark lance. Every scholarship student was known to carry one. And I had my hood.

I put the hood up. I walked into the crowd.

That was easier than it sounds. People let you through if you walk like someone sent you. I came out at the front, between the porter and the door. My back was to the oak. My hands were empty. I turned around and looked at him.

He was bigger up close. He had sweat in his eyebrows. It was not a bad face. It was a face that had been shouting for an hour and was scared of what it would do next.

"Move," he said.

"She's in there."

"*I know she's in there!* That's why!" His voice cracked. "They'll hang her for that clerk. They hang us for anything."

"She sold him pears," I said.

That was not the answer he wanted. It was not really an answer at all. He drew his arm back.

I knocked.

The lance came into my left hand out of nothing. That was normal. Half the Year Fives at Hallowcrest could call a weapon. Then it folded. The door it folded into stood up in front of me. It was black and narrow, with the ring facing out. The lantern hit it in the center and burst.

Burning oil ran down the wood like rain down a window. The heat came through the door onto my hands. Fire spilled onto the stones at the foot of the door and lay there in a puddle. It went nowhere else. The door stood between the fire and the shop. The door did not care.

Someone screamed. Someone always does.

The next thing to come was a cobblestone. I did not see who threw it. I saw it over the top of the door for a moment, turning in the air. I could have stepped aside. But if I stepped aside, the stone would hit the bow window. Or the candle. Or the old woman's face.

I did not step.

It hit me above the left eye. There was a white flash and a noise like a dropped plank. Then I was still standing. I had decided that before. Something warm ran into my eyebrow.

The crowd went quiet. It was a special kind of quiet. I had heard it on other streets. It never lasted long.

The porter stared at the burning puddle. He had let go of the handle. His hands hung open.

At the far end of the street, whistles started. It was Seal reinforcements, a whole squad by the sound. They were running up from the river. The crowd heard them too. It began to fall apart at the edges. People remembered they had somewhere else to be.

I folded the door back into a lance. I folded the lance back into nothing. I kept my head down and left with them.

Behind me, through the bow window, the old woman had turned on her stool to watch me go. Her cat sat on the sill outside, between the glass and the flames. It was washing one paw.

* * *

"Is that blood?" said Ysolde.

"Some of it."

"Whose?"

"Mine. A man with a cobblestone."

"I didn't ask what he was holding."

She looked at the cut. Then she took a clean handkerchief from her desk drawer. She folded it into a pad and handed it to me without a word. That was the nearest she ever came to fussing. Her office was high in the east wing. It was narrow and tidy. It had three locked cabinets and a fourth that was not locked, because it only held forms.

"Sit down. Wick Street?"

"You heard."

"I hear everything that happens to the Golden Seal. They make sure of it." She sat and took the cap off her pen. "The pear woman was let go a quarter of an hour ago. There was no evidence. Someone upstairs had words." The pen did not move. "Someone in a hood stood in front of a Seal post with a door-shield. He took a stone for it. The report says: young, hooded, door-shield. There are maybe two hundred young men in Solmarch who carry a blocking lance."

"Then it's fine."

"Is it." That was not a question. She took a form from the fourth cabinet. "Moving on. Form 13-B. Your drill-warden."

"It wasn't a Veil."

"It was an enemy construct with Hush dust packed in its core. The form covers any enemy with Hush dust. Section four asks if it was a Veil." She turned the form to face me. Section four had two boxes. *Yes. No.* "You will tick no. Then section four-a asks you to explain why you are filling in a Veil form for something that is not a Veil."

"Because you asked me to."

"That is not one of the choices."

"Then the form is wrong."

"The form," said Ysolde, "has been wrong since the time of King Gerulf. It is still the form."

The door opened without a knock. Ysolde stood up. I stood up a second after her. I was still holding the handkerchief to my eyebrow.

King Theodric the Fourth was tall. He stooped a little in doorways, even when he did not need to. He wore shirtsleeves. There was ink on one cuff, and reading glasses were pushed up into his gray hair. He looked like what he had been doing. He had been reading something long and dull in a chair by a fire. The sword at his hip was plain gray and plainly old. I had never seen him without it.

"Sit, sit," he said. Then he saw my face. "What happened to you?"

"A cobblestone, Your Majesty."

"I can see it was a cobblestone." He looked at Ysolde. Ysolde looked at her form. He looked back at me, at the handkerchief and the cut. He looked for so long that I wondered what he saw. His face changed in a way I had no name for. Then it was gone, and he asked, quite gently, "Are you eating?"

"Yes, sir."

"Properly?"

"There's a boy in my Lance who keeps giving me his eggs."

"Good," said the King of Verandel. He was serious. "Let him." He touched my shoulder as he walked past. It was once, and it was quick, as if he wanted to check that I was there. To Ysolde he said, "Don't keep him long. He's bleeding on your forms." Then he left.

Ysolde sat down. I sat down too.

"Strent," she said.

My hand stayed on the handkerchief. "What about him?"

"The vice-headmaster sent a note to the bursar this morning. *Student out of bed, west workshops, after midnight. No harm done. I took a gentle view.* Your name is on it." She put the note on the desk between us. The writing was small and neat. A tiny owl was drawn in the margin, the way some teachers do to show they are not angry. "This morning, two of Daubert's wardens were back in their racks with their chests cracked. I want a report on Strent."

"There's nothing to report."

"You were seen."

"By a man who grades essays in the dark because lamps hurt his head."

"Eden."

"He was kind," I said. "He sent me to bed."

All of that was true. I could hear the humming while I said it. Four notes up, and the fifth one did not come. I did not tell her about that. I did not tell her about the top step, or my hand on the rail. I did not know why. No, that was not true. I knew why. I was the Black Order. I did not freeze on stairs.

She looked at me for a long time. Then she opened the third cabinet, the locked one. She took something out and put it on the desk. She kept her hand flat on top of it.

It was an envelope. It was old. The paper had turned the color of weak tea. The wax seal was pale gray, and I did not know it. She did not turn it over.

"What is that?" I asked.

"Orders you haven't earned." She put it back in the cabinet and locked it. "Not tonight."

"Because I didn't write a report."

"Because you lied to me about a teacher. You are not good at it, and you think you are." She took a new sheet of paper. She wrote on it, fast and square, and folded it in three. "New work. An Ember cell in the lower city, in the tannery streets. Four of them, maybe five. I think the clerk's ledger was going to them."

I took the paper.

"And Eden." She did not let go of it right away. "Watch the Tallis boy."
