# Not Technically a Veil

"Last car down, son. You riding it or writing it a poem?"

The funicular man had a pipe he never lit and a lantern he did, and he held the lantern up to my face as though he expected to know it. He didn't. Nobody does; that's what the face is for.

"Riding," I said.

"Curfew's tenth bell."

"I have a pass."

"Course you do." He took it, glanced at it upside down, and gave it back. "They all have passes. The ones without passes take the Long Stair and break their necks. Get in."

The car is a wooden box on iron wheels, with benches on two sides and windows that rattle, and it goes down the cliff face below Hallowcrest on a cable of braided vein-stone thick as a man's arm. On the way down, if you sit on the left, you see Solmarch come up to meet you: the river, the palace on its hill across the water, the lower city in a long smear of lights. Tonight there was a smudge in the smear. A part of Lantern Ward where the red lanterns had gone out in a ring, as if someone had put a thumb on them.

I sat on the left. I watched the ring the whole way down.

* * *

The monthly debrief is the one meeting Ysolde holds at the palace instead of the bookbinder's, because the King likes to drop in, and the King can't drop into a bookbinder's. You enter by the Gardeners' Gate on the river side and climb a stair that smells of potting soil, and nobody stops you because a clerk at the bottom has been told that a student comes on the first of the month to collect a book.

The quick way from the funicular to the Gardeners' Gate is through Lantern Ward. It goes past Quillwright Lane, and past the market, and past the stall where an old woman sells candied pears.

The stall was shut. Shutters down, padlocked, a Golden Seal notice pasted across them in that particular yellow they use. *Closed pending inquiry.* Her cat was sitting on top of the shutters with its paws folded under it, looking at the notice as though it might read it if it waited long enough.

I stopped. I don't usually.

The clerk who'd bought one pear a night from her for three weeks had vanished from the face of the world twelve days ago, in a dye-seller's cellar, because of me. A missing revenue clerk is the kind of thing the Golden Seal is very good at. They ask who saw him last. They ask who saw him every night.

Two streets further on, I found out where the lanterns had gone.

* * *

There was a chandlery on the corner of Wick Street, an old one with a bow window and a sign shaped like a candle, and the Seal had taken it over as a post. Somebody had put a yellow pennant on the sign. Somebody else had pulled it down and put it in the gutter.

The crowd filled the street from wall to wall. Lantern Ward people: dyers, porters, the fried-dough girl from the market, the families from the south duchies who came north in the bad years and never left. Not a mob yet. A crowd that hadn't decided. They'd taken the lanterns off their own doors and were holding them, unlit, which I didn't understand until I smelled it. Lamp oil, raw. Every lantern full.

Somebody at the front was shouting a name I didn't know, over and over. Then the name of the pear woman, which I did. Then: "She's seventy-one! She's *seventy-one!*"

Through the bow window, in the light of one candle, I could see her. Sitting on a stool in the middle of the shop. Small, upright, with her hands in her lap. Behind her, two Seal juniors in yellow armbands who couldn't have been much older than me, one of them with his hand on his baton and the other with his hand on the first one's arm.

The door was shut. A chandlery has a good door. Oak, iron-studded, made to keep out thieves who know what lamp oil sells for.

A man near the front, broad, a porter's strap across his chest, lit his lantern.

It caught all at once, because he'd slopped oil down the outside of the glass, and the crowd made a noise, half pulling back and half leaning in. He wasn't holding it like a light. He was holding it by the bail, at arm's length, swinging it a little. Measuring the distance to the door.

Thirty feet. Then the door burns, and the frame burns, and a chandlery full of tallow and wicks and casks burns like a struck match, and an old woman on a stool in the middle of it.

I worked it out in the time it takes to say it. Seventy-one. Two boys with batons. One candle. I could open a door in the porter's shadow and drop him into whatever's under Wick Street, and twenty people would see a man fall through the ground, and by morning every Seal investigator in Solmarch would be on Wick Street with a tape measure. I could put Candor through the lantern from the dark, and twenty people would see a white light, and Ysolde would see a report.

What I was allowed was a short dark lance that a scholarship student is known to carry, and my hood.

I put the hood up and walked through the crowd.

It's easier than it sounds. People let you through if you walk as if you've been sent. I came out at the front between the porter and the door with my back to the oak and my hands empty, and turned round, and looked at him.

He looked at me. He was bigger close up. Sweat in his eyebrows. Not a bad face. A face that had been shouting for an hour and was frightened of what it would do next.

"Move," he said.

"She's in there."

"*I know she's in there.* That's why." His voice cracked. "They'll hang her for that clerk. They hang us for anything."

"She sold him pears," I said.

That wasn't the answer he wanted. It wasn't really an answer. He drew his arm back.

I knocked.

The lance came into my left hand out of nothing, which was ordinary enough; half the Year Fives at Hallowcrest can call a weapon. Then it folded, and the door it folded into stood up in front of me, black and narrow, ring outward, and the lantern hit it dead center and burst.

Oil and flame ran down the panel like water down a window. The heat came through the wood onto my hands. Fire went down to the cobbles at the foot of the door and lay there burning in a puddle, and didn't go anywhere else, because the door was between it and the chandlery and the door didn't care.

Somebody screamed. Somebody always does.

The second thing that came was a cobblestone. I didn't see who threw it. I saw it over the top of the door for an instant, turning, and I could have stepped. Stepping would have meant the bow window, and the candle, and the old woman's face.

I didn't step.

It took me above the left eye. There was a white flash and a noise like a dropped plank, and then I was still standing, which I'd decided beforehand, and something warm was running into my eyebrow.

The crowd had gone quiet. It's a particular quiet; I've heard it before, on other streets. The quiet of people who've just seen what they almost did.

The porter was staring at the burning puddle. He'd let go of the bail. His hands hung open.

At the far end of the street, the whistles started. Seal reinforcements, a whole squad by the sound, coming up from the river at a run. The crowd heard them too, and began to come apart at the edges, people remembering they had somewhere else to be.

I folded the door back into a lance and the lance back into nothing, and kept my head down, and went with them.

Behind me, through the bow window, the old woman had turned on her stool to watch me go. Her cat was on the sill outside, between the glass and the flames, washing one paw.

* * *

"Is that blood?" said Ysolde.

"Some of it."

"Whose?"

"Mine. A man with a cobblestone."

"I didn't ask what he was holding." She looked at the cut for a moment, then took a clean handkerchief out of her desk drawer, folded it into a pad, and handed it to me without comment, which is the nearest she comes to fussing. Her office is high in the east wing, narrow and tidy, with three locked cabinets and a fourth that isn't locked because it only holds forms. "Sit. Wick Street?"

"You heard."

"I hear everything that happens to the Golden Seal. They make sure of it." She sat down and uncapped a pen. "The pear woman was released a quarter of an hour ago. No evidence. Somebody upstairs had words." Her pen didn't move. "Somebody hooded stood in front of a Seal post with a door-shield and took a stone for it. Description: young, hooded, a door-shield. There are perhaps two hundred young men in Solmarch who carry a blocking lance."

"Then it's fine."

"Is it." Not a question. She pulled a form from the fourth cabinet. "Moving on. Form 13-B. Your drill-warden."

"It wasn't a Veil."

"It was a hostile construct with Hush residue packed into its core. The form applies to any hostile entity with Hush residue. Section four asks whether it was a Veil." She turned the form round to show me. Section four had two boxes. *Yes. No.* "You'll tick no. Then section four-a asks you to explain why you're filing a Veil form for something that isn't one."

"Because you asked me to."

"That isn't one of the options."

"Then the form's wrong."

"The form," said Ysolde, "has been wrong since the reign of Gerulf. It's still the form."

The door opened without a knock, and Ysolde stood up, and I stood up a moment after her, still holding the handkerchief to my eyebrow.

King Theodric IV is a tall man who stoops a little in doorways, whether they need it or not. He was in shirtsleeves, with ink on one cuff and reading glasses pushed up into gray hair, and he looked like what he'd been doing, which was reading something long and dull in a chair by a fire. The sword at his hip was plain gray and plainly old. I've never seen him without it.

"Sit, sit," he said. Then he saw my face. "What happened to you?"

"A cobblestone, Your Majesty."

"I can see it was a cobblestone." He looked at Ysolde. Ysolde looked at her form. He looked back at me, at the handkerchief and the cut, for long enough that I started to wonder what he was seeing, and something went across his face that I don't have a name for. Then it was gone, and he said, quite gently, "Are you eating?"

"Yes, sir."

"Properly?"

"There's a boy in my Lance who keeps giving me his eggs."

"Good," said the King of Verandel, seriously. "Let him." He touched my shoulder on his way past, once, briefly, as if to check that it was there, and said to Ysolde, "Don't keep him long, he's bleeding on your forms," and went out.

Ysolde sat down. So did I.

"Strent," she said.

My hand stayed where it was, on the handkerchief. "What about him?"

"The vice-headmaster filed a note with the bursar's office this morning. Student out of bed, west workshops, after midnight, *no harm done, lenient view taken.* Your name." She set the note on the desk between us. Small, neat handwriting. A little sketch of an owl in the margin, the way some teachers do to show they're not angry. "Two of Daubert's wardens were found this morning back in their racks with their chests cracked. I would like a report on Strent."

"There's nothing to report."

"You were seen."

"By a man who grades essays in the dark because lamps hurt his head."

"Eden."

"He was kind," I said. "He sent me to bed."

All of which was true. I could hear the humming while I said it. Four notes up, and the fifth not coming. I didn't tell her that, or about the top step, or my hand on the rail. I don't know why. That isn't true; I do. I'm the Black Order. I don't freeze on stairs.

She looked at me for a long time. Then she opened the third cabinet, the locked one, and took something out and put it on the desk, and her hand stayed flat on top of it.

An envelope. Old. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, and the seal on it was a wax I didn't recognize, pale gray. She didn't turn it over.

"What's that?" I said.

"Orders you haven't earned." She slid it back into the cabinet and locked it. "Not tonight."

"Because I didn't write a report."

"Because you lied to me about a teacher, and you're not very good at it, and you think you are." She took a fresh sheet and wrote on it, quick and square, and folded it in three and held it out. "New work. An Ember cell, lower city, the tannery streets. Four, perhaps five. I think they're where the clerk's ledger was going."

I took it.

"And Eden." She didn't let go of the paper straight away. "Watch the Tallis boy."
