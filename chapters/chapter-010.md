# Clean Paper

> "Rules are for people who intend to keep their promises." - Lysette Hollen, Discipline Committee handbook, margin note

The Committee owned one ink pad, and it had been dry for nine days.

Lysette pressed the seal into it anyway, out of habit. Then she pressed it onto the warrant and looked at the result. It was the color of a sunburned knee. She set the seal down.

On the requisition spike by the door, her request for a new pad was still stuck where she'd left it. Under it sat a request for lamp oil, and under that, a request for two training batons to replace the two she'd broken. Every one carried the bursar's little stamp. *Deferred.*

Everything was deferred this month. Oil had gone up by a third since the trouble in Lantern Ward. The Undercroft had spent its whole construct budget replacing a warden nobody would explain. And the Discipline Committee, which had seven officers on paper, had exactly one this week. Three were on the Concord honor rota. Two had the coughing fever. The chair was the Crown Prince, who had other things to do.

So it was her. Her, one baton, and a pink seal.

A letter lay on the desk with a gold seal that was not pink at all. She'd been not opening it since breakfast.

She slid it to the corner of the desk, square with the edge, and opened the file instead.

* * *

She'd asked the registry for it at the start of the week. The Committee is allowed to ask for any student with more than thirty absences. It took the registry four days to find it. When it came, it was the thinnest file she'd ever held.

*Wren, Eden. Born PR 94, Thornmere duchy. Parents: none listed. Guardian: the Crown (royal scholarship, ward status). Entered Hallowcrest PR 108. Bell test: three. Disciplinary: none. Medical: see headmistress. Correspondence: none.*

"Correspondence, none," she read out loud, to the empty office.

Five years, and not one letter from home or from anywhere. Everyone got letters. Felix Ashgrove got a parcel from the Verdant Thorn every second week, full of socks. Rowan had nobody, and he still got letters from a whole frontier garrison, sixty men with terrible spelling.

*Medical: see headmistress.* She'd already seen the headmistress's handwriting, on a roof, about a foot from her face.

"There's nothing wrong with it," she muttered. "That's what's wrong with it."

A file with something wrong in it is a story. This one was clean the way a scrubbed floor is clean. It made you wonder what had been spilled.

She wrote in her own book: *Wren: no family, no letters, no illness on record.* She looked at it.

"Not your business," she told the page. "You know it isn't."

Then she drew a neat line through it. She'd written it anyway.

* * *

Lance Nine ate at the long table by the east windows, by order. Lysette came at noon with her book under her arm. Four of them were there. One was missing.

Rowan wasn't really missing. He sat at the next table with Clara and Clara's friends from the healers' wing. He'd brought a pot of honey from somewhere and was trying to pry it open with a spoon handle. "Give it here," Clara said, laughing. "You'll break the spoon."

"I won't! It's a very strong spoon!"

She was laughing at him, and he was laughing because she was. They laughed at the same moment, as if somebody had counted them in.

"Lysette!" Rowan called from the next table, waving a spoon dripping with nothing yet. "Do you want honey? I'm about to have honey!"

"No, thank you, Tallis."

"You say that now!"

She sat at the far end of her own table, opened her book, and ticked Felix and ticked Wren.

Wren had a cut over his left eye. It was a day old, closed with a strip of plaster someone had put on badly, crooked.

"What happened to your face?" she asked.

He looked up from his bread. "A door."

"You walked into a door."

"It was more that the door was there."

"A door," she repeated.

"A very rude one."

"Did it have a name?"

"It didn't introduce itself."

She didn't believe him and couldn't say why. It wasn't Committee business either. She ticked the box for *present* and moved on.

Seraphine Aurenmark arrived the way she always did, without being asked. She didn't sit. She stood behind Felix with a hand on his chair and tipped her head at Wren, like a cat eyeing a closed cupboard.

"Eden. That cut is very dashing. Was it for a lady?"

"No."

"Shame. I'd have liked to hear about her." She smiled. "You'd save me again, right? If I needed it. Like last time."

Lysette knew about last time only as a rumor. It went around Year Five in a version with a different number of wolves each time. The princess at twelve, lost in a hunting forest, and a boy nobody had heard of. That the boy was Wren had always sounded to Lysette like the kind of story people tell because it's funnier.

Wren seemed to really think about it, chewing.

"Probably," he said.

Seraphine laughed. It was a real laugh, surprised out of her. But under it, for a second, her face went still and careful. Then she put that away and turned to Felix.

"Right. Attempt one. Felix Ashgrove. Why did the griffon leave the Lantern Ball early?"

Felix looked up at her.

"It was *feathered out*," said Seraphine. "Tired. Feathered. It's a pun, you're allowed to make a noise."

Felix considered it. "Griffons don't go to the Ball."

"That's not the—"

"They're not allowed in the atrium. There was a rule after the one that ate the cake."

Seraphine stared at him. Then she pulled a pen from her sleeve, wrote a small, neat *1* on the back of her left hand, and showed it to him. She went back to her own table without another word. Felix watched her go. He didn't look like a man who'd won anything.

"You're staring," said Felix quietly, not looking up.

"I'm supervising."

"Mm."

She realized she'd been watching all of it. Seraphine and Felix. Wren chewing. The plaster on his eyebrow. The next table had gotten louder at some point, because Rowan had gotten the honey open and spilled it. She hadn't turned around.

She looked at the clock over the hatches. Twenty past one.

She'd sat down at noon.

She didn't turn around now, either. She made herself not. She ticked a box that didn't need ticking.

* * *

The duel was at the third bell, in the Long Yard, and it was hers, because there was nobody else.

Florian Eschenbach of Year Four had said something about Gisela Morvaine's father's debts in a corridor. Gisela had asked for satisfaction in the proper form, on the proper paper, which Lysette had to admit she liked.

The rules said one Committee officer to judge, and a second to confirm. There was no second.

She'd prepared. She walked the ring before they came and checked the chalk. It was a clean, unbroken line, twelve paces across. She checked both blades and both students for anything up their sleeves.

"Anyone who steps down onto the sand gets written up," she told the thirty-odd spectators on the yard wall. None of them could see the color of her seal from up there, so they believed her.

"Rules, please," she told the duelists. "First clean touch. Blunted blades. Stay inside the chalk."

"Understood," said Gisela.

"Whatever," said Florian.

It went fast. Gisela was better and knew it. Florian circled and circled, then came in low. She turned his blade and touched him on the collarbone, clean, and the wall cheered.

"Foul!" said Florian, before the cheer was over. He pointed at the chalk. "She was out. Look. Her heel's over."

Lysette looked. Gisela's left heel was over the line. It was a finger's width, no more, but over. Gisela stared at her own foot like it belonged to someone else.

"Officer?" said Florian. "You see it. Everybody sees it."

"I see a heel," said Lysette.

"Well, then!"

"I wasn't," she said. "I *wasn't*. I know where I was!"

"She was out!"

"Was not!"

The wall started arguing. It's what walls do.

"I need a minute," she said.

"For what?" Florian scoffed. "It's right there!"

With a second officer, they'd have compared what they'd each seen. With ink, Lysette could have sealed a provisional ruling and sent it to the chair, and let the Crown Prince decide. She had neither. She had her own eyes, which had been on the blades and not the feet. And she had one other thing.

She hadn't used it in eight days. She'd stood in the Undercroft with her hands shaking and her Tenet right there, and hadn't. She wasn't going to think about why on a dueling ground in front of thirty people.

She knelt by the ring and put her palm flat on the sand.

Witness doesn't come cheap. It never has. It takes from behind the eyes, and the farther back you go, the more it takes. Half a minute gives you a headache. Five minutes gives you a nosebleed. She'd once gone back an hour and slept for a day after. She went back thirty seconds.

The yard turned thin and gray around her. Over it, in pale gold ghost-light, the bout played again. The circling. The low attack. The turn and the touch. Gisela's ghost-heel came down inside the line. Clearly inside, a hand's width.

And the line moved.

Lysette watched it. A ghost-thread of chalk slid across the sand like something pulled on a string, an inch, two inches, toward Gisela's foot, right at the moment of the touch. On the other side of the ring, Florian's ghost-hand hung down at his hip with two fingers crooked. His ghost-lips were moving.

His Tenet was something small. A drift, a nudge, the kind that moves dust or turns a page. She'd read it in his file once and forgotten it. It was enough to move a line of chalk.

She let the light go. The headache slammed in behind her eyes like a door in another room.

"Touch to Morvaine," she said. "Eschenbach, you'll come to the Committee office at the fifth bell. Bring the chalk."

He went white.

"You can't prove—"

"I can," said Lysette. "Fifth bell."

On the wall, the people who'd been arguing went quiet. Then they started arguing about something else.

Gisela came over afterward to thank her.

"How did you know?" she asked. "I thought I'd lose on the heel."

"I looked again," said Lysette. "Properly. It's my job." That was almost true. She didn't say it was the first time in eight days she'd been sure of something she'd seen.

* * *

She opened the letter in her room that night, with the shutters closed and no lamp. She had no oil either.

Her mother wrote the way she sat a court. Upright, no ornament, every sentence exactly the length of a sentence. The Seal was being laughed at in the lower city. A clerk had vanished, an old woman had been held and released, and the Seal had looked clumsy and cruel in the same week, which was a talent. The Hollen name was still spoken in a certain tone at certain tables. It would be, until the Black Order was dragged into the light and shown to the kingdom for what it was. *Until then, we are the family of a traitor, and we will conduct ourselves as if we were not.*

Then, lower down, the handwriting changed slightly, the way it did when her mother wrote something she hadn't planned.

*One good thing. On Wick Street, when the crowd brought oil to our post, a young man came out of nowhere and stood between our door and a burning lamp, and took a stone for it, and was gone before anyone could ask his name. Two of our juniors are alive because of him, and an old woman. I mean to find him and thank him. Hollens pay their debts. Your brother would have liked him, I think.*

*Corentin would have been twenty on the fourteenth. Wear the locket, if it's mended.*

Lysette sat on the edge of her bed with the letter in her lap for a long while.

"Twenty," she said to the dark. Nobody answered. Nobody had in a long time.

The locket was not mended. It lay in a drawer in two pieces, with the chain coiled beside it. She didn't take it out.

Instead she emptied her coat pockets onto the blanket, the way she did every night. A Committee officer's pockets should hold what she means them to hold and nothing else. Her pencil. Her book. Half a broken baton, no use to anyone.

And the handkerchief.

It had silver stitching along one edge and a little feather worked into the corner. It still had a crease where it had been folded small. One side had a faint mark where she'd pressed it to her face on the roof, three weeks ago. It smelled of nothing much. Grass. Cold air. Him, a little, probably, though she wasn't going to look into that.

"I'll wash it," she'd told him. Like a promise. Like a rule.

The basin stood on the stand by the window. There was water in the jug. There was soap. Soap, at least, the bursar hadn't deferred.

She sat with the handkerchief in both hands. The letter lay on the blanket beside her. *I mean to find him and thank him.* The broken baton. The dark.

Then she folded it again, small, along the old crease, and put it back in her pocket.
