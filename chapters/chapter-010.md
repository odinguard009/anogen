# Clean Paper

> "Rules are for people who intend to keep their promises." - Lysette Hollen, Discipline Committee handbook, margin note

The Committee owned one ink pad, and it had been dry for nine days.

Lysette pressed the seal into it anyway, from habit. Then she pressed the seal onto a warrant and looked at the mark. It was the color of a sunburned knee. She put the seal down.

On the spike by the door, her request for a new pad was still stuck where she had left it. Under it was a request for lamp oil. Under that was a request for two new training batons, to replace the two she had broken. Each one had the bursar's little stamp on it. *Deferred.*

Everything was deferred this month. Oil cost a third more since the trouble in Lantern Ward. The Undercroft had spent its whole budget on a new warden, and no one would say why. The Discipline Committee had seven officers on paper. This week it had one. Three were away on the Concord honor duty. Two had the coughing fever. The chair was the Crown Prince, who had other things to do.

So it was her. Her, one baton, and a pink seal.

A letter lay on the desk. It had a gold seal that was not pink at all. She had not opened it since breakfast.

She pushed it to the corner of the desk, in line with the edge. She opened a file instead.

* * *

She had asked the registry for the file at the start of the week. The Committee could ask for any student with more than thirty absences. The registry took four days to find it. When it came, it was the thinnest file she had ever held.

*Wren, Eden. Born PR 94, Thornmere duchy. Parents: none listed. Guardian: the Crown (royal scholarship, ward status). Entered Hallowcrest PR 108. Bell test: three. Disciplinary: none. Medical: see headmistress. Correspondence: none.*

"Correspondence, none," she read out loud to the empty office.

Five years, and not one letter from home. Not one from anywhere.

"Everyone gets letters," she said. "Felix gets socks every second week. Rowan has no family, and sixty men at a fort write to him. They spell badly, but they write."

Wren had nothing.

*Medical: see headmistress.* She had already seen the headmistress's writing. It was on a roof, about a foot from her face.

"There's nothing wrong with it," she muttered. "That's what's wrong with it."

A file with a problem in it told a story. This one was clean, like a floor someone had scrubbed too hard.

"So what did you spill?" she asked the page.

She wrote in her own book: *Wren: no family, no letters, no illness on record.* She looked at the line.

"It's not your business," she told the page. "You know it isn't."

She drew a neat line through the words. But she had written them.

* * *

Lance Nine ate at the long table by the east windows. That was an order. Lysette came at noon with her book under her arm. Four of them were there. One was missing.

Rowan was not really missing. He sat at the next table with Clara and Clara's friends from the healers' wing. He had brought a pot of honey from somewhere. He was trying to open it with the handle of a spoon.

"Give it here," Clara said, laughing. "You'll break the spoon."

"I won't! It's a strong spoon!"

She laughed at him. He laughed because she did. They laughed at the same moment, as if someone had counted them in.

"Lysette!" Rowan called. He waved a spoon with nothing on it yet. "Do you want honey? I'm about to have honey!"

"No, thank you, Tallis."

"You say that now!"

She sat at the far end of her own table. She opened her book. She ticked Felix, and she ticked Wren.

Wren had a cut over his left eye. It was a day old. Someone had put a crooked strip of plaster on it.

"What happened to your face?" she asked.

He looked up from his bread. "A door."

"You walked into a door."

"It was more that the door was there."

"A door," she said.

"A rude one."

"Did it have a name?"

"It didn't say."

She did not believe him, and she could not say why.

"Did the door say sorry?" she asked.

"It wasn't that kind of door."

It was not Committee business. She ticked the box for *present* and moved on.

Seraphine Aurenmark arrived the way she always did, without being asked. She did not sit. She stood behind Felix with a hand on his chair. She tilted her head at Wren and stared.

"Eden. That cut is very dashing. Was it for a lady?"

"No."

"Shame. I would have liked to hear about her." She smiled. "You'd save me again, right? If I needed it. Like last time."

Lysette knew about last time only as a rumor. It went around Year Five, and it had a different number of wolves each time. The princess was twelve. She was lost in a hunting forest. A boy no one had heard of came and saved her. That the boy was Wren had always sounded to Lysette like a story people tell because it is funny.

Wren thought about it while he chewed. He really did think.

"Probably," he said.

Seraphine laughed. It was a real laugh, and it surprised her. But under it, for a second, her face went still and careful. Then she put that away and turned to Felix.

"Right. Attempt one. Felix Ashgrove. Why did the griffon leave the Lantern Ball early?"

Felix looked up at her.

"Because it was *feathered out*," said Seraphine. "Tired. Feathered. It's a pun. You're allowed to make a noise."

Felix thought about it. "Griffons don't go to the Ball."

"That's not the point."

"They're not allowed in the big hall. There was a rule after the one that ate the cake."

Seraphine stared at him. Then she took a pen from her sleeve and wrote a small, neat *1* on the back of her left hand.

"That's one," she said. "Attempt two is tomorrow."

"Is there a prize?"

"Yes. You laugh."

She went back to her own table. Felix watched her go. He did not look like a man who had won.

"You're staring," said Felix quietly. He did not look up.

"I'm supervising."

"Mm."

She noticed she had been watching all of it. Seraphine and Felix. Wren, chewing. The plaster on his eyebrow. The next table had gotten louder at some point.

"I got it open!" Rowan shouted. "And on my sleeve!"

Clara laughed. Lysette did not turn around.

She looked at the clock over the food windows. It was twenty past one.

She had sat down at noon.

She did not turn around now, either. She ticked a box that did not need ticking.

* * *

The duel was at the third bell, in the Long Yard. It was hers, because there was no one else.

Florian Eschenbach of Year Four had said something about Gisela Morvaine's father's debts. He said it in a hallway. Gisela asked for a duel in the proper way, on the proper paper. Lysette had to admit she liked that.

The rules said one Committee officer should judge, and a second should confirm. There was no second.

She had prepared. She walked around the ring and checked the chalk. The line was whole, twelve paces across. She checked both blades. She checked both students' sleeves.

"Anyone who steps down onto the sand gets written up," she told the thirty or so people on the yard wall. They could not see the color of her seal from up there. So they believed her.

"Rules, please," she told the duelists. "First clean touch wins. Blunt blades. Stay inside the chalk."

"Understood," said Gisela.

"Whatever," said Florian.

It went fast. Gisela was better, and she knew it. Florian walked in circles, and then he came in low. She turned his blade and touched him on the collarbone. It was a clean touch, and the wall cheered.

"Foul!" Florian said, before the cheer was over. He pointed at the chalk. "She was out. Look. Her heel is over."

Lysette looked. Gisela's left heel was over the line. It was the width of a finger, no more, but it was over. Gisela stared at her own foot. It might have belonged to someone else.

"Officer?" said Florian. "You see it. Everyone sees it."

"I see a heel," said Lysette.

"Well, then!"

"I wasn't out," said Gisela. "I *wasn't*. I know where I was!"

"She was out!"

"I was not!"

The wall began to shout. Half of them said out. Half of them said in.

"I need a minute," said Lysette.

"For what?" Florian laughed at her. "It's right there!"

With a second officer, they would have compared what each one saw. With ink, Lysette could have sealed a first ruling and sent it to the chair. The Crown Prince could decide. She had neither. She had her own eyes, and they had watched the blades, not the feet. And she had one other thing.

She had not used it in eight days. She had stood in the Undercroft with her hands shaking and her Tenet right there, and she had not used it. She would not think about why on a dueling ground in front of thirty people.

She knelt by the ring. She put her palm flat on the sand.

Witness was not cheap. It took from behind her eyes. Half a minute gave her a headache. Five minutes gave her a nosebleed. Once she had gone back an hour, and she had slept for a day after. This time she went back thirty seconds.

The yard turned thin and gray around her. In pale gold ghost-light, the fight played again. The circling. The low attack. The turn and the touch. Gisela's ghost-heel came down inside the line. It was inside by a hand's width.

And the line moved.

Lysette watched it. A thread of chalk slid across the sand, as if someone pulled it on a string. It moved an inch, then two, toward Gisela's foot, at the moment of the touch. On the other side of the ring, Florian's ghost-hand hung down at his hip. Two fingers were bent. His ghost-lips were moving.

His Tenet was small. It could nudge things, the way wind moves dust or turns a page. She had read it in his file once and forgotten it. It was enough to move a line of chalk.

She let the light go. The headache slammed in behind her eyes, like a door slamming in another room.

"Touch to Morvaine," she said. "Eschenbach, come to the Committee office at the fifth bell. Bring the chalk."

He went white.

"You can't prove it."

"I can," said Lysette. "Fifth bell."

"How? You didn't even look at my feet!"

"I didn't need your feet. I looked at your hand."

He opened his mouth. Then he shut it.

On the wall, the people who had been arguing went quiet. Then they started to argue about something else.

Gisela came over afterward to thank her.

"How did you know?" she asked. "I thought I would lose on the heel."

"I looked again," said Lysette. "Properly. It's my job."

"You looked at his hands."

"Yes."

"Nobody ever looks at the hands."

"I do," said Lysette. "Now go and sit down. You are shaking."

That was almost true, about looking again. She did not say it was the first time in eight days she had felt sure of something she saw.

* * *

She opened the letter in her room that night. The shutters were closed, and she had no lamp. She had no oil, either.

Her mother wrote the way she sat in court. She was upright, with no decoration. Every sentence was exactly as long as a sentence should be. The Seal was a joke in the lower city. A clerk had vanished. An old woman had been held and then let go. The Seal had looked foolish and cruel in the same week, which took skill. The Hollen name was still said in a certain voice at certain tables. It would be, until the Black Order was dragged into the light. Then the kingdom would see what it was. *Until then, we are the family of a traitor, and we will act as if we were not.*

Then, lower down, the writing changed a little. It did that when her mother wrote something she had not planned.

*One good thing. On Wick Street, a crowd brought oil to our post. A young man came from nowhere. He stood between our door and a burning lamp, and he took a stone for it. He was gone before anyone could ask his name. Two of our juniors are alive because of him, and an old woman. I mean to find him and thank him. Hollens pay their debts. Your brother would have liked him, I think.*

*Corentin would have been twenty on the fourteenth. Wear the locket, if it is mended.*

Lysette sat on the edge of her bed with the letter in her lap.

"Twenty," she said to the dark. No one answered. No one had answered for a long time.

The locket was not mended. It lay in a drawer in two pieces, with the chain coiled beside it. She did not take it out.

Instead she emptied her coat pockets onto the blanket, as she did every night. Her pencil. Her book. Half a broken baton, no use to anyone.

And the handkerchief.

It had silver thread along one edge and a small feather sewn in the corner. It still had a fold where someone had made it small. One side had a faint mark. She had pressed it to her face on the roof, three weeks ago. It smelled of not much. Grass. Cold air. Him, a little, maybe. She was not going to think about that.

"I'll wash it," she had told him. It had sounded like a promise. It had sounded like a rule.

The basin stood on the stand by the window. There was water in the jug. There was soap. At least the bursar had not deferred soap.

She sat with the handkerchief in both hands. The letter lay on the blanket beside her. *I mean to find him and thank him.* The broken baton. The dark.

Then she folded it again, small, along the old fold. She put it back in her pocket.
