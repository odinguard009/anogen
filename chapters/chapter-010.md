# Clean Paper

> "Rules are for people who intend to keep their promises." - Lysette Hollen, Discipline Committee handbook, margin note

The Committee owned one ink pad, and it had been dry for nine days.

Lysette pressed the seal into it anyway, out of habit, and then into the warrant, and looked at the result, which was the color of a sunburned knee. She set the seal down. On the requisition spike by the door, her request for a new pad was still impaled where she'd put it, under a request for lamp oil, under a request for two training batons to replace the two she'd broken, and every one of them had the bursar's little stamp at the bottom: *Deferred.*

Everything was deferred this month. The bursar had a list. Oil had gone up a third since the trouble in Lantern Ward, and the Undercroft had eaten its whole construct budget replacing a warden nobody would explain, and the Discipline Committee, which had seven officers on paper, had this week exactly one, because three were on the Concord honor rota and two had the coughing fever and the chair was the Crown Prince and had other things to do.

So it was her. Her and one baton and a pink seal.

There was a letter on the desk with a gold seal that was not pink at all. She'd been not opening it since breakfast.

She moved it to the corner of the desk, square with the edge, and opened the file instead.

* * *

She'd asked the registry for it at the start of the week, in the way the Committee is entitled to ask for any student with more than thirty absences. It had taken the registry four days to find it. When it came, it was the thinnest file she'd ever held.

*Wren, Eden. Born PR 94, Thornmere duchy. Parents: none listed. Guardian: the Crown (royal scholarship, ward status). Entered Hallowcrest PR 108, Bell test: three. Disciplinary: none. Medical: see headmistress. Correspondence: none.*

Correspondence none. Five years, and not a single letter home, not a single letter from anywhere. Everyone got letters. Felix Ashgrove got a parcel from the Verdant Thorn every second week, full of socks. Rowan, who had nobody, got letters from an entire frontier garrison, sixty men with terrible spelling.

Medical: see headmistress. She'd seen the headmistress's handwriting already, on a roof, about a foot from her face.

There was nothing wrong with the file. That was what she kept coming back to. A file with something wrong in it is a story. This one was clean the way a scrubbed floor is clean, where you start to wonder what was spilled.

She wrote, in her own book: *Wren: no family, no letters, no illness on record.* She looked at it. Then she drew a line through it, neatly, because it wasn't Committee business, and she knew it wasn't, and she'd written it anyway.

* * *

Lance Nine ate at the long table by the east windows, by order. Lysette came to it at noon with her book under her arm and found four of them there and one missing.

Rowan wasn't missing, exactly. He was at the next table, with Clara and Clara's friends from the healers' wing, and he'd brought a pot of honey from somewhere and was trying to get it open with a spoon handle, and Clara was laughing at him, and he was laughing because she was. They had a way of laughing at the same moment, as if somebody had counted them in.

Lysette sat down at the far end of her own table and opened her book and ticked Felix and ticked Wren.

Wren had a cut over his left eye. Fresh, a day old, closed with a strip of plaster that somebody had put on badly, crooked.

"What happened to your face?"

He looked up from his bread. "A door."

"You walked into a door."

"It was more that the door was there."

She didn't believe him and couldn't have said why, and it wasn't Committee business either, so she ticked the box for *present* and moved on.

Seraphine Aurenmark arrived the way she always did, which was without being asked. She didn't sit. She stood behind Felix with her hand on the back of his chair and looked across the table at Wren with her head tipped, the way a cat looks at a closed cupboard.

"Eden," she said. "That cut's very dashing. Was it for a lady?"

"No."

"Shame. I'd have liked to hear about her." She smiled. "You'd save me again, right? If I needed it. Like last time."

Lysette knew about last time only as rumor, the kind that goes round the Year Fives in a version with a different number of wolves each telling. The princess at twelve, lost in a hunting forest, and a boy nobody had heard of. That the boy was Wren had always seemed to Lysette like the kind of story people tell because it's funnier.

Wren thought about it. He seemed to actually think about it, chewing.

"Probably," he said.

Seraphine laughed. It was a real laugh, startled out of her. But under it, for a second, something in her face went still and careful, and then she put it away, and turned to Felix.

"Right," she said. "Attempt one. Felix Ashgrove. Why did the griffon refuse to fly on the Lantern Ball?"

Felix looked up at her.

"Because it didn't have a partner," said Seraphine. "Because it was *feathered out*."

Felix considered this. "Griffons don't go to the Ball."

"That's not the—"

"They're not allowed in the atrium. There was a rule after the one that ate the cake."

Seraphine looked at him for a long moment. Then she took a pen out of her sleeve and wrote a small, neat *1* on the back of her left hand, and showed it to him, and went back to her own table without another word. Felix watched her go. He didn't look like a man who'd won anything.

Lysette realized she had been watching all of it, Seraphine and Felix, Wren chewing, the plaster on his eyebrow, and that the next table had got louder at some point, Rowan had got the honey open and spilled it, and she hadn't turned round.

She looked at the clock over the hatches. Twenty past one.

She'd sat down at noon.

She didn't turn round now either. She made herself not, and ticked a box that didn't need ticking.

* * *

The duel was at the third bell, in the Long Yard, and it was hers, because there was nobody else.

Florian Eschenbach of Year Four had said something about Gisela Morvaine's father's debts in a corridor, and Gisela Morvaine had asked for satisfaction in the proper form, on the proper paper, which Lysette had to admit she appreciated. To first clean touch, blunted blades, inside the chalk ring. A Committee officer to judge, with a second officer to confirm. There was no second officer.

She'd prepared. She'd walked the ring before they came and checked the chalk, a clean, unbroken line, twelve paces across. She'd checked both blades for blunting and both students for anything up their sleeves. She'd told the thirty-odd spectators on the yard wall that anyone who stepped down onto the sand would be written up, and since none of them could see the color of her seal from up there, they'd believed her.

It went fast. Gisela was better and knew it. Florian circled, and circled, and then came in low, and she turned his blade and touched him on the collarbone, clean, and the wall cheered.

"Foul," said Florian, before the cheer was finished. He was pointing at the chalk. "She was out. Look. Her heel's over."

Lysette looked. Gisela's left heel was over the line. By a finger's width, no more, but over. Gisela looked down at her own foot as if it belonged to someone else.

"I wasn't," she said. "I *wasn't*, I know where I was."

The wall started arguing. It's what walls do.

With a second officer, they'd have compared what they'd each seen. With ink, Lysette could have sealed a provisional ruling and sent it to the chair and let the Crown Prince decide. She had neither. She had her own eyes, which had been on the blades and not the feet, and she had one other thing.

She hadn't used it in eight days. She'd stood in the Undercroft with her hands shaking and her Tenet right there, and hadn't, and she wasn't going to think about why on a dueling ground in front of thirty people.

She knelt by the ring and put her palm flat on the sand.

Witness doesn't come cheap. It never has. It takes from behind the eyes, and the longer you go back, the more it takes; half a minute is a headache, five minutes is a nosebleed, and she'd once done an hour and slept for a day after. She went back thirty seconds.

The yard went thin and gray around her. Over it, in pale gold ghost-light, the bout played itself again: the circling, the low attack, the turn and the touch. Gisela's ghost-heel came down inside the line. Clearly inside, a hand's width inside.

And the line moved.

Lysette watched it. A ghost-thread of chalk, sliding on the sand like something pulled on a string, an inch, two inches, toward Gisela's foot, at the moment of the touch. On the other side of the ring, Florian's ghost-hand was down at his hip, two fingers crooked, and his ghost-lips were moving.

His Tenet was something small. A drift, a nudge, the kind that moves dust or turns a page. She'd read it in his file once and forgotten it. It was enough to move a line of chalk.

She let the light go. The headache came in behind her eyes like a door slammed in another room.

"Touch to Morvaine," she said. "Eschenbach, you'll come to the Committee office at the fifth bell. Bring the chalk."

He went white. On the wall, people who had been arguing went quiet and then started arguing about something else.

Gisela Morvaine came over afterward to thank her. Lysette said it was her job, which it was, and didn't say that it was the first time in eight days she'd been sure of something she'd seen.

* * *

She opened the letter in her room that night, with the shutters closed and no lamp, because she had no oil either.

Her mother wrote the way she sat a court: upright, without ornament, every sentence the length of a sentence. The Seal was being laughed at in the lower city. A clerk had vanished and an old woman had been held and released and the Seal had been made to look clumsy and cruel in the same week, which was a talent. The Hollen name was still spoken in a certain tone at certain tables, and would be, until the Black Order was dragged into the light and shown to the kingdom for what it was. *Until then, we are the family of a traitor, and we will conduct ourselves as if we were not.*

Then, lower down, the handwriting changed slightly, as it did when her mother was writing something she hadn't planned.

*One good thing. On Wick Street, when the crowd brought oil to our post, a young man came out of nowhere and stood between our door and a burning lamp, and took a stone for it, and was gone before anyone could ask his name. Two of our juniors are alive because of him, and an old woman. I mean to find him and thank him. Hollens pay their debts. Your brother would have liked him, I think.*

*Corentin would have been twenty on the fourteenth. Wear the locket, if it's mended.*

Lysette sat on the edge of her bed with the letter in her lap for some time.

The locket was not mended. It was in a drawer, in two pieces, with the chain coiled beside it. She didn't take it out.

Instead she emptied her coat pockets onto the blanket, the way she did every night, because a Committee officer's pockets should hold what she means them to and nothing else. Her pencil. Her book. Half a broken baton, which was no use to anyone.

And the handkerchief.

Silver stitching along one edge, a little feather worked in the corner. It still had a crease where it had been folded small, and a faint mark on one side where she'd pressed it to her face on the roof three weeks ago. It smelled of nothing much. Grass. Cold air. Him, a little, probably, though she wasn't going to examine that.

She'd told him she'd wash it.

The basin was on the stand by the window. There was water in the jug. There was soap; soap, at least, the bursar hadn't deferred.

She sat with the handkerchief in both hands, and the letter on the blanket beside her, *I mean to find him and thank him*, and the broken baton, and the dark.

Then she folded it again, small, along the old crease, and put it back in her pocket.
