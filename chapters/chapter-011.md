# Nine Minutes of Somebody Else's Maze

"Who's carrying the slate?"

No one answered. Okoru waited. He was chewing a piece of dried licorice root. He kept it in his cheek and never shared it. He held the slate out at the end of his one arm. He asked again, with his eyebrows this time.

"Hollen has the slate," said Felix.

"I have the Committee's clipboard," said Lysette. "That's different."

"Is it?"

"It has a clip."

Okoru dropped the slate into Clara's hands. She stood closest, so she looked like the one who would take it. Clara looked at the slate the way she looked at most things, with no opinion at all. She put it under her arm.

We stood in the cold first gallery of the Undercroft, under the Bell Door. Four Lances had gone in that morning. They had come out the far side in different shapes. Lance One was on the board at six minutes forty. No one would beat that, because Lance One never left anything behind. The star-readers had finished at nineteen minutes. One of them had a burnt sleeve. The other had put out the fire with a wet glove. They were still arguing about whose fault it was.

"Graded field run," said Okoru. "Nine minutes on the glass. Every Lance gets a different maze, so don't ask the ones who finished. They don't know yours. They would lie anyway."

"What's in it?" said Rowan. He had been bouncing on his heels since we came down.

"Walls that move. Some walls that aren't there. At the fourth minute, the Kennel opens. The Kennel is whatever the bursar could pay for."

"What's the Kennel?"

"A lesson in saving money." Okoru turned the glass over. "Tenets are allowed. Relics are not. The exit is where the exit is. Go."

The Bell Door opened. Behind it was a passage as wide as two men. It was the color of wet slate. Rowan ran through before the first sand finished falling.

* * *

I knew one thing after forty steps. I knew it from the door frame, before I even looked.

The lamps were wrong.

At every turn in the Undercroft, a glowstone hung on a chain. It was a green-white lump the size of a thumb. Each one made one shadow. A gate post, a bench, a boot. One shadow each.

Here, every shadow had a second one. It was faint. It lay under the first, like a ghost. And all the faint ones leaned the same way. They pointed down the passage and off to the left.

That meant a bigger light, far ahead and very strong. Whoever built this maze had hung a harbor lamp over the exit. They had forgotten that light goes around corners.

I did not say so. I was Wren. Wren slept. Wren had sixty-one absences and a lance for blocking.

"Dead end!" Rowan called from the front.

It looked like one. There was a rough wall, a gutter, and a rusty ring in the stone. Lysette stepped up beside him. She touched the wall with two fingers. Her eyes went pale, and the edge of each eye lit up like the rim of a coin.

The wall remembered for her.

A ghost-light rose in the stone. It was a small gray man in a leather apron. He pulled a painted canvas across the passage and dropped a bar to hold it. It took about as long as a blink. Then it faded, and Lysette lifted her hand.

"It's canvas," she said. "Two bars, top and bottom. Someone put it there this morning."

"You *saw* that?" said Rowan.

"I'm a Witness, Tallis. That's what the word means."

He kicked out the bottom bar. The canvas fell flat. More passage lay behind it. Clara stepped over it. She said, to no one in particular, "She's good."

Lysette's neck turned red above her collar. She wrote something on her clipboard. I am sure it was not a mark.

I stayed at the back. I watched the faint shadows.

At the second turn, the passage split three ways. Felix went down on one knee. His Tenet was called Trail. It was quiet and did not glow. He just went still, and his eyes lost their focus. It looked like he was trying to hear one voice at a loud table. He pointed right.

"Boots," he said. "Heavy ones. Many of them, going in and out. The floor is worn smooth."

"Worn means people walk it," said Lysette.

"People walk every maze to set it up. But this one was walked a hundred times." He frowned. "That's more than you need."

"So it's the right way," said Rowan. "Come on."

"Or it's bait," I said.

They all looked at me. I hate that.

"Left," I said. "I think. I'm guessing."

"You're guessing," said Lysette.

"My Tenet makes shadows do things. It's only a feeling." I shrugged with one shoulder. It was a good shrug. It looked sorry. I had used it for years. "Left feels deeper."

Felix looked at the left branch. Then he looked at me, half a second longer than he needed to. Then he stood up.

"Left," he said. He sounded like a man who had decided to be wrong with friends.

We walked a long way down the next passage. Only then did I stop watching the back of his neck.

* * *

At the fourth minute, the Kennel opened. The bursar had bought what he could pay for.

Five of them came out of the dark in a line. The board called them dust-hounds. Each one had a frame of cane with canvas stretched over the ribs. Each had sand in its belly for weight and a tin bell for a tongue. They were cheap and fast, and they were not funny at all.

One tin tongue rang. Then all of them rang. The noise came down the passage like a sack of knives.

"Back!" Felix had his bow out before the second ring. "Everyone back!"

Okoru's rule was no relics. Tenets were fine, and so were plain weapons. Felix shot an arrow through the first hound's front leg. It tripped over its own foot and crashed into the second. They fell in a pile against the gutter, rattling. Sand poured out of one, like stuffing from a torn pillow.

"Three left," said Clara. Her voice was flat like a bench.

They split around the pile. Two ran at Felix. One ran at Rowan.

"Get behind me!" Rowan told everyone.

"Don't!" said Lysette. "You're in the open! You don't have a line! You're—"

He lit his hand.

It was Spark, not Dawn. A small gold flare, the size of a lantern, flew up into the cane faces. Whatever the hounds saw with, it was not made for gold. The first two froze. Their tin tongues clacked, and their heads swung from side to side, as if the passage had changed shape. The third ran into the wall. It was chasing its own shadow.

Rowan was grinning. Then he saw Felix's arm.

"Huh," said Felix.

A broken cane rib had cut across his arm. It was not deep. But the blood ran out in a thin line, like ink from a pen. He looked at it with polite interest.

Clara was already there. She had not run. She had been standing, and then she was next to him. She held his wrist in both of her small hands. Her lips moved on words I could not hear. You do not watch that part. I watched his sleeve instead.

The edges of the cut came together as I looked. They turned pink, and they closed.

"Oh," said Felix. "That's strange." He sounded as if she had pulled a coin from his ear.

For about four seconds, the five of us were a real Lance. Rowan's light held the hounds. Felix loaded his bow again. Lysette took two steps left and called out the weak points.

"The bottom rib! It's tied there! Look, all four are tied there!"

Felix shot the knot on the nearest hound. It came apart in the middle of a step. Then Rowan's hand began to shake, and his light went out. The next hound turned around.

"Run," I said.

I used the voice I keep for bad news in a warehouse. No one argued.

* * *

I had always thought the worst part of being chased by a sandbag was the noise. It is not. The worst part is that they never stop. They have no mind to lose. A hound that has your footsteps keeps them until its sand runs out. It follows you around corners, down stairs, and across a floor that someone just washed.

We ran with the glass ticking behind us. Rowan carried Clara over a gap she could never have jumped alone. Felix hit a wall twice. He swore in a language I did not know he had.

"Was that Karsi?" Rowan panted.

"Shut up and run," said Felix.

Then, at a square junction under a nearly dead lamp, Lysette stopped so fast that I almost hit her. There were two passages. Both were lit, and both were empty. Behind us, the tin tongues were close.

"Which?" she said.

Both shadows leaned left. The faint ones, the borrowed ones, all pointed the same way. I could not tell her why I was sure. I would have had to tell her what I was.

"That one," I said. "Please."

The *please* worked. I did not say it often. She looked at me once, fast, and went.

The left passage got smaller. It became a crawl under a low arch. A rusty gate hung halfway down. You had to go under it on your stomach. Lysette went first, then Clara, then Rowan with his shoulders turned sideways. Felix went next. He reached back through the gap.

"Hand!" he said.

I took it. He pulled.

The hound reached the arch just as my boots cleared it. It pushed its head under the bar and one front paw into the passage. Its tin tongue rang at my ankle.

I did what Wren would do. I kicked it. I kicked badly. My shoe came off in its canvas mouth.

Lysette had stopped on the other side to see if I had made it.

"You lost a shoe," she said.

"Yes."

"And your face." She held her clipboard up like a shield. She frowned at the spot above my eyebrow. "That's still open."

"I walked into a door. It will close."

"It's been nine days." She said it fast. Then she looked at the clipboard, as if the number might be written there. "It's Committee records. If it's infected, I'm the one who fills in the form."

"It's not infected."

"Fine." She dug in her sleeve. She held out a square of waxed cloth with a strip of tape across it. It was Committee issue. It had a stamp. "It's for the floor, Wren. You're bleeding on the floor."

I was not. I took it anyway and pressed it over the cut. It was warm from her sleeve. I had not expected that.

Ahead, the passage ended at a plain gray door with a brass ring. Above it hung the biggest glowstone I had seen in the Undercroft.

The exit.

* * *

We came out at eight minutes fifty-one. Okoru was waiting with the glass. He turned it over and looked at the sand. Then he looked at me.

"Third," he said. "Behind Lance One and the Marchmont Lance. You lose a minute for the hounds you didn't tag." He chewed. "At the second turn you went left. Your Trail said right. And then left was right. No one does that by feel."

"It was a guess," I said.

"Was it."

That was all he said. Okoru left his sentence hanging in the air for you to pick up. I did not pick it up.

Everyone was bent over or sitting. Rowan lay flat on the stone with his arms out.

"I can never move again," he said.

"You can," said Clara. "You'll just complain."

She sat beside him and looked at the ceiling. Lysette wrote with a very straight back. She had the face of someone who had found two things she was not ready to write down.

I lifted my head and looked up.

The finish hall had a gallery. A narrow iron balcony ran along the north wall, for the judges. Today it was empty except for one man. I knew his gray staff coat. The cloth was still stiff, with creases in the sleeves. He leaned on the rail with his arms crossed. He was not watching the board. He was not watching Valerius's Lance. He was not watching me.

He was watching Rowan.

His thumb lay along his sleeve. The nail was black. I had thought it was coal, a few weeks ago. But coal smears. This was a deep brown-black with a yellow edge. It was the stain you get from oak-bark liquor.

That was what Ysolde had written on the paper she gave me. *Tannery streets.*

I blinked. I did not mean to. Rowan laughed at something Felix said. He laughed so loud that it bounced off the roof. The man on the gallery stood up straight, as if the sound had told him what he wanted to know.

By the time I turned all the way around, the balcony was empty. The door at the end of it was swinging shut, slowly, on its own weight. It looked like a door that someone had closed with great care, so it would not slam.
