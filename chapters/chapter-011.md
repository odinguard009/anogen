# Nine Minutes of Somebody Else's Maze

"Who's carrying the slate?"

Nobody answered. Okoru waited, chewing a sliver of dried licorice root that he keeps in his cheek like a sailor and never shares. Then he held the slate out at the end of his one arm and said it again with his eyebrows.

"Hollen has the slate," said Felix.

"I have the Committee's clipboard," said Lysette. "That's different."

"Is it?"

"It has a clip."

Okoru dropped the slate into Clara's hands. She was the only one standing close enough to look like she'd take it. Clara looked at it the way she looks at most things, with no opinion at all, and tucked it under her arm.

We stood in the cold first gallery of the Undercroft, under the Bell Door. Four Lances had gone in that morning and come out the far side in different shapes. Lance One was on the board at six minutes forty, and nobody would beat that, because Lance One never leaves anything on the table. The star-readers had finished at nineteen. One had a burnt sleeve that the other had put out with a wet glove, and they were still arguing about whose fault it was.

"Graded field run," said Okoru. "Nine minutes on the glass. Every Lance gets a different maze, so don't ask the ones who finished. They don't know yours, and they'd lie anyway."

"What's in it?" said Rowan. He'd been bouncing on his heels since we came down.

"Walls that move. Some walls that aren't there. At the fourth minute the Kennel opens, and the Kennel is whatever the bursar could afford."

"What's the Kennel?"

"A lesson in budgeting." Okoru flipped the glass. "Tenets, yes. Relics, no. The exit is where the exit is. Go."

The Bell Door opened on a passage as wide as two men and the color of wet slate. Rowan was through it before the first sand finished falling.

* * *

Here is what I knew in the first forty steps. I knew it from the doorframe, before I even looked.

The lamps were wrong.

A glowstone hangs on a chain at every junction in the Undercroft. It's a green-white lump the size of a thumb, and each one throws a single shadow. A gate post, a bench edge, a boot. One shadow each.

Here every shadow had a second one, faint, lying under the first like a ghost. And all the faint ones leaned the same way, down the passage and off to the left.

That meant a bigger light, far ahead and very strong. Whoever built this maze had hung a harbor lamp over the exit and forgotten that light goes around corners.

I didn't say so. I was Wren. Wren sleeps. Wren has sixty-one absences and a lance for blocking.

"Dead end," Rowan called from the front.

It looked like one, with a rough wall, a gutter and a rusted ring in the stone. Lysette stepped up beside him and touched the wall with two fingers, and her eyes went pale while the rim of each iris lit up like the edge of a coin.

The wall remembered for her.

A ghost-light rose in the stone, a little gray figure in a leather apron. He pulled a painted canvas across the passage and dropped a bar to hold it. It took about a blink, and then it faded and Lysette lifted her hand.

"Canvas," she said. "Two bars, top and bottom. It's from this morning."

"You *saw* that?" said Rowan.

"I'm a Witness, Tallis. That's what the word means."

He kicked out the bottom bar and the canvas dropped flat, with more passage behind it. Clara stepped over it and said, to nobody in particular, "She's very good."

Lysette's ears went red above her collar. She wrote something on her clipboard. I'm sure it wasn't a mark.

I stayed at the back and watched the faint shadows.

At the second junction the passage split three ways, and Felix went down on one knee. His Trail is a quiet Tenet that doesn't glow. He just goes still, and his eyes go out of focus, like he's trying to hear one voice at a loud table. He pointed right.

"Boots. Heavy ones. Lots of them, going in and out. The floor's worn smooth."

"Worn means people walk it," said Lysette.

"People walk every maze to set it up. But this one got walked a hundred times." He frowned. "That's more than you'd need."

"So it's the right way," said Rowan. "Come on."

"Or it's bait," I said.

They all looked at me. I hate that.

"Left," I said. "I think. I'm guessing."

"You're guessing," said Lysette.

"My Tenet makes shadows do things. It's just a feeling." I shrugged with one shoulder. It's a good shrug. Apologetic. I've worn it for years. "Left feels deeper."

Felix looked at the left branch, then at me, half a second longer than he needed to. Then he stood.

"Left," he said, like a man who has decided to be wrong in good company.

It was a long way down the next corridor before I stopped watching the back of his neck.

* * *

At the fourth minute the Kennel opened. The bursar had bought what he could afford.

Five of them came out of the dark in a line. Dust-hounds, the board called them, with cane frames, canvas stretched over the ribs, sand in the belly for weight and a tin bell clapper for a tongue. They were cheap and quick and not funny at all.

One tin tongue rang. Then they all did, and the noise came down the passage like a sack of knives.

"Back!" Felix had his bow off before the second bell. "Everybody back!"

Okoru's rule was no relics. Tenets were fine, and so were plain weapons. Nobody told him to stop. He put an arrow through the first hound's front leg. It tripped over its own foot and slammed into the second. They piled up against the gutter, rattling, and sand poured out of one like stuffing from a split pillow.

"Three left," said Clara, flat as a bench.

They split around the pile. Two went for Felix. One went for Rowan.

"Get behind me," said Rowan, to everyone.

"Don't!" said Lysette. "You're in the open, you don't have a line, you're—"

He lit his hand.

It was Spark, not Dawn. A small gold flare, the size of a lantern, went straight up into the cane faces. Whatever the hounds saw with, it wasn't built for gold. The first two froze with their tin tongues clacking and swung their heads side to side, like the passage had changed shape. The third ran into the wall chasing its own shadow.

Rowan was grinning. Then he saw Felix's arm.

"Huh," said Felix.

A splintered cane rib had gone across his forearm. It wasn't deep, but the blood ran out in a thin line like ink from a pen. He looked at it with polite interest.

Clara was already there. She hadn't run; she'd been standing, and then she was next to him, holding his wrist in both small hands while her lips moved on words I couldn't hear. You don't watch that part. I watched his sleeve instead.

The edges of the cut came together as I looked, went pink, and sealed.

"Oh," said Felix. "That's strange." He sounded like she'd pulled a coin out of his ear.

For about four seconds, the five of us were a Lance. Rowan's light held the hounds. Felix reloaded. Lysette took two steps left and called the weak points.

"Bottom rib! It's tied there! Look, all four are tied there!"

Felix shot the knot on the nearest hound. It came apart in the middle of its stride. Then Rowan's hand started shaking, and the light flickered out. The next hound turned around.

"Run," I said.

I used the voice I keep for bad news in a warehouse. Nobody argued.

* * *

I'd always thought the worst part of being chased by a sandbag was the noise. It isn't. It's that they never stop, because they have no judgment to lose. A hound that has your footsteps keeps them until its sand runs out, around corners, down stairs, across a floor somebody just mopped.

We ran with the glass ticking behind us, and Rowan carried Clara over a gap she'd never have cleared on her own. Felix clipped a wall twice and swore in a language I didn't know he had.

"Was that Karsi?" Rowan panted.

"Shut up and run," said Felix.

Then, at a square junction under a nearly dead lamp, Lysette stopped so fast I almost ran into her. There were two passages, both lit and both empty, and behind us the tin tongues were close.

"Which?" she said.

Both shadows leaned left. The faint ones, the borrowed ones, all pointed the same way. I couldn't tell her why I was sure without telling her what I was.

"That one," I said. "Please."

The *please* did it. I don't say it much. She looked at me once, fast, and went.

The left passage shrank to a crawl under a low arch. A rusted portcullis hung halfway down. You had to go through on your stomach. Lysette went first, then Clara, then Rowan with his shoulders turned sideways. Felix went next and reached back through the gap.

"Hand!" he said.

I took it and let him haul.

The hound got there as my boots cleared the arch. It shoved its head under the bar and a front paw into the passage. Its tin tongue rang at my ankle.

I did what Wren would do. I kicked it. Badly. My shoe came off in its canvas jaw.

Lysette had stopped on the other side to see if I'd made it.

"You lost a shoe," she said.

"Yes."

"And your face." She held her clipboard up like a shield and frowned at the spot above my eyebrow. "That's still open."

"I walked into a door. It'll close."

"It's been nine days." She said it fast. Then she looked at the clipboard, like the number might be written on it. "It's Committee records. If it's infected, I'm the one who fills in the form."

"It's not infected."

"Fine." She dug in her sleeve and held out a square of waxed lint with a strip of tape across it. Committee issue. It had a stamp. "It's for the floor, Wren. You're bleeding on the floor."

I wasn't. I took it anyway and pressed it over the cut. It was warm from her sleeve. I hadn't expected that.

Ahead, the corridor ended at a plain gray door with a brass ring. Above it hung the biggest glowstone I'd seen in the Undercroft.

The exit.

* * *

We came out at eight minutes fifty-one. Okoru was waiting with the glass. He turned it over and looked at the sand. Then he looked at me.

"Third," he said. "Behind One and the Marchmont Lance. You lose a minute for the hounds you didn't tag." He chewed. "You went left at the second junction. Your Trail said right. And then left was right. Nobody does that by feel."

"It was a guess," I said.

"Was it."

That's all he said. Okoru has a way of leaving his sentence hanging in the air for you to pick up. I didn't pick it up.

Everyone was bent over or sitting. Rowan lay flat on the stone with his arms out.

"I can never move again," he announced.

"You can," said Clara. "You'll just complain."

She sat beside him and studied the ceiling. Lysette wrote very straight, with the face of someone who'd found two things she wasn't ready to put on paper.

I lifted my head and looked up.

The finish hall has a gallery. A narrow iron balcony runs along the north wall for examiners. Today it was empty except for one man. He wore a plain gray staff coat, a new one, with the fold creases still in the sleeves, and he leaned on the rail with his arms crossed. He wasn't watching the board or Valerius's Lance or me.

He was watching Rowan.

His thumb lay along his sleeve. The nail was black. I'd taken it for coal a fortnight ago. But coal smears, and this was grained, a deep brown-black with a yellow edge. It was the stain you get from oak-bark liquor.

That was what Ysolde had written on the slip she gave me. *Tannery streets.*

I blinked. I didn't mean to. Rowan laughed at something Felix said, loud enough to bounce off the vault. The man on the gallery straightened, as if the sound had told him what he wanted to know.

By the time I turned all the way around, the balcony was empty. The door at its end was swinging shut on its own weight, slowly. The way a door does when someone has been very careful not to slam it.
