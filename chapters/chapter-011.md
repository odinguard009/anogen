# Nine Minutes of Somebody Else's Maze

"Who's carrying the slate?"

Nobody answered. Okoru waited, chewing whatever he chews, a sliver of dried licorice root, I think, that he keeps in his cheek like a sailor and never offers around. Then he held the slate out at the end of his one arm and said it again with his eyebrows.

"Hollen has the slate," said Felix.

"I have the Committee's clipboard," said Lysette. "That's different."

"Is it?"

"It has a clip."

Okoru dropped the slate into Clara's hands, because she was the only one standing near enough to look as though she'd accept it, and Clara looked at it the way she looks at most things, with no particular opinion, and tucked it under her arm.

We were in the first gallery of the Undercroft, under the Bell Door, in the cold. Four Lances had gone in before us that morning and come out at the far side in various states. Lance One was posted on the board at six minutes forty, which nobody would beat, because Lance One never leaves anything on the table. The star-readers had come out at nineteen, one of them with his sleeve on fire, put out by the other with a wet glove, arguing about whose fault it was.

"Graded field run," Okoru said. "Nine minutes on the glass. A different maze for every Lance, so don't ask the people who finished. They don't know yours and they'll lie anyway. Walls move. Some of the walls aren't there. At the fourth minute the Kennel opens, and the Kennel is whatever the bursar could afford."

"What's in the Kennel?" said Rowan, who had been bouncing on his heels since we came down.

"A lesson in budgeting." He flipped the glass. "Tenets, yes. Relics, no. The exit is where the exit is. Go."

The Bell Door opened on a passage the width of two men and the color of wet slate, and Rowan was through it before the sand had finished its first slide.

* * *

Here is what I knew in the first forty paces, because I knew it from the doorframe and not because I'd looked: the passage lamps were all wrong.

A glowstone on a chain hangs at every junction in the Undercroft, a thumb-sized lump of green-white, and each of them throws one shadow. The post of a gate, the edge of a bench, a boot. One. Here every shadow had a second one, faint, lying under the first like a ghost of it, and the faint ones all leaned the same way, off to the left and down the passage. That meant a bigger light somewhere ahead, far and strong. Whoever set the maze had hung a lamp over the exit fit for a harbor and forgotten that light goes round corners.

I didn't say so. I was Wren, who sleeps, who has sixty-one absences and a lance for blocking.

"Dead end," said Rowan, from the front.

It looked like one. Rough wall, a gutter, a rusted ring in the stone. Lysette stepped up beside him and put two fingers to the wall without a word, and her eyes went the color they go, pale, with the rim of the iris lit like the edge of a coin.

The wall remembered for her. A ghost-light came up in it, a little gray figure in a leather apron, drawing a panel of painted canvas across the passage and dropping a bar to hold it. It happened in about the time it takes to blink, and then it played out, and Lysette took her hand away.

"Canvas. Two bars, top and bottom." She did not look at Clara. "I'd say it's from this morning."

"You *saw* that?" Rowan said.

"I'm a Witness, Tallis, it's what the word means."

He kicked the bottom bar loose, and the canvas fell flat, and behind it was more passage. Clara stepped over the canvas and said to nobody in particular, "She's very good," and Lysette's ears went red above her collar and she wrote something on her clipboard that I'm sure wasn't a mark.

I stayed at the back and watched the faint shadows.

At the second junction the passage split three ways, and Felix went down on one knee with his bow case on his back. His Trail is a quiet Tenet. It doesn't light up. He just goes still and his eyes lose their focus, as if the floor has started talking and he's trying to hear the one voice at the table. He pointed to the right-hand branch.

"Boots. Heavy. A lot of them, going in and out. The path's worn."

"Worn means somebody's walked it," said Lysette.

"Somebody walks every maze to set it. And somebody walked this one a hundred times." He frowned. "More times than you'd need."

"Then it's right," said Rowan. "Come on."

"Or it's bait," I said.

They all looked at me, which I hate.

"Left," I said. "I think. I'm guessing."

"You're guessing," said Lysette.

"My Tenet makes shadows do things. It's a feeling." I shrugged with one shoulder. It's a good shrug, an apologetic one, I've worn it for years. "Left feels deeper."

Felix looked at the left branch. He looked at me, for a half second longer than he needed. Then he stood and said, "Left," in the voice of a man who has decided to be wrong in company, and it was a long way into the next corridor before I let myself stop watching the back of his neck.

* * *

At the fourth minute the Kennel opened, and the bursar had indeed bought what he could afford.

They came out of the dark in a line, five of them. Dust-hounds, the board called them. Cane frames, canvas stretched over the ribs, sand in the belly for weight, a tin bell clapper for a tongue. Cheap, and quick on four legs, and not remotely amusing. One tin tongue rang. Then they all did, and the sound came down the passage at us like a sack of cutlery.

"Back!" Felix had the bow off his shoulder before the second one rang. Nobody told him not to. The Undercroft's rule is relics no, Tenets yes, and ordinary weapons the way they come. He put an arrow through the first one's front leg and the creature went over its own foot into the second. They piled into a heap against the gutter, rattling, with sand running out of one of them like a pierced ham.

Three left. They split around the heap. Two for Felix, one for Rowan.

"Get behind me," said Rowan, to everyone.

"Don't," said Lysette. "You don't have the line, you're standing in the open, you—"

He lit his hand.

Spark, not Dawn. A small gold flare, the size of a lantern, thrown straight up into the cane faces. Whatever the hounds saw with, it wasn't made for gold. The first two stopped dead, tin tongues clacking, and turned their heads from side to side as if the passage had changed shape. The third veered and ran on its own shadow into the wall.

Rowan was grinning. Then he saw the arm.

Felix's forearm, where the second hound had gone past him. Not deep. A long slash from a splintered rib of cane, and the blood came out of it in a line like ink from a pen. He said, "Huh," and looked at it with interest.

Clara was already there. She hadn't run; she'd been standing, and then she was beside him, and she took his wrist in both of her small hands without a word and her lips moved on something I couldn't hear. It's a thing you don't watch. I watched his sleeve. The edges of the cut came together as I looked, quietly, and pinked, and sealed, and Felix said, "Oh. That's strange," as if she'd found a coin in his ear.

For about four seconds the five of us were a Lance. Rowan's light held them, Felix reloaded, Lysette had two steps to the left and an eye on the frames, calling the joints. "Bottom rib, it's tied there, look, all four are tied there." Felix took the knot on the near one and it went to pieces in the middle of its stride. Then the light flickered and was gone, because Rowan's hand had begun to shake, and the nearest hound turned around.

"Run," I said.

I said it in the voice I use for bad news in a warehouse, and nobody argued.

* * *

I'd always assumed the worst thing about being hunted by a sandbag was the noise. It's not. It's that they stop for nothing. They have no judgment to give up; a hound that has your footfall keeps it until the sand runs out, round corners, down steps, across a floor that has just been wet.

We ran with the glass running behind us. Rowan carried Clara over a gap that I don't think she'd have cleared. Felix clipped a wall twice and cursed, quietly, in a language I didn't know he had. And then at a small square junction under a lamp that was almost dark, Lysette pulled up so suddenly that I nearly went into her. Two passages, both lit. Both empty. Behind us the tin tongues, close.

"Which?" she said.

Both shadows leaned left. Both. The faint ones, the borrowed ones, tilted the same way toward the passage to the left. I couldn't have told her why I was sure without telling her what I was.

"There," I said. "Please. That one."

The *please* did it. I don't say it much. She looked at me once, quick, and went.

The left passage narrowed to a crawl under a low arch with a rusted portcullis half down. You went through on your stomach. Lysette first, then Clara, Rowan with his shoulders turned, then Felix, who put his hand through the gap behind him for me, and I took it and let him haul. The dust-hound arrived as my boots cleared the arch. It got its head under the bar and a front paw into the passage, tin tongue ringing at my ankle, and I did what Wren would do. I kicked it. Badly. The shoe came off in its canvas jaw.

Lysette had stopped on the other side to see whether I'd made it.

"You've lost a shoe," she said.

"Yes."

"Your face." She had her clipboard in front of her like a shield, and she frowned into the dark above my eyebrow. "That's still open."

"I walked into a door. It'll close."

"It's been nine days." She said it fast, and then looked at the clipboard, as if the number might be written there. "Committee records. If it's infected I'm the one who has to fill in the form."

"It isn't."

"Fine." She dug in her sleeve and held out a square of waxed lint with a strip of tape across it. Committee issue; there's a stamp. I looked at it. She said, quite loudly, "It's for the floor, Wren, you're bleeding on the floor," which I wasn't, and I took it and pressed it over the cut, and it was warm from being in her sleeve. I hadn't expected it to be warm.

Ahead of us the corridor ended in a plain gray door with a brass ring, and over it hung the biggest glowstone I'd seen in the Undercroft. The exit.

* * *

We came out of the maze at eight minutes fifty-one. Okoru was waiting with the glass. He turned it over, and looked at the sand, and then at me.

"Third," he said. "Behind One and the Marchmont Lance. A minute off for the hounds you didn't tag." He chewed. "You went left at the second junction against a Trail that said right, and then you were right. Nobody does that by feel."

"It was a guess," I said.

"Was it." He looked at me. That's all. Another man might have said something more, but Okoru has a way of leaving his own sentence in the air for you to pick up. I didn't pick it up.

Everyone was bent over, or sitting, or in Rowan's case lying flat on the stone with his arms out, announcing that he could never move again. Clara sat beside him and looked at the ceiling. Lysette was writing, very straight, with a face that said she'd found two things in her list she wasn't willing to put on paper yet.

I lifted my head and looked up.

The finish hall has a gallery. A narrow iron balcony, running along the north wall for examiners to watch from, and it was empty except for one person. A man at the rail in a plain gray staff coat, a new one, with the fold-creases still in the sleeves. He had his arms crossed on the iron and he wasn't looking at the board, or at Valerius's Lance, or at me.

He was looking at Rowan.

His thumb lay along his sleeve, and it was black at the nail. I'd taken it for coal, a fortnight ago. Coal smears. This was grained, a deep brown-black with a yellow edge, the stain you get from oak-bark liquor. Which was what Ysolde had written on the slip she gave me. *Tannery streets.*

I blinked. I didn't mean to. Rowan laughed at something Felix said, loud enough to come off the vault, and the man on the gallery straightened as if the sound had told him what he wanted to know.

By the time I turned all the way round, the balcony was empty, and the door at its end was swinging shut on its own weight, slowly, the way a door does when someone has been very careful not to slam it.
