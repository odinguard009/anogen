# Perrin

A wheelbarrow is the best disguise on the plateau. Nobody has ever looked a person in the face over one.

I had pushed this one thirty yards across the stable yard, with a pitchfork laid on top and my hood down. A boy shifting dirty straw is somebody else's problem. That was the theory, and it was working. Two Committee juniors in yellow armbands went past with a list of names. The shorter one kept muttering, "Wren, Wren, Wren," like a man searching for his keys, and walked right by the barrow.

I tipped it out at the muck heap and leaned the fork on the wall. Then I went round the back of the tack room. The roof hangs over a stack of feed sacks there, and nobody goes near, because the smell is something you'd have to want.

Felix was sitting on a sack with his bow case across his knees and a napkin in his lap. He didn't look up.

"You're late," he said. "And you smell like a horse."

"The barrow's not mine."

"I know. It's Hob's. He's been hunting for it since breakfast." He moved over. "Sit."

I sat. It's the fourth place on the plateau where nobody has ever thought to look for me, and the only one where someone's waiting. Felix found it in Year Three and has never said how, and I've never asked. We eat here on the days we eat, under a slow brown rain of dust from the loft, with one yellow cat who has opinions about us.

He unfolded the napkin. Inside were two buns, the dark kind from the north ovens with caraway baked into the crust, still a little warm.

"Eaten today?" said Felix.

"Technically."

"Mm." He gave me the bigger one.

He wanted to start with the arm. He turned up his sleeve and showed me where Clara had closed the cut, and there was nothing there, not a line or a pink thread. He turned it over in the light twice, like a man checking a coin he'd found in his own pocket.

"I've had cuts that took a month," he said. "I've got one on my knee I'll have forever. Hers took about as long as a sneeze."

"Strong healer."

"Is that what you'd call it?" He ate a piece of bun. "Seraphine told me joke number four last night. A horse walks into an infirmary."

"Was it good?"

"The horse had a limp. That was the joke. The limp." He thought about it. "I laughed, because that's what you do. She wrote a four on her hand and took a bow. I think I committed some kind of fraud."

"You're keeping count."

"I'm told it's a scoring system." He picked a caraway seed off the crust and studied it. "Yesterday's maze. My Trail said right. A hundred boots said right. You said left because it felt deeper."

"It was a guess."

"I've been wrong in front of Okoru before. He's never looked at me like that on the way out." He flicked the seed at the cat. The cat ignored it. "I don't mind being wrong. What bugs me is this. My feet said right. You said left. And you won."

"Lucky, then."

"You're a lot of things. I've never once seen you lucky."

I had no answer to that. The next thing he said was worse.

The cat came and sat in the square of sun between us and stared at nothing. Far off, the lowchapel bell rang once for the half hour, and Hob started shouting about his barrow. It would have been a very good lunch.

Felix put down his bun. He did it the way a man puts down a drink before a toast.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You're going to."

"I tried to think of a way you wouldn't dodge. I couldn't. So." He looked at his hands, not at me. They're long, pale, careful hands, the kind that tie fishing flies. "Why won't you let anyone near you? And I don't just mean me. Anyone. Rowan's been trying since the first week. Food, questions, all his little tricks. And you keep stepping out from under him like he's an awning coming down."

"Felix."

"Let me finish. Lysette's got a timetable. I think you've been counting the days she's tried to catch you. I saw you counting." He was quiet for a second. "I've never asked you anything in three years. Have I?"

"No."

"Do you want to know why?"

I didn't answer. I don't think it was a real question.

"Because you never told me there was a locked door," said Felix, gently. "You just stood in front of it."

* * *

I didn't sleep that afternoon. I tried, which is worse.

I lay on the roof of the Old Observatory with my coat over my face, and the war came anyway, the way it does, at the edge of the blanket. There were banners somewhere, and a river the wrong color. A row of men in black armor stood three deep in a field. They'd been standing there a very long time, waiting for me to show up and say what for.

The Tutor was far off and busy in the half-dream, and he didn't look round. He never does at that hour. The Dragon did.

*You didn't answer the boy,* said Sepiroth. It was the dry voice he uses when he'd like to be thought above it.

*Leave it.*

*I heard the whole thing. Landlords hear things. Very nice young man. Practically a sheepdog. And you sat there with a bun in your hand for as long as it takes to boil an egg. I counted.*

*Leave it,* I said again. He did, which is rarer than you'd think. The afternoon slid into evening, and I never found a single thing to say.

I'd had a speech for this once. I was fourteen and new, with a list of rules written inside my wrist. Never be at the center of anything. Never be at the edge, either, because edges are where people look. Never let one person know three things about you.

Everyone I'd ever let close, I'd used up. The King had Ysolde, and Ysolde had a locked cabinet. I had the Duke and Greta and Bertram, and a house I couldn't even visit without a letter. A Thornmere scholarship boy isn't supposed to have a family. Every road to that house ran past that fact.

The speech didn't cover a boy with a bun.

I got up at dusk, stiff, with caraway still in my teeth. Under the apple tree, Lysette's garden had been watered. She'd come while I slept and gone again without a note. She doesn't leave notes.

* * *

I was at the sacks before him the next day.

He came round the corner carrying a cloth bundle and stopped when he saw me. He said nothing, which is Felix's greatest talent. He sat. He opened the cloth. Two buns.

"Eaten today?" he said.

"No."

He looked at me. I'd never said no before.

"I'm going to answer what you asked," I said. "It'll be a bad answer. Let me finish."

"All right."

"I can't give you mine." It came out flat, which I hadn't wanted. I started again and it came out flat again. "You asked why I don't let people close. I'd like to. That's the first half. The second half is that some things about me aren't safe for you to hold. Not because you'd tell. Because you'd have to *carry* them. I've watched what that does. It makes people careful, and then it makes them sad."

Felix looked at the cat. The cat had come back.

"I'm not going to tell you what," I said. "And I won't make up something that sounds close enough to count. That would be worse."

"That's it?"

"That's it. I'm sorry."

"Mm." He broke his bun in half and held out the bigger piece. "Here."

"Felix—"

"Perrin."

"What?"

"It's my middle name. Felix Perrin Ashgrove. It was my mother's grandfather's, and he was a very bad poet. Don't use it. I've never said it to anybody who wasn't giving me a blessing or a bill." He pushed the bread another inch. "You know the whole of mine now. I thought somebody should."

I took the bun. It was an ordinary bun, but my hand wasn't steady, and he pretended not to see. That's another of his talents.

In my line of work, a name isn't just a name. Someone who hands you the middle one has handed you the house, the key, and whatever's in the cellar. If I were the kind of man who lent things out, a rope like that could tie up half a battlefield. He didn't know that. He knew it was a poet and a bun.

"I can't give you mine back," I said.

"I know."

"I want to."

"I know that too." He ate. "It was never a trade."

The yard was very quiet. Even Hob had gone silent. I think the barrow had turned up near the well, in someone else's hands.

I ate the bun. I'm not sure what else I did.

It might have ended there, with the cat and the caraway and Felix not talking, and it would have been the best ending of anything in a year. It didn't. Round the corner of the tack room, at a brisk Committee walk, came Lysette Hollen, in full uniform, with her clipboard under one arm and two wrapped lunches in the other.

She stopped when she saw us. Felix lifted one hand off his knee, politely. I think that was his whole greeting.

"Wren," she said.

"Hollen."

"A stable boy told me where you are. Eleven days I've tried to work out where you eat, and on the eleventh I thought of *asking.*" She had two parcels. Brown paper, string, a smell of onions. She held one out toward me without looking at me. She was looking at a spot six inches above the cat. "It's Committee efficiency. If I know you're fed, I don't have to count you. It saves a form."

"Lysette—"

"You're a flight risk," she said.

She put the parcel on the sack beside me and tucked the other under her arm. That one was hers. So she'd planned to eat lunch too, just not with us. She moved the clipboard to the other arm, which took longer than it should have.

"There's a rota," she told the cat. "I've put you on it. Wherever you are."

Then she walked off round the corner with her back so straight it must have hurt.

Felix watched the corner for a while.

"Rota," he said.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"Mm," said Felix. He ate the last of his bun. And for the first time since Year Three, I saw him smile at something that wasn't a joke at all.
