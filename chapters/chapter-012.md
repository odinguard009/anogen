# Perrin

A wheelbarrow is the best disguise on the plateau. Nobody has ever looked a person in the face over one.

I had pushed this one thirty yards across the stable yard with a pitchfork laid on top and my hood down, on the theory that a boy who is shifting dirty straw belongs to someone else's problem. It was working. Two Committee juniors in yellow armbands went past with a clipboard between them and a list of names, and the shorter one said, "Wren, Wren, Wren," out loud, like a man searching for his keys, and walked right on by the barrow.

I tipped it out at the muck heap. I leaned the fork on the wall. Then I went round the back of the tack room, where the roof overhangs a stack of feed sacks and nobody goes, because the smell is something you'd have to want.

Felix was sitting on a sack with his bow case across his knees and a napkin in his lap, and he did not look up.

"You're late," he said. "And you smell like a horse."

"The barrow's not mine."

"I know. It's Hob's. He's been looking for it since breakfast." He moved over. "Sit."

I sat. This is the fourth place on the plateau where no one has ever thought to look for me, and the only one where somebody's been waiting. Felix found it in Year Three, and he has never once said how, and I have never once asked. We eat here on the days we eat. Under the eaves, a slow brown rain of dust from the loft, one yellow cat who has opinions about us.

He unfolded the napkin. Two buns, the dark kind from the north ovens with caraway baked into the crust, still faintly warm.

"Eaten today?" said Felix.

"Technically."

"Mm." He gave me the bigger one.

We did the arm first, since he wanted to; he turned his sleeve up and showed me the place where Clara had closed it, and there was nothing there, not a line, not a pink thread. He turned it over under the light twice, like a man examining a coin he'd found in his own pocket. "I've had cuts that took a month," he said. "I had a cut once that I still wear on my knee. Hers took about as long as sneezing."

"Strong healer."

"Mm. Is that what you'd call it?" He ate a piece of bun. "Seraphine told me joke number four last night. A horse walks into an infirmary."

"Was it good?"

"The horse had a limp. That was the joke. The limp." He thought. "I laughed, because it's a thing you do, and she wrote four on her hand and took a bow, and I'm fairly sure I've committed some kind of fraud."

"You've kept count."

"I'm told it's a scoring system." He picked a caraway seed off the crust and looked at it. "Yesterday's maze. My Trail said right, with a hundred boots to prove it. You said left because it felt deeper."

"It was a guess."

"I've been wrong in front of Okoru before. He's never looked at me like that on the way out." He flicked the seed to the cat, who ignored it. "I don't mind being wrong. It's being wrong at your suggestion and then seeing the suggestion turn out better than my feet that I'm still turning over."

"Lucky."

"You are a lot of things, but I've never once seen you lucky."

It was the first thing he said that I had no answer to, and I was almost grateful for the next one, because it was worse.

The cat came and sat in the rectangle of sun between us and looked at nothing. A long way off, the lowchapel bell rang once for the half hour, and Hob began shouting about his barrow. It would have been a very good lunch.

Felix put down his bun, the way a man puts down a drink at the start of a toast.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You're going to."

"I've been trying to think of a way to do it that you wouldn't dodge. I can't. So." He didn't look at me; he looked at his hands. He has long, pale, careful hands, the kind that tie flies. "Why won't you let anyone near you? And I don't mean me. I mean anybody. Rowan has been trying since the first week of term, with food, with questions, with all the little ways he has, and you keep stepping out from under him like he's an awning that's coming down. Lysette's got a timetable. You've counted the days she's tried to catch you, I think. I saw you counting." He was quiet. "I have never asked you anything in three years, have I."

"No."

"Do you want to know why?"

I didn't answer. I don't think it was a question.

"Because you never said you had a locked door," said Felix, quite gently. "You just acted like a person who didn't."

* * *

I did not sleep that afternoon. It's the one thing I can say for the afternoon.

I lay on the roof of the Old Observatory with my coat over my face, and the war came anyway, the way it does, at the edge of the blanket. Banners somewhere. A river the wrong color. A row of men in black armor standing three deep in a field who had been standing there a very long time, waiting for me to arrive and say what for. The Tutor, in the dream, was far off and busy and did not look round. He never does at that hour. The Dragon did.

*You didn't answer the boy,* said Sepiroth, in the dry voice he uses when he'd like to be thought above it.

*Leave it.*

*I heard the whole thing. Landlords hear things. A very nice young man, practically a hedge. And you sat there with a bun in your hand for as long as it takes to boil an egg. I counted.*

*Leave it,* I said again, and he left it, which is rarer than you'd think, and the afternoon went down into evening without me finding a single thing to say.

I'd had a speech, once, for this. Years ago, when I was fourteen and new and had a list of rules written on the inside of my wrist. Never be at the center of anything. Never be at the edge of it, either, because edges are where people look. Never let one person know three things about you. Everyone I'd ever let near, I'd used up. The King had Ysolde, and Ysolde had a locked cabinet, and I had the Duke and Greta and Bertram and a house I could not walk into without a letter, because every road to it ran past the one thing a Thornmere scholarship boy could never be seen to have, which was a family.

The speech didn't cover a boy with a bun.

I got up at dusk, stiff, with caraway still in my teeth. Under the apple tree Lysette's garden had been watered, which meant she'd come at some hour I'd slept through, and had gone again without leaving a note, the way she doesn't.

* * *

I was at the sacks before him next day.

He came round the corner of the tack room carrying a cloth bundle and stopped when he saw me. He didn't say anything, which is Felix's greatest talent. He sat. He opened the cloth. Two buns.

"Eaten today?" he said.

"No."

He looked at me. I had never said no before.

"I'm going to answer what you asked," I said, "and it's going to be a bad answer, and I'd like you to let me finish."

"All right."

"I can't give you mine." It came out flat, which I hadn't wanted, and I started again, and it came out flat again. "You asked why I don't let people close. I'd like to. That's the first half. The second half is that there are things about me that aren't safe for you to hold. Not because you'd tell. Because you'd have to *carry* them. I've watched that. It weighs on people. It makes them careful and then it makes them sad." I took a breath. "I'm not going to tell you what. And I'm not going to lie and make something up that sounds close enough to count. That would be worse."

Felix looked at the cat. The cat had come back.

"That's it?"

"That's it. I'm sorry."

"Mm." He broke his bun in half, and held out the bigger half. "Here."

"Felix—"

"Perrin."

"What?"

"It's my middle name. Felix Perrin Ashgrove. It was my mother's grandfather's, and he was a very bad poet. Don't use it. I've never said it to anybody who wasn't getting a blessing or a bill." He pushed the bread another inch. "You know the whole of mine. I thought somebody should."

I took the bun. It was an ordinary bun. My hand wasn't steady, and he pretended not to see that, which is another of his talents.

I hadn't known what it would do. In my line of work a name isn't just a name. A person who hands you the middle one has handed you the whole house, and the key to it, and whatever's in the cellar, and if I'd ever been the kind of man who lent things out, a rope like that could have tied up half a battlefield. He didn't know that. He knew it was a poet and a bun.

"I can't give you mine back," I said.

"I know."

"I want to."

"I know that too." He ate. "It was never a trade."

The yard was very quiet. Even Hob had gone silent, though I think that was only the barrow turning up, somewhere near the well, in someone else's hands.

I ate the bun. I'm not sure what else I did.

It might have ended there, with the cat and the caraway and Felix not talking, in about the best way anything has ended in a year. It didn't, because round the corner of the tack room, at a brisk Committee walk, in full uniform, with her clipboard under one arm and two wrapped lunches in the other, came Lysette Hollen.

She stopped when she saw us. Felix lifted one hand off his knee, politely. I think that was the whole of his greeting.

"Wren," she said.

"Hollen."

"I had your location from a stable boy. Eleven days I've been trying to work out where you eat, and on the eleventh I thought of *asking.*" She was holding the two parcels very straight in front of her. Brown paper, string, a smell of something with onions in it. She held one out in my direction without looking at me. She was looking at a spot about six inches above the cat. "Committee efficiency. If I know you're fed, I don't have to count you. It saves a form."

"Lysette—"

"You're a flight risk," she said.

She put the parcel on the sack beside me. She tucked the other one under her arm, and I realized that it was hers; she'd brought hers too. She went red from the collar up and said, to the cat, "There's a rota. I've put you on it. Wherever you are," and walked off round the corner of the tack room with her back so straight it must have hurt.

Felix watched the corner for a while.

"Rota," he said.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"Mm," said Felix, and ate the last of his bun, and for the first time since Year Three I saw him smile at something that wasn't a joke at all.
