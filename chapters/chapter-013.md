# The Eleventh Lamp

The Long Stair has four hundred and eleven steps and nineteen lamps. One man looks after them. He has done it for thirty years, and he has never been late. That is how I knew something was wrong. At the tenth bell, the eleventh lamp was dark.

I was lying flat on the wall above the landing, under a fig tree that hung out over it. It is the one place on the Stair where no one looks up. Below me, a man in a leather apron worked his way down the lamps with a long brass pole. He was not lighting them. The pole had a small cup on the end, to put out flames. He put it over each flame, one at a time. He was careful about it, like a man closing a row of eyes.

He was good at it. He had the shuffle, the stoop, and the way of touching his cap. His shadow lay along the steps ahead of him, long and friendly.

It did not breathe.

That is a small thing. Everyone's shadow goes up and down a little, if you have taught yourself to see it. His lay on the stone like a dropped coat.

I came down off the wall. My lance was already in my left hand. He turned with the pole. He spoke in the kind old voice of Lothar Pell.

"Evening, young master. Past curfew, ain't it?"

Then he swung the pole.

The cup was full of burning oil. He had saved one flame. I snapped the lance shut into the door-shield and took the hit there. The oil burst across the wood in a wet gold sheet. It ran down the grain and over the edge. It ran onto my left hand, where I held the ring. It got inside my glove.

I do not remember deciding to open the door at his feet. I remember the glove sticking to my skin. I remember the smell. Then a long gray thing with many joints sank through the step, as if the stone were water. It wore a lamplighter's apron. It went in up to the waist, with Lothar Pell's face still on the front of it.

"Young master," it said.

Candor does not need much light. One coal from the broken cup was still burning on the step. That was enough.

It was over in the time it takes to put out a candle. What was left was a coat, a pair of boots, a cap, a pole, and gray dust across four steps like spilled flour. I peeled off my glove. The black leather came away in one piece. It left skin on the inside. I looked at my palm. Then I put the glove in my pocket.

The cap on the step belonged to the real Lothar Pell. I left it where it lay.

Then I went up to the roof and was sick, quietly. After that, I slept.

* * *

"There's been a murder," said a girl in Year One.

She said it to the whole line outside the dining hall. Then she said it to the bursar's clerk. Then she twisted her hands in her sleeves and said it to the first Discipline Committee officer who came running. That was Lysette. She had a baton in her hand, and her hair was falling down on the left.

The girl's name was Mathilde Lindqvelle. She had come down the Stair for early chapel and stepped in something. She had screamed, and she had not really stopped for an hour.

I know all this because Lysette caught me by the sleeve at the end of the line. She put her other hand flat on my chest.

"You. You're with me."

"I haven't done anything."

"You have sixty-two absences. Your timetable puts you under a lamp on the Stair twice a week. That makes you my witness for who goes up and down it." She was already walking. She was fast, once she had decided. "Don't speak unless I ask. Hold the rope."

I had planned to go the other way. It was a good plan, too, with a hole in the dormitory wall and a pass for the infirmary. The hole was twenty yards behind me. I walked in the other direction, with the end of a rope in my left hand.

It hurt. My new glove was stiff and dry. I had put it on over a bandage made from a stolen napkin. The pain was thin and hot. I held the rope with my fingers and not my palm.

"New glove," said Lysette. She did not look at me.

"The other one wore out."

She said nothing. She made a note.

"Which hand do you write with?" she asked.

"The right."

"Then why do you hold the rope with your fingers?"

"It's a habit."

"It's a bad habit," said Lysette. She wrote that down too.

* * *

When we reached the eleventh lamp, a half-circle of Year Ones stood at the foot of the landing. Someone had tied a yellow flag to the post.

The Golden Seal had beaten the Committee there. A man stood over the gray dust in a good coat. Two juniors in armbands stood behind him. A bag lay open at his feet. He was writing in a notebook. He was calm, like a man who has found what he came for. He was tall, about forty, with a pale face and a neat, short beard. He did not look up. His voice sounded like a drawer closing.

"This is Academy ground," said Lysette. "It belongs to the Bronze Bell. Under the charter, any death on the plateau belongs to the Discipline Committee until the Headmistress lets it go."

"And you are?"

"Lysette Hollen. Vice-chair."

"Hollen." He looked up, very briefly. "Of course. I am Inspector Rochefort, Golden Seal, Lantern Ward. I am sorry for your family's trouble."

I felt her go stiff. You can feel that through a rope.

"The charter gives us one hour," she said.

"I know what it says. I also know that three nights ago, a duchy tax office was destroyed. Five days before that, a clerk vanished from a cellar. I am still digging in that cellar. And now an Academy lamplighter lies across four steps as grit." He closed the notebook. "I am not a patient man. I am a careful one. You have your hour. I will stand here. The bag stays open. You touch nothing. If your Witness finds something, you tell me first. Before anyone else."

"You know my Tenet."

"Everyone knows your Tenet. You used it on that Eschenbach boy on Tuesday. All of Lantern Ward has heard." He smiled, a small smile, not unkind. "You will be useful to me, Miss Hollen. I would rather you were willing."

I understood then. Rochefort wanted a Black Order he could arrest. He wanted a Hollen to tell him the Black Order did it. He had come to hear her say it.

I looked at my rope.

* * *

The best part of a plan is the moment you see it will not work.

Mine was simple. I would stand at the back with my hood up and my hand behind me. I would let the ghost-light show whatever it showed. I would slip away when the inspector started shouting. I had turned to go. I had got as far as my heel.

"Wren," said Lysette. She did not look around. "Hold the lamp."

She put it in my right hand. It was the glass of the eleventh lamp. Someone had unhooked it, and it was still warm.

"Closer. Hold it up."

I held it up. The light fell on the wall and on her hand. She laid her palm on the stone beside the post. Her eyes went pale.

Witness is not a show when you see it up close. People who have seen it in drills think it is a flash. It is not. It is slow, tight, ugly work. The hand shakes. The breath stops. The stone gives up what it carried in one long thread.

She was reading six hours back. Then five. The faint light of the Stair rose in the wall in ghost-gray. I saw the edge of the leaves and the stooped shadow of the old lamplighter. Then the shadow stopped in front of a lamp.

It went out. The snuffer came down. The next lamp went out.

I watched myself in her wall. I was not a face. I was only a dark upright shape coming down from the top of the stone. The old man's shape turned. There was a flare of orange, and it was gone fast. Then something tall and black and narrow stood where the lamplight should have been. One thin line of light ran along its edge. I knew it, because I had carried it for years.

A door. And in the middle of it, small and round, a ring.

Everything after that was gray and slow. A cloud of motion. A long thing sinking. A flat blue spark. Then nothing, only the Stair, the dark lamps, the night, and the leaves still moving. A figure in black went away up the steps. One hand was held carefully against its side.

Lysette let go of the wall.

Her face was white, with a bright patch of color high on each cheek. She did not look at the inspector. She turned to the half-circle of juniors. Then she turned, on purpose, to the one person near her who was not staring. It was me.

"Did you see?" she said.

"Yes."

"A figure in black. A door." She was breathing hard. "That's not a shadow trick. I know the Tenets. That's not an ordinary door." She said it as if she were reading it off the inside of her own head. Then she spoke in a different voice. "No one on this plateau opens a door that size."

There was a very small silence.

"Everything I have seen says murder," said Inspector Rochefort. He said it gently, like a man who had waited for permission.

"It's not murder," said a voice behind us.

Okoru had come down the Stair from the top. His sleeve was pinned across his chest, and his jaw worked on his licorice root. He carried nothing. The juniors stepped aside for him. He crouched by the edge of the dust, neat as a soldier. He put his one finger in it and rubbed it against his thumb.

"This was a Veil," he said. "I have shoveled a basin of this stuff off the Wall." He looked up at Rochefort. "The lamplighter's name was Pell. His wife has been looking for him since eleven last night. If I were you, I'd go and tell her myself. Before she reads it in someone's ledger."

Rochefort did not answer right away. He had a bag at his feet, and a notebook, and a half-circle of witnesses. For the first time, he looked a little stuck.

Lysette put her hand over her mouth for a second. Then she took it down.

"Okoru, did you see the replay? I can give you the Testimony. I can put it on a plate for you."

"Do it."

She did it right there. She knelt on the cold step with a clean glass plate from her bag. Her fingers moved over its face. The gray in the wall came away from the stone in tiny threads and settled in the glass. It took four minutes. She never let the plate tilt once.

Her breath was steady now. It had not been steady at the wall. She kept her eyes on the work so well that she might have been alone. No one said a word until she finished. The inspector did not. Okoru did not. Mathilde Lindqvelle did not. She was crying quietly in someone's coat.

I stood there with a lamp in my hand. I watched someone do, with great care, a thing I had never been able to do at all. She was the best I had ever seen.

If anyone on this plateau was ever going to find me, it would be her.

She stood up and gave Okoru the plate.

"There's your Veil," she said. "And that is whoever killed it."

"Hollen," said Rochefort.

"I know what it was. I know what it killed. I'm telling you he left a *door.*"

The bright color was back in her face. Her eyes were wet, and I do not think she knew about either one. She said it to the Stair, to Rochefort, and to Okoru. But the whole time, she was looking at the faint dark mark on the wall where there had been a ring.

"The Black Order was here," she said. "I'll find out who opens the doors."
