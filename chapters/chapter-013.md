# The Eleventh Lamp

The Long Stair has four hundred and eleven steps and nineteen lamps, and the lamplighter who tends them has done it for thirty years and has never once been late. That was how I knew it was wrong. At the tenth bell the eleventh lamp was dark.

I was on the wall above the landing, flat, under the shade of an overhanging fig, because it's the one place on the Stair where nobody looks up. Below me a man in a leather apron was working his way down the lamps with a long brass pole. Not lighting them. The pole had a little cup on the end, a snuffer, and he put it over each flame in turn with the care of a man closing a row of eyes.

He was good at it. He had the shuffle, the stoop, the way of touching his cap. His shadow lay along the steps in front of him, long and agreeable.

His shadow didn't breathe. It's a small thing. Everyone's shadow rises and falls a little, if you've trained yourself to see it. His lay on the stone like a dropped coat.

I came down off the wall with my lance already folded in my left hand, and he turned with the snuffer, and said, in the kind old voice of Lothar Pell, "Evening, young master. Past curfew, ain't it?"

Then he swung the pole.

The cup was full of lit oil. He had been saving one flame. I took it on the shield, and it came apart across the door in a wet gold sheet that ran down the grain and over the edge, and over my left hand where it held the ring. It got into the glove. I don't remember deciding to open the door at his feet. I remember the glove sticking to me, and the smell, and then the long grey many-jointed thing in the lamplighter's apron going down through the step as if the stone were water, up to the waist, with Lothar Pell's face still on the front of it, and still saying, "Young master."

Candor does not need much light. A coal from the spilled snuffer, still burning on the step, was plenty.

It was done in the time it takes to put out a candle. What was left was a coat, a pair of boots, a cap, a pole, and the grey dust lying across four steps like spilled flour. I peeled my glove off in one piece of black leather and left skin on the inside, and looked at my palm, and put the glove in my pocket. The cap on the step was the real Lothar Pell's. I left it where it lay.

Then I went up to the roof to be sick, and did, quietly, and afterward I slept.

* * *

"There's been a murder," said a girl in Year One.

She said it to the whole of the refectory queue, and then to the bursar's clerk, and then, with her hands twisting in her sleeves, to the first officer of the Discipline Committee who came running. That was Lysette, with her baton in her hand and her hair coming down at the left. The girl's name was Mathilde Lindqvelle. She'd come down the Stair for early chapel and she had stepped in something, and had screamed a very great scream, and not stopped for an hour.

I know all this because Lysette took me by the sleeve at the bottom of the queue, put the other hand flat on my chest, and said, "You. You're with me."

"I haven't done anything."

"You've done sixty-two absences. Your timetable puts you under a lamp on the Stair at least twice a week, and you're my witness to who goes up and down it." She was already walking. She's quick when she's decided. "Don't speak unless I ask. Hold the rope."

I had been planning to go the other way. I had a good plan, as it happened, with a hole in the dormitory wall and a pass for the infirmary. The hole was twenty yards behind me and I walked the other direction with the end of a cordon rope in my left hand.

It hurt. The new glove was stiff and bone-dry and I'd put it on over a burn dressing I'd made myself from a stolen napkin. It hurt in a thin hot way, and I held the rope with the fingers and not the palm.

"New glove," said Lysette, not looking.

"The other wore out."

She didn't say anything. She made a note.

* * *

By the time we reached the eleventh lamp, a half-circle of Year Ones had formed at the foot of the landing, and the lamp itself had a yellow pennant tied around the post.

The Golden Seal had been quicker than the Committee. There was a man standing over the grey dust in a good coat, with two juniors in armbands behind him and a bag open at his feet, and he was writing in his notebook with the unhurried air of someone who has found what he came to find. Tall, fortyish, with a pale face and a trim, careful beard. He didn't look up. He had a voice like a closing drawer.

"Academy grounds," said Lysette. "This is the Bronze Bell's jurisdiction. Under the charter, any death on the plateau is a matter for the Discipline Committee until the Headmistress releases it."

"And you are?"

"Lysette Hollen. Vice-chair."

"Hollen." He looked up. It was a very short look. "Of course. Inspector Rochefort, Golden Seal, Lantern Ward. I'm sorry for your family's trouble."

I felt her stiffen. It is possible to feel that through a rope.

"The charter," she said, "says one hour."

"I'm aware of what it says. I'm also aware that three nights ago a duchy tax office was destroyed, and that five days before that a clerk disappeared from a cellar I'm still digging in, and that a lamplighter of the Academy is now apparently lying across four steps in the form of grit." He closed the notebook. "I'm not a patient man. I'm a methodical one. You have your hour. I'll stand here, and the bag stays open, and you may touch nothing. If your Witness finds something you will tell me what it is, before anyone else."

"You know my Tenet."

"Everyone knows your Tenet. You used it on that Eschenbach boy on Tuesday; the whole of Lantern Ward's heard." He smiled, small and not unkind. "You'll be useful to me, Miss Hollen. I'd rather you were willing."

I realized then that Rochefort wanted a Black Order he could arrest, and a Hollen to tell him the Black Order did it. He'd come to hear her say it.

I looked at my rope.

* * *

The best part of a plan is the moment you see that it was never going to work. Mine had been to stand at the back with my hood up and my hand behind me, let the ghost-light show whatever it was going to show, and slip off in the confusion when the inspector started shouting. I turned to go. I'd got as far as my heel. Lysette said, without looking round, "Wren, hold the lamp," and put it in my right hand.

It was the eleventh lamp's own glass. Someone had unhooked it. It was still warm.

"Closer. Hold it up."

I held it up. The light from it fell on the wall and on her hand, and she laid her palm on the stone beside the post, and her eyes went pale.

Witness is not a spectacle, up close. People who've seen it in the drill think it's a flash. It's a slow, tight, dreadful kind of work: the hand shakes slightly, the breath stops, and the stone gives up what it's been carrying in a long continuous thread. She was reading six hours back. Five. The stair's faint light came up in the wall in ghost-gray, a fringe of leaves, the old lamplighter's shadow with its stoop. Then the shadow stopped, in the replay, in front of a lamp.

It went out. The snuffer came down. The next went out. I watched myself in her wall: not a face, only a dark upright shape on the top of the stone, coming down. The old man's shape turned. A flare of orange, quickly gone. Then, on the wall where lamplight ought to have been, something tall and black and narrow stood up, edged by one thin line of light along one side, and I knew it, because I'd carried it for years.

A door. In the middle of it, small and round, a ring.

Everything after that was grey and slow. There was a cloud of motion, a long thing sinking, a flat blue spark. Then nothing: the stair, the lamps unlit, the night, the leaves going on, and a figure in black going away up the steps with one hand held rather carefully against its side.

Lysette let go of the wall.

Her face was white, with a high color at the top of each cheek. She did not look at the inspector. She turned to the half-circle of juniors, and then, quite deliberately, to the one person near her who wasn't staring: me.

"Did you see?" she said.

"Yes."

"A figure in black. A door." She was breathing hard. "That shape isn't a shadow-shaping. I've seen the Tenets. That's not an ordinary door." She said it as if she were reading it off the inside of her own head. Then, in a different voice: "Nobody on this plateau opens a door that size."

There was a very small silence.

"Everything I've seen says murder," said Inspector Rochefort, and he said it gently, as a man says something he's been waiting to be allowed to say.

"It's not murder," said a voice behind us.

Okoru had come down the Stair from the top, with his sleeve pinned across his chest and his jaw working on its root. He was carrying nothing. He walked into the circle of juniors and they parted for him, and he crouched, with a soldier's economy, by the edge of the dust, and put his one finger into it, and rubbed it against his thumb.

"This was a Veil," he said. "I've shoveled a basin of this stuff off the Wall." He looked up at Rochefort. "The lamplighter's real name was Pell. His wife's been looking for him since eleven last night. I'd go and tell her myself, if I were you, rather than wait to count her in a ledger."

Rochefort didn't answer at once. For a man with a bag at his feet he looked, for the first time, a little fixed in place.

"Did you see the replay?" said Lysette to Okoru. She had her hand against her mouth for a second and she took it down. "I can give you the Testimony. I can lay it on a plate."

"Do."

She did it there, kneeling on the cold step with a clean glass plate from her satchel, her fingers moving over its face, the grey of the wall coming away from the stone in tiny threads and settling in the glass. It took her four minutes. She never once let the plate tilt. Her breath was steady, which it hadn't been on the wall, and she kept her eyes on the work so completely that she might have been alone, and nobody in the circle, not the inspector, not Okoru, not Mathilde Lindqvelle weeping quietly in someone's coat, said a word until she'd finished. I stood there with a lamp in my hand and watched somebody do, with great care, a thing I had never in my life been able to do at all. It was extraordinarily good.

If anyone on this plateau was ever going to find me, it would be her.

She stood and gave Okoru the plate.

"There's your Veil," she said. "And that's whoever killed it."

"Hollen—"

"I know what it was. I know what it killed. I'm saying he left a *door.*" She had the high color in her face again, and her eyes were wet, and I don't think she knew either of those things. She said it to the Stair, to Rochefort, and to Okoru, but she was looking, the whole time, at the faint dark mark on the wall where there'd been a ring. "The Black Order was here. I'll find out who opens the doors."
