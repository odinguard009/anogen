# The Eleventh Lamp

The Long Stair has four hundred and eleven steps and nineteen lamps. The man who tends them has done it for thirty years and has never once been late. That's how I knew something was wrong. At the tenth bell, the eleventh lamp was dark.

I was lying flat on the wall above the landing, under an overhanging fig. It's the one place on the Stair where nobody looks up. Below me a man in a leather apron was working his way down the lamps with a long brass pole. He wasn't lighting them. The pole had a little cup on the end, a snuffer, and he put it over each flame in turn. He did it carefully, like a man closing a row of eyes.

He was good at it. He had the shuffle, the stoop, the way of touching his cap. His shadow lay along the steps ahead of him, long and friendly.

It didn't breathe.

That's a small thing. Everyone's shadow rises and falls a little, if you've trained yourself to see it. His lay on the stone like a dropped coat.

I came down off the wall with my lance already folded in my left hand. He turned with the snuffer and spoke in the kind old voice of Lothar Pell.

"Evening, young master. Past curfew, ain't it?"

Then he swung the pole.

The cup was full of lit oil. He'd been saving one flame. I snapped the lance shut into the door-shield and took it there. The oil burst across the wood in a wet gold sheet. It ran down the grain, over the edge, and onto my left hand where it held the ring. It got inside the glove.

I don't remember deciding to open the door at his feet. I remember the glove sticking to my skin, and the smell. Then a long gray many-jointed thing in a lamplighter's apron sank through the step like the stone was water. It went in up to the waist, with Lothar Pell's face still on the front of it.

"Young master," it said.

Candor doesn't need much light. A coal from the spilled snuffer, still burning on the step, was plenty.

It was over in the time it takes to put out a candle. What was left was a coat, a pair of boots, a cap, a pole, and gray dust spread across four steps like spilled flour. I peeled off my glove. The black leather came away in one piece and left skin on the inside. I looked at my palm. Then I put the glove in my pocket.

The cap on the step belonged to the real Lothar Pell. I left it where it lay.

Then I went up to the roof and was sick, quietly. Afterward I slept.

* * *

"There's been a murder," said a girl in Year One.

She said it to the whole refectory queue. Then she said it to the bursar's clerk. Then, with her hands twisting in her sleeves, she said it to the first Discipline Committee officer who came running. That was Lysette, baton in hand, with her hair coming down on the left.

The girl's name was Mathilde Lindqvelle. She'd come down the Stair for early chapel and stepped in something. She'd screamed, and she hadn't really stopped for an hour.

I know all this because Lysette caught me by the sleeve at the end of the queue. She put her other hand flat on my chest.

"You. You're with me."

"I haven't done anything."

"You've got sixty-two absences. Your timetable puts you under a lamp on the Stair twice a week. That makes you my witness for who goes up and down it." She was already walking. She's fast once she's decided. "Don't speak unless I ask. Hold the rope."

I'd been planning to go the other way. It was a good plan, too, with a hole in the dormitory wall and a pass for the infirmary. The hole was twenty yards behind me. I walked the other direction with the end of a cordon rope in my left hand.

It hurt. The new glove was stiff and dry, and I'd put it on over a burn dressing I'd made from a stolen napkin. It was a thin, hot kind of hurt. I held the rope with my fingers and not my palm.

"New glove," said Lysette, not looking.

"The other wore out."

She said nothing. She made a note.

* * *

By the time we reached the eleventh lamp, a half-circle of Year Ones had formed at the foot of the landing. Someone had tied a yellow pennant to the post.

The Golden Seal had beaten the Committee there. A man stood over the gray dust in a good coat, with two juniors in armbands behind him and a bag open at his feet. He was writing in a notebook, calm, like someone who has found what he came for. He was tall, about forty, with a pale face and a neat, careful beard. He didn't look up. His voice sounded like a drawer closing.

"This is Academy grounds," said Lysette. "It's the Bronze Bell's jurisdiction. Under the charter, any death on the plateau belongs to the Discipline Committee until the Headmistress releases it."

"And you are?"

"Lysette Hollen. Vice-chair."

"Hollen." He looked up, very briefly. "Of course. Inspector Rochefort, Golden Seal, Lantern Ward. I'm sorry for your family's trouble."

I felt her stiffen. You can feel that through a rope.

"The charter says one hour," she said.

"I know what it says. I also know that three nights ago a duchy tax office was destroyed. Five days before that, a clerk vanished from a cellar I'm still digging in. And now an Academy lamplighter is lying across four steps in the form of grit." He closed the notebook. "I'm not a patient man. I'm a methodical one. You have your hour. I'll stand here, the bag stays open, and you touch nothing. If your Witness finds something, you tell me first. Before anyone else."

"You know my Tenet."

"Everyone knows your Tenet. You used it on that Eschenbach boy on Tuesday. All of Lantern Ward's heard." He smiled, small and not unkind. "You'll be useful to me, Miss Hollen. I'd rather you were willing."

I understood then. Rochefort wanted a Black Order he could arrest. He wanted a Hollen to tell him the Black Order did it. He'd come to hear her say it.

I looked at my rope.

* * *

The best part of a plan is the moment you see it was never going to work.

Mine was simple. Stand at the back with my hood up and my hand behind me. Let the ghost-light show whatever it was going to show. Slip away when the inspector started shouting. I'd turned to go. I'd gotten as far as my heel.

"Wren," said Lysette, without looking round. "Hold the lamp."

She put it in my right hand. It was the eleventh lamp's own glass. Someone had unhooked it, and it was still warm.

"Closer. Hold it up."

I held it up. The light fell on the wall and on her hand. She laid her palm on the stone beside the post, and her eyes went pale.

Witness isn't a show up close. People who've seen it in drills think it's a flash. It isn't. It's slow, tight, ugly work. The hand shakes. The breath stops. The stone gives up what it's carried in one long thread.

She was reading six hours back. Five. The Stair's faint light rose in the wall in ghost-gray, with a fringe of leaves and the old lamplighter's stooped shadow. Then the shadow stopped in front of a lamp.

It went out. The snuffer came down. The next lamp went out.

I watched myself in her wall. I wasn't a face, only a dark upright shape coming down off the top of the stone. The old man's shape turned. There was a flare of orange, gone fast. Then something tall and black and narrow stood up where the lamplight should have been. One thin line of light edged its side. I knew it, because I'd carried it for years.

A door. And in the middle of it, small and round, a ring.

Everything after that was gray and slow. A cloud of motion. A long thing sinking. A flat blue spark. Then nothing, only the Stair, the unlit lamps, the night, and the leaves going on. A figure in black went away up the steps with one hand held carefully against its side.

Lysette let go of the wall.

Her face was white, with a high color at the top of each cheek. She didn't look at the inspector. She turned to the half-circle of juniors. Then, on purpose, she turned to the one person near her who wasn't staring. Me.

"Did you see?" she said.

"Yes."

"A figure in black. A door." She was breathing hard. "That's not a shadow-shaping. I know the Tenets. That's not an ordinary door." She said it like she was reading it off the inside of her own head. Then, in a different voice, she said, "Nobody on this plateau opens a door that size."

There was a very small silence.

"Everything I've seen says murder," said Inspector Rochefort. He said it gently, like a man who'd been waiting to be allowed.

"It's not murder," said a voice behind us.

Okoru had come down the Stair from the top, his sleeve pinned across his chest, his jaw working on its root. He carried nothing. The juniors parted for him. He crouched by the edge of the dust, soldier-neat, put his one finger into it, and rubbed it against his thumb.

"This was a Veil," he said. "I've shoveled a basin of this stuff off the Wall." He looked up at Rochefort. "The lamplighter's name was Pell. His wife's been looking for him since eleven last night. If I were you, I'd go tell her myself. Before she reads it in somebody's ledger."

Rochefort didn't answer right away. For a man with a bag at his feet, he looked, for the first time, a little stuck.

Lysette put her hand over her mouth for a second, then took it down.

"Did you see the replay, Okoru? I can give you the Testimony. I can lay it on a plate."

"Do it."

She did it right there. She knelt on the cold step with a clean glass plate from her satchel. Her fingers moved over its face. The gray in the wall came away from the stone in tiny threads and settled in the glass. It took four minutes. She never let the plate tilt once.

Her breath was steady now, which it hadn't been at the wall. She kept her eyes on the work so completely that she might have been alone. Nobody said a word until she finished. Not the inspector, not Okoru, not Mathilde Lindqvelle, who was crying quietly in someone's coat.

I stood there with a lamp in my hand. I watched someone do, with great care, a thing I'd never been able to do at all. She was the best I'd ever seen.

If anyone on this plateau was ever going to find me, it would be her.

She stood and gave Okoru the plate.

"There's your Veil," she said. "And that's whoever killed it."

"Hollen—" said Rochefort.

"I know what it was. I know what it killed. I'm saying he left a *door.*"

The high color was back in her face. Her eyes were wet, and I don't think she knew either of those things. She said it to the Stair, to Rochefort, and to Okoru. But the whole time, she was looking at the faint dark mark on the wall where there'd been a ring.

"The Black Order was here," she said. "I'll find out who opens the doors."
