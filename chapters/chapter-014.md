# Seven for Seven

> "An absence is a debt. Debts are collected." - Discipline Committee motto, unofficial

"That's the fourth runner," said Felix, from the apple tree.

I didn't open my eyes. I lay on my back in the thyme at the far end of the roof, with my coat folded under my head and my boots crossed. It was the ninety-first minute of what would be, if nobody ruined it, my seventh nap in seven days. I could hear him fine. Felix has the voice of a man reading out a very short menu.

"Where?"

"Bell tower. He's on the roof with a spyglass. He's looking at the dormitory."

"Good."

"He has a little flag. It says *Committee.*" A pause, and a rustle of leaves. "I think he's been told to wave it at someone."

"Good."

"I'm going to stop reporting these if you only say *good.*"

I took that as encouragement and went on not opening my eyes.

Nobody tells you a perfect record is this tiring. On day one I'd lain down at noon under a table in the library annex, with a sign on my chest that said *Studying*. Lysette walked in at twelve-oh-six with a Committee runner on each arm and a warrant. On day two the warrant became two warrants. On day three there was a wire across the gap in the boiler-room wall. I had to start taking notes. It's the only time in my life I've been grateful to a ledger. *Nap 3. Boiler room. Thirty-eight minutes. Undisturbed, apart from a bucket.* Signed *E. Wren*, and witnessed, for want of anyone else, by an apple tree.

Six in a row. If I got through this one, I'd be the first person in the history of the Royal Academy to train for sleeping. It's not on the Ladder. There's no pin for it.

I'd also bet Felix a month of carrying his Strategy book. I'd rather not go into that.

"Seraphine's joke," said Felix. "Number nineteen. Want it?"

"No."

"A man goes to a baker and says he'd like a loaf. The baker says, 'I'll see what I can rise to.'" He thought about it. "I laughed. Now I know I'm not supposed to laugh until I mean it. I couldn't find a way to say that without sounding like I was complaining, so I told her I *chuckled.* She wrote nineteen on her hand and drew a little box round it."

"You're a double agent."

"I'm a man with a lunch arrangement." He didn't sound angry. "Two lunches go to the roof door at noon, in a basket on a hook. One's for you. I don't know who the other's for, and I've been told not to ask. In return, I tell the Committee you're probably at the library. That's a lie. And I tell you the Committee's probably at the library. That one isn't. It's a good deal."

"For who?"

"Everybody who wants to eat."

I opened my eyes a slit and looked up through the apple leaves. The roof of the Old Observatory is mostly sky. The dome sits at one end, scabbed with old copper, with the broken telescope sticking out of it like a bored finger. The herb beds make a rough cross of raised planks around the apple tree. It's a dwarf apple, bent by wind, with maybe nine apples, which Lysette had told someone she was saving for jam. Felix had one foot on a branch and his back to the trunk. The sky was hard and pale, with one cloud in it. It was a very good day for sleeping.

"Fifth runner," said Felix.

I shut my eyes.

"Count the week with me," I said.

"Do I have to?"

"Day four. The dormitory."

"A Year Two under your bed for six hours," said Felix. "With a whistle."

"Day five, a cellar. Day six, the boiler room again, and a runner on the Long Stair with a coil of *rope.* Nobody ever explained the rope."

"She has seven officers on paper," said Felix. "One in practice. She's throwing that one at the whole plateau."

I'd been over the week in my head, the way you go over a bad bridge. Felix's lunches, next to all that, were a mercy. I'd stopped asking what he told her. It had only occurred to me this morning that I'd also stopped asking why she'd never once asked him about the roof.

And in seven days, not one Committee runner had climbed the roof stair.

I lay there and let that sit on me.

It's a narrow stair, forty-one steps up the east side of the Old Observatory, behind a door with a rusty latch. The only people who've ever used it are a headmistress with a bad eye, a girl who cries, and me. The door had a notice on it. On day one I'd walked past and read it without reading it, the way you do. *CLOSED FOR REPAIR. Masonry. By order.* It was in a square, tidy hand. The ink was the color of dried blood, which is what the Committee's inkpots are filled with. It was the same ink and the same hand as warrants one through nine.

I'd taken the notice for the bursar's. The bursar never repairs anything.

"Felix," I said.

"Mm."

"The sign on the stair."

"Which sign?"

"The one that says the roof's being repaired."

"I didn't think there was one." He was quiet a moment. "I'd have said the roof was in excellent repair. Apart from the telescope."

"It's been there since day one. I thought she was hunting me. The warrants, the runners, the whole campaign." I opened both eyes and looked at the sky. "She's been keeping them off the stair."

"Wren," said Felix, slowly, "that's either the nicest thing anyone's done for you, or the most organized trap in history."

"It can be both."

"Ah," said Felix.

"For seven days."

"Ah," said Felix again, differently. Then, much more quietly, "Eden. Somebody's on the stair."

"Don't move," I whispered. "Don't breathe. Be a tree."

"I'm already a tree," he breathed back. "I'm a very good tree."

I was already still. It's the first thing my body does. I slowed my breath and made my face the shape of a sleeping face. I heard the latch lift on the old iron. I heard the hinge say what it always says. Then there was nothing, for as long as a person takes to stand on a threshold and not decide.

Then light, slow footsteps on the planks.

Felix said nothing at all. The tree said nothing. I think the cloud went away. I heard her go past the dome. I heard the planks of the first bed. And I heard, not a yard from my head, the clunk of the little tin watering can. I'd knocked it over three weeks ago and set it back carefully, a thumb's width from where she keeps it.

She stood over me. I could tell by the shadow, which turned the red behind my eyelids a dull brown.

I'd had the right line ready for a month. A little apology, half a joke. *I was only resting here, I didn't know it was yours.* It's a good line. I'd even rehearsed the surprise.

"You're in the rosemary," said Lysette.

It wasn't what I expected. She said it very low, hardly voiced, down to the thyme. I'd been lying beside the rosemary all week. My left hand rested in the herb bed with one finger against a wooden plant label. It had been snapped in half and had a year of weather on it. Somebody had mended it with a twist of copper wire and a short stick. Three weeks ago, and nobody had asked me to.

She was looking at the label, I thought. Then at my hand. I did not open my eyes.

The silence went on. I heard her crouch. I heard a fingernail tick once against the copper wire. Then I heard the can nudged that thumb's width back to where it belonged.

"Seven days," she said, to no one. "I sent runners to the boiler room *twice.*"

I counted the leaves above me. I felt the apple tree lean in over us, politely, with Felix not breathing in it. I thought, if she says my name, I'll have to answer. Then my record is spoiled, and I carry Felix's Strategy book for a month, and probably a lot more.

She didn't say my name.

Something heavy and warm came down over my shoulders. It was a coat, her coat, dark wool, still warm from her. The lining was Committee yellow. It smelled of ink and chalk and, faintly, mint. She tucked the collar in around my neck. She did it fast, the way you do something you wish you weren't doing. Then I heard the planks going off toward the dome.

The can scraped. She was watering the beds, left to right, like someone who could do it in the dark. I lay under her coat with my eyes shut and my heart making a noise I hoped the soil would soak up. I counted nine sprinkles. Then a pause. Then the sound of paper.

I slitted one eye. I'd earned it.

She knelt at the end of the herb bed in her shirt, with her sleeves rolled up and her back to me. She held a folded sheet with a stamp at the top. It was today's warrant. It had a number and a clause and a signature. I could see from where I lay that every line was filled in, ready to go, in a hand that didn't shake.

She tore it across. Then across again. Four pieces. She held them for a moment in her flat palm, as if she were weighing them.

Then she pushed each piece into the soil with her thumb, one at a time, in a row, the way you plant seed.

She wiped her hand on her skirt. She sat on the edge of the herb bed with her knees up, her back to me, her face to the dome and the sky and the long gold flat of Solmarch sloping down toward the river. And that was all. She didn't go. She stayed there with her chin on her arms and the little tin can beside her and the whole bright noon around her. She looked like a person who'd come to sit with something and decided it would do.

After a while she spoke to the dome.

"Three weeks."

I didn't move.

"And you never told anyone." It wasn't a question. I'm not a fool, so I didn't answer it.

Behind me in the tree, very softly, Felix shifted his weight onto his other foot.

I stayed where I was. I was warm. It was the best nap of my life, and I'm entering it in the book, and not one word of why.
