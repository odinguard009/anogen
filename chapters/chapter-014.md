# Seven for Seven

> "An absence is a debt. Debts are collected." - Discipline Committee motto, unofficial

"That's the fourth runner," said Felix, from the apple tree.

I didn't open my eyes. I was on my back in the thyme at the far end of the roof, with my coat folded under my head and my boots crossed, in the ninety-first minute of what would be, if nobody ruined it, my seventh nap in seven days. I could hear him fine. Felix has the voice of a man reading out a very short menu.

"Where?"

"Bell tower. He's on the roof of it, with a spyglass. He's looking at the dormitory."

"Good."

"He has a banner. A little flag. It says *Committee.*" A pause, and a rustle of leaves. "I think he's been told to wave it at someone."

"Good."

"I'm going to stop reporting these if you only say *good.*"

I took that as encouragement, and went on not opening my eyes.

The thing about a perfect record is that nobody tells you it'll be this tiring. Day one I'd gone down at noon in the library annex under a table, with a sign on my chest, *Studying*, and Lysette had walked in at twelve-oh-six with a Committee runner on each arm and a warrant. Day two the warrant became two warrants. Day three there'd been a wire across the gap in the boiler-room wall. I'd had to start taking notes. It's the only time in my life I've been grateful to a ledger: *Nap 3. Boiler room. Thirty-eight minutes. Undisturbed, apart from a bucket.* Signed, *E. Wren,* witnessed, for want of anyone, by an apple tree.

Six in a row. If I got through this one I'd have seven, and I would be the first person in the history of the Royal Academy to have trained for sleeping. It's not on the Ladder. There's no pin for it.

I had also bet Felix a month of carrying his Strategy book. I'd rather not go into that.

"Seraphine's joke," said Felix. "Number nineteen. Do you want it?"

"No."

"A man goes to a baker and says he'd like a loaf. And the baker says, 'I'll see what I can rise to.'" He considered. "I laughed at it. I'm now aware that I'm not supposed to laugh until I mean it, and I've been unable to find a way to say this without it sounding like I'm complaining, so I've told her I *chuckled*. She wrote nineteen on her hand and then, I think, drew a small box round it."

"You're a double agent."

"I'm a man with a lunch arrangement." He said it without any heat. "Two lunches go to the roof door at noon in a basket on a hook. One's for you. I don't know who the other's for, and I've been told not to ask. In exchange I tell the Committee Wren is probably at the library, which is a lie, and also I tell you the Committee is probably at the library, which isn't. That's the whole of it. It's a good deal."

"For who?"

"Everybody who wants to eat."

I did open my eyes at that, a slit, to look up through the apple leaves. The roof of the Old Observatory is mostly sky. The dome sits at one end, scabbed with old copper, with the broken telescope sticking out of it like a bored finger, and the herb beds make a rough cross of raised planks around the apple tree. Dwarf apple, bent from wind, holding perhaps nine apples, which Lysette had told someone she was saving for jam. Felix had one foot on a branch and his back to the trunk, looking at something I couldn't see. The sky was a hard, pale, midday blue and there was one cloud in it. It was a very good day for sleeping.

"Fifth runner," said Felix.

I shut my eyes.

I had been over the week in my head, the way you go over a bad bridge. Day four the dormitory, where a Year Two had been posted under my bed for six hours with a whistle. Day five a cellar. Day six the boiler room again, the whole sprawling stinking length of it, and a runner on the Long Stair with a coil of *rope*, for reasons nobody explained. Lysette had seven officers on paper and one in practice, and she was throwing the whole of the one at the whole of the plateau. Felix's lunches, by comparison, were a mercy. I'd stopped asking what he told her. It had occurred to me only this morning that I'd also stopped asking why she'd never once asked him for the roof.

And not once, in seven days, had a Committee runner climbed the roof stair.

I lay and let that sit on me, in the thyme, with my eyes shut.

It's a narrow stair. Forty-one steps up the east side of the Old Observatory, behind a door with a rusting latch, and the only people who've ever used it are a headmistress with a bad eye, a girl who cries, and me. The door had a notice on it. Day one I'd walked past it and read it without reading it, the way you do. *CLOSED FOR REPAIR. Masonry. By order.* Square, tidy hand. Ink the color of dried blood, which is what the Committee's inkpots are filled with. The same ink and the same hand as warrants one through nine.

I'd taken the notice for the bursar's. The bursar never repairs anything.

"Felix," I said.

"Mm."

"The sign on the stair."

"Which sign?"

"The one that says the roof's being repaired."

"I didn't think there was one." He was silent a moment. "I'd have said the roof was in excellent repair. Apart from the telescope."

"Since day one," I said. "I thought she was hunting me. The warrants, the runners, the whole campaign." I opened both eyes and looked at the sky. "She's been keeping them off the stair."

"Ah," said Felix.

"For seven days."

"Ah," said Felix again, rather differently, and then, a good deal more quietly, "Eden. There's somebody on the stair."

I was already still. It's the first thing the body does. I lay in the thyme with my breath slowed, and my face the shape of a sleeping face, and I heard the latch on the roof door go up on the old iron, and the hinge say what it always says. And then nothing, for the length of time a person takes to stand on a threshold and not decide.

Then light, slow footsteps on the planks.

Felix said nothing at all, and the tree said nothing, and I think the cloud went away. I heard her go past the dome. I heard the planks of the first bed. I heard, not a yard from my head, the clunk of the little tin watering can, which I'd knocked over three weeks ago and set back, carefully, a thumb's width from where she keeps it.

She stood over me. I could tell from the shadow, and from how it changed the red behind my eyelids to a dull brown.

I'd had the right reply ready for a month. A little apology, half a joke, *I was only resting here, I didn't know it was yours.* It's a very good line. I had even rehearsed the surprise.

"You're in the rosemary," said Lysette.

It was not what I'd expected. It was very low, hardly voiced, said down to the thyme. I'd been lying beside the rosemary the whole of the week, my left hand in the herb bed, one finger against a little wooden plant-label that had been snapped in half and had a year's worth of weather on it. Somebody had mended it. A twist of copper wire, a short stick. Three weeks ago, and I hadn't been asked to.

She was looking at the label, I thought. And then at my hand. I did not open my eyes.

There was a long silence. I heard her crouch. I heard a fingernail tick against copper wire, once, and then the can, nudged that thumb's width back to where it belonged.

"Seven days," she said, to no one. "I've sent runners to the boiler room *twice.*"

I counted the leaves above me. I felt the apple tree leaning in over the two of us, politely, and Felix not breathing in it. I thought: if she says my name, I will have to answer, and my record will be spoiled, and I'll be carrying Felix's Strategy book for a month, and probably a good deal else.

She didn't say my name.

Something heavy and warm came down over my shoulders, a coat, her coat, dark wool, still holding the warmth of her. It had a lining of Committee yellow and it smelled of ink and chalk and, very faintly, of mint. She tucked the collar in around my neck. She did it quickly, the way you do something when you'd like not to have done it, and then she moved away, and I heard the planks going off toward the dome.

The can scraped. She was watering the beds, then. Left to right, like a person who has done it in the dark. I lay under her coat with my eyes shut and my heart making a noise I hoped the soil would absorb, and I counted nine sprinkles, and then a pause, and then the sound of paper.

I slitted one eye. I'd earned it.

She was kneeling at the end of the herb bed, in her shirt, with her back to me and her sleeves rolled up. In her hand she had a folded sheet, official, with a stamp at the top. It was today's warrant. It had a number on it, and a clause, and a signature, and I could see from where I lay that it had been filled in down to the last line, ready, in a hand that did not shake.

She tore it across. And across again. Four pieces. She held them a moment in the flat of her palm, as if she was weighing them.

Then she pushed each one into the soil of the bed with her thumb, one at a time, in a row, the way you set seed.

She wiped her hand on her skirt. She sat down on the edge of the herb bed with her knees up and her back to me, and her face to the dome, and the sky, and the long gold flat of Solmarch going down toward the river. And that was all. She didn't go. She stayed there with her chin on her arms and the little tin can beside her and the whole bright noon around her, like a person who has come to sit with something and has decided it will do.

After a while she said, to the dome, "Three weeks."

I didn't move.

"And you never told anyone." It wasn't a question. I'm not a fool, so I didn't answer it.

Behind me in the tree, very softly, Felix shifted his weight onto the other foot.

I stayed where I was. I was warm. It was the best nap of my life, and I'm entering it in the book, and not one word of why.
