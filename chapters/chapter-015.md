# The Hanging Loaf

"You walked past that fish seller twice," said Lysette.

She stood about four feet behind my left shoulder and used the voice she uses to read a rule out loud. I kept walking. It was a gray afternoon in the lower town, and the street smelled of salt and mud. A man sold eels from a barrel, and I walked past the eels and thought hard.

She had followed me since the cable car, and I had not seen her once. I had spent four years learning not to be followed, and the Committee had ruined that in nine minutes.

"It's a good fish seller," I said.

"Your pass says you are going to a book mender. The book mender is the other way, behind the clock." She walked up beside me with her clipboard held against her chest. She wore a gray felt hat with a strap under the chin, and she looked like a busy aunt. "You made two false turns. You stopped at a window and didn't look in it. Then you stopped at a lamp post and did something to your boot. What did you do?"

"Nothing."

"And why were you trying not to be followed?"

"I wasn't."

"You were. It's fine. You're quite good." She frowned at the street. "It's your shoulders. They're too loose. People who aren't being followed hold them like—" She stopped to think. "Like they carry a tray."

I did not know I carried a tray. I put that away for later.

Here was my problem. Four streets east of us were the tannery streets, where they make leather. A small group of Ashen Crowns hid there, four or five of them, and one man had a thumb stained brown from oak bark. He had watched Rowan across a gallery, and finding him was my job today. Ysolde had written it on a slip of paper, and she expected me to fail.

Now a Hollen of the Golden Seal stood at my elbow with a clipboard, a good excuse, a free afternoon and a firm belief that I had a secret. I did, and that was the awful part. She was right about everything except what the secret was.

If I sent her away, she would look harder. If I took her to the tanneries, she would stand in the middle of a group that wanted her classmate. At the first sign of trouble she would jump between them and anything. That is who she is. I thought it through twice, and neither road was safe.

"I'll show you where I'm going," I said.

She blinked.

"It's not the book mender. You're right. It's a place on Candlewick Lane, and I go once a week. You can see it, write it down and stop following me." I looked straight ahead. "I would like you to do it with the lights on."

"That is a helpful answer," said Lysette. "I find it suspicious."

"It's a short walk."

She wrote something down, and we went.

* * *

I took her the long way, because the long way was the safe way, and I still had not decided anything.

We walked down the rope street, where workers lay thick ropes out in the sun and the whole street smells of tar. We passed the back of the dye market, where the ground has stripes of blue and red and a yellow that makes your teeth feel strange. Then we passed the pear woman's stall.

It was open again, with the shutters up and a yellow notice half scraped off. The old woman sat on a stool behind her pears with a small, stern cat in her lap. She saw us, but she did not know me. Her eyes passed over my hood and stopped on Lysette's hat, and she nodded at the hat, very seriously.

"That's the woman the Golden Seal took," said Lysette. "On Wick Street."

"Is it?"

"A boy helped her. He wore a hood." She did not look at me. "My mother wrote to half the city. She wants to find him."

"What would she say to him?"

"I don't think that's the point." She walked on a few steps. "You'd think people want to say thank you. They don't. They want him to know someone saw."

I kept my face the same. It's a good face for that.

The lane goes downhill between old houses, and their top floors lean toward each other over the street. Halfway down, the lane bends. There is a door there with a baker's long shovel nailed above it, and under the shovel hangs a loaf of bread with a ribbon tied around it.

"Is that a decoration?" said Lysette.

"No."

"It has a ribbon."

"It's the hanging loaf."

I opened the door. A cracked cowbell hung over it, and it rang like a small angry fight. Everyone in the front of the room turned around.

* * *

The bakery was long, narrow and hot. A big old oven stood in the back wall, and its heat was so strong you could lean on it. The air smelled of yeast and burnt sugar, and flour lay on everything like snow.

Most of the people were from the south, from families who came north long ago and never went home. Women stood at the counter in dark red headscarves. A man rested one leg on a barrel, and two grandmothers picked stones out of lentils in the corner. At the back, in front of the oven, ten children sat on benches with slates.

"Wren!"

I had expected one of them, not all of them. The slates hit the benches like dropped plates. A small girl with a missing front tooth ran across the floor and hit me at the knee. A boy of seven stood straight by the table, because he had decided he was too old for hugs.

"Good afternoon, Mister Wren," he said in his best grown-up voice.

"Bodil," I said. "Halla. Not now."

"You're late," said the girl at my knee. "You said you'd come in the morning."

"It's still afternoon."

"It's almost evening." She looked at Lysette, and then she looked at the clipboard. "Who is that?"

"This is Lysette."

"The one you said was a thistle?"

Nobody spoke. I wish I could say I had said it kindly. I wish I could say we were talking about plants. I wish I had been anywhere else.

"A thistle," said Lysette.

"It's a very good plant," I said. "Finches love it."

"Oh, finches love it," said Halla, very kindly. "And it hurts."

Lysette said nothing. She stood in the middle of the bakery in her hat with her pencil in the air, and ten children looked at her. They were not unkind. They looked at her the way you look at a new bird that has walked into the kitchen. Her face went red from the collar up, and at the back a grandmother shook with silent laughter.

A short, wide woman came around the counter with flour on her apron and a book under one arm. She wiped her hands and looked at me over her glasses.

"Two weeks," she said. "I wrote you down for last week."

"Mistress Brisa. I had exams."

"You had a nap." She turned the book around on the counter and ran a finger down a row of numbers. "Fourteen loaves. Six for the morning bench, eight for the hook. Four silver." She looked at Lysette. "Is this the supervisor?"

"The—" Lysette stopped. "Who told you about a supervisor?"

"Hush, girl," said the baker. "He talks about nothing else."

That hit her, and I watched it land. Her pencil stopped, and she stared at the oven door very hard, as if it had asked her a question. I had seen her do this once before, in the low chapel. Her mouth was tight, but she was not angry.

I paid. Four silver is almost half of my school money for a month, and I have never missed it. Six loaves go to the benches each morning, so no child comes in hungry and no parent has to pretend not to see. The other eight go on the hook outside, with the ribbon. That is the whole rule of the hook. If you have no bread, take a loaf. If you can, pay for one later. Nobody asks which. The ribbon is there so people can see it from the end of the lane, and it tells them the walk is worth it.

"You don't eat any of it," said Lysette quietly.

She had come up next to me at the counter, and I had not heard her.

"I eat what I'm given."

"That's not what I said."

"No." I looked at the loaves. "It's not for me. That's the deal."

She opened her mouth and closed it. Then, in front of the whole bakery, in the heat and the flour and the noise of slates, she opened her clipboard to a clean page. At the top she wrote in her neat hand, the hand that signs warrants. *Candlewick Lane. Bakery. Charity without a license. Every week.*

She looked at it. Then, in small letters underneath, she wrote: *No further action.*

"For the record," she said.

"For the record."

"I'm not following you any more today."

"I noticed."

"It's your shoulders. You don't have the tray." She put the clipboard under her arm, and a child gave her a cup of hot barley water. Nobody had asked her to take it, but she took it with great dignity. "I'll stay a while. To finish the form."

"There's no form."

"There will be."

I walked back up the hill alone, which gave her an hour to herself. Today was not the day for the Ashen Crowns. Another day would come, and I would have to find them in the dark, the way I find most things.

* * *

The Dragon is not a good listener. I don't say that to be unkind. He is very old, and he thinks he is a mountain, and mountains don't take notes.

He started the moment I lay down on the roof that night, as he always does. It was as if we had argued for two days and I had only left for a drink of water.

*You paid four silver for bread you won't eat. Four silver! In my day, four silver bought a small city. You could be rude to the mayor too. You could have bought pears. Do you know how many people tried to pay me with pears—*

*I don't want to hear about pears.*

*—and not one was ripe. The trouble with new fruit is that the people who pick it know nothing about—*

He stopped.

That is not like him. He never stops. In eight years he has stopped in the middle of a sentence only two times. Both times something had hit the roof of the world and rung it like a bell.

The war dream lay over the roof. I could feel the weight of a thousand black helmets. I could feel the field, and the river with the wrong color.

*Landlord?*

*I promised someone,* said Sepiroth.

It was quiet. It was not his normal voice. It was lower and rougher, as if it came from a part of him he never shows. I lay under the apple tree with my hand open on the grass, and I thought about my mother, humming at a window.

*Who?*

The dream stayed silent. After a while, I understood that he would not say.
