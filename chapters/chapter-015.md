# The Hanging Loaf

"You've passed that fishmonger twice," said Lysette.

She said it from about four feet behind my left shoulder, in the voice she uses when she's reading a rule aloud. I kept walking. A man was selling eels out of a barrel, and I kept walking past the eels, and I thought, in order: that she'd been there since the funicular, that I hadn't seen her, that I had spent four years learning not to be followed, and that it had taken exactly nine minutes in Lantern Ward for the Discipline Committee to undo it.

"It's a good fishmonger," I said.

"Off-campus pass for Wren, E. Destination: a bookbinder's. The bookbinder's is the other way, behind the clock." She came up level with me, her clipboard against her chest. She had changed her coat and put on a hat, a gray felt one with a chin strap, and it made her look like somebody's very efficient aunt. "I've counted two false turns, a stop at a window you didn't look in, and a stop at a lamp-post where you did something with your boot. I'd like to know what, and I'd like to know why you were trying not to be followed."

"I wasn't."

"You were. It's all right. You're quite good." She frowned at the cobbles. "It's the shoulders. You keep them too loose. People who aren't being followed hold them like—" She thought about it. "Like they're carrying a tray."

I had not been aware that I carried a tray. I filed it away.

Here is where I was. East of us, four streets, past the dye vats and the old salt exchange, were the tannery streets, and somewhere in them was a cell of Ashen Crowns, four or five, who had a man with an oak-bark thumb who'd watched Rowan across a gallery. That was the job. Ysolde had given it to me on a slip of paper with the particular dryness of a woman who expects you to fail. And a Hollen of the Golden Seal was standing at my elbow with a clipboard, a Committee pretext, an hour of free afternoon and the unshakeable conviction that I had a secret.

Which I did. That was the awful part. She was right about everything except what it was.

If I shook her, she'd keep looking, and think the more of it. If I led her to the tanneries, a girl with a Witness Tenet would be standing in the middle of a cell of Embers that wanted one of her classmates, and at the first sign of trouble she'd throw herself between them and anything, because that's what she does. I went through it twice. Neither road went anywhere I could walk.

"I'll show you where I was going," I said.

She blinked.

"It's not the bookbinder's. You're right. It's a place down Candlewick, I go once a week. You can see everything. Then you can write it down, and stop following me." I looked straight ahead. "I'd rather you did it with the lights on."

"That," said Lysette, after a moment, "is a remarkably cooperative answer. I find it suspicious."

"It's a short walk."

She wrote something. We went.

* * *

I took her the long way, because the long way was the safe way, and also because I hadn't decided yet.

Down the Ropewalk, where they lay out the hawsers in the sun and the whole street smells of tar and warm hemp. Along the back of the dye market, where the ground is stained in stripes: indigo, madder, a yellow that gets into your teeth just to look at. Past the pear woman's stall, which was open. Shutters up, the Seal's yellow notice scraped half off, and the old woman herself sitting on a stool behind the pears with a very small, very stern cat on her lap. She saw us. She didn't know me. Her eyes went over my hood and stopped on Lysette's hat, and she nodded to the hat, gravely, like a woman acknowledging another power.

"She's the one the Seal took," Lysette said. "Wick Street."

"Is she?"

"There was a boy. Hooded." She said it without looking at me. "My mother's written to half the city. She wants to find him."

"Does she know what she'd say?"

"I don't think that's the point of it." She walked on a few paces. "You'd think the thing people want to say is *thank you.* It's not. They want him to know it was seen."

I kept my face the way it was. It's a good face for it.

Candlewick Lane runs downhill between two rows of old houses with their upper floors leaning together overhead, and about halfway down, where the lane bends, there is a door with a baker's peel nailed over it, and under the peel, on a hook, a loaf of bread with a ribbon tied round it.

"Is that a *display*?" said Lysette.

"No."

"It's got a ribbon."

"That's the hanging loaf."

I opened the door. The bell over it was a cracked cowbell, and it went off like a small violent argument, and the whole front of the room turned around.

* * *

The bakery was long and narrow and hot, with a counter at the front and a very large old oven set into the back wall, giving out a heat you could lean on. Southern refugees, mostly. The families who came north from the duchies and never went back, who take whatever lanes the city isn't using. There were women at the counter in headscarves the color of old wine, a man with one leg propped on a barrel, two grandmothers in a corner picking through lentils. And at the back, on benches in front of the oven, between a flour-bin and a table, sat nine or ten children with slates.

"*Wren!*"

I had expected one of them. I had not expected all of them. The slates went down like a rattle of dropped plates, and a small girl with a front tooth missing came across the floor at me with her arms out and hit me at about the knee, and a boy of seven, who had decided he was too old for that, stood very straight by the table and said "Good afternoon, Mister Wren," in a tone of tremendous diplomacy.

"Bodil," I said. "Halla. Idris. Not now."

"You're *late.* You said Tuesday."

"It's Tuesday."

"It's *nearly* Wednesday." She considered the girl in the hat. Her eyes traveled to the clipboard. "Who's that?"

"This is Lysette."

"The one you said was a thistle?"

There are some silences that stand up by themselves. I would like to say that I'd said it fondly, in the context of a larger conversation about shrubs, and I would like to have been standing anywhere else when I did.

"A thistle," said Lysette.

"It's a very good plant," I said. "It's good for the finches."

"Oh, it's *lovely* for the finches," said Halla, with enormous goodwill, "and it hurts."

Lysette said nothing at all. She stood in the middle of the bakery, in her hat, with her pencil poised, while ten children examined her openly and without malice, the way you'd look at a new kind of bird that had walked into the kitchen. Her ears had gone red. A grandmother at the back was shaking silently.

Then a short, wide woman in a floury apron came round the end of the counter wiping her hands, with a ledger under one arm, and looked at me over a pair of spectacles, and said, "Two weeks. I had you down for last Tuesday."

"Mistress Brisa. I had exams."

"You had a *nap.*" She turned the ledger round on the counter, and ran her finger down a column. "Fourteen loaves. Six for the morning bench, eight for the hook. Four silver." She looked at Lysette. "Is this the supervisor?"

"The—" Lysette stopped. "Who told you about a supervisor?"

"Hush, girl," said Mistress Brisa, "he talks about nothing else."

That one went in. I watched it go in. The pencil stopped, and Lysette did a thing I've seen her do once before, in the lowchapel: she looked at the wall very hard, as if there might be a sentence written on it, and her mouth was tight in a way that had nothing to do with annoyance.

I paid. Four silver is nearly half of a scholarship stipend and I've never once noticed it leave. It buys six loaves in the morning for the benches, so none of the children come in hungry and none of the parents have to pretend not to notice, and eight to go on the hook outside, with the ribbon. That's the hook's whole rule. You take a loaf if you have none. You pay one forward if you can. Nobody asks which. The ribbon is so you can tell, from the end of the lane, whether it's worth the walk.

"You don't eat any of it," said Lysette quietly.

She had come up beside me at the counter. I hadn't heard her.

"I eat what I'm given."

"That isn't what I said."

"No." I looked at the loaves. "It's not for me. That's the arrangement."

She opened her mouth. She shut it. Then, in front of the whole of the bakery, in the heat and the flour and the rattling of slates being picked up again, she opened her clipboard, turned to a clean page, and wrote at the top of it, in the square tidy hand that signs warrants: *Candlewick Lane. Bakery. Unlicensed charity. Recurring.* She looked at it. And she wrote beneath it, quite small: *No further action.*

"For the record," she said.

"For the record."

"I'm not following you any more today."

"I noticed."

"It's the shoulders. You don't have the tray." She tucked the clipboard under her arm and, with great dignity, accepted from Halla a cup of hot barley water that nobody had asked her about. "I'm going to stay a little while. To finish the form."

"There's no form."

"There will be."

I took the long road back up the hill alone, at last, to give her the hour. It wasn't the Embers' day. It would be another. I'd have to find them in the dark, the way I find most things.

* * *

The Dragon is not a good listener. I don't say that unkindly. He's very old and he thinks of himself as a mountain, and mountains don't take notes.

He started when I lay down on the roof that night, the way he always does, as if we'd been in the middle of an argument for the last two days and I'd stepped out for a drink of water. *You paid four silver for bread you won't eat. Four silver! In my day four silver would have bought you a small city and the right to be rude to its mayor. You could have had the pears. Do you know how many people have tried to bribe me with pears—*

*I don't want to hear about the pears.*

*—and not once have I received any that were actually ripe, the entire trouble with modern fruit is that the people picking it have no notion of—*

He stopped.

It's not like him. He doesn't pause. In eight years I've known him to break off in the middle of a sentence precisely twice, both times because something had gone through the roof of the world and rung it like a bell.

The war dream lay over the roof. I could feel the weight of a thousand black helmets, and the field, and the river the wrong color.

*Landlord?*

*I promised someone,* said Sepiroth.

It was very quiet. It wasn't his voice. I mean that it was lower than his voice, and had a different grain, as if it had come up out of a part of him he doesn't take out to show people. I lay under the apple tree with my hand open at my side, on the grass, and thought about my mother humming at a window.

*Who?*

The dream was silent. After some time it went on being silent and I understood that he wasn't going to say.
