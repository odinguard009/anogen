# Court Is in Session

The notice said two hours and ten minutes. Felix read it out loud, and by the end of it the time was two hours and six.

"'The prince, first on the Ladder, calls Wren, eighth on the Ladder, to a formal duel,'" he read. "'Long Court, sixth bell, before the whole year. If he refuses, he loses his rung, and the Committee must ask why he refused.'" He stopped. "That last part is underlined."

"Someone did it by hand," said Rowan. "With a ruler."

"It's him. He would use a ruler."

"Is that bad?" asked Rowan.

"It means he planned it," said Felix. "He wants everyone to read it twice."

We stood in the big front hall in front of the notice board, and about forty people pretended not to look at me. Someone had already scratched a small bet into the corner of the frame. The hall smelled of wet wool, and boiled cabbage drifted in from the dining room.

The first rung may call anyone within eight rungs, once a term. I read that rule the year I came, because I read every rule by looking for the exits. This one had a single small exit. I could refuse. Then the Committee would ask why Wren would not fight, and soon someone would ask what Wren was.

"You could just lose," said Rowan.

"That's the plan."

"No, I mean on purpose. Fast."

"Rowan," said Felix.

"What? It's good advice!"

It was good advice, and it was also the second-worst thing I could do. A man on the eighth rung who loses fast gets noticed for losing fast. Valerius had picked his moment well, as he always does. He had read the rule the same year I had.

"Have you eaten today?" Felix asked quietly.

"Not yet."

He put half a bun in my glove and said nothing else. It was the nicest thing anyone did for me that day.

* * *

The waiting room under the Long Court benches is a stone box with one bench and a water jug. It smells of old sand and other people's fear. I sat there with my lance across my knees and listened to the crowd come in above me. I heard boots on wood, a lot of boots.

Nobody comes to Ladder duels, because they are boring: two people walk in circles for ten minutes while a judge writes down a number. Today people came in rows.

Okoru ducked through the door with a piece of licorice root in his cheek. He was tall and thin, and he never stood straight under a low roof.

"Rules," he said. "Both blades have blunting rings. He asked for no healers on the sand. We have three judges, me, the old one with the cane, and Dame Ostwald. She asked me to say she is only here for the tea."

"Is there tea?"

"There is not. She is very sad about it." He moved the root to the other cheek. "A touch on the body is a clean hit. A judge rings the bell, and the duel is over."

"Fine."

"Fine," said Okoru. "That's what you say before you get serious. I've heard you say it twice."

"I won't get serious."

"Good. Then say it more quietly." He walked to the door and turned back. "One more thing. The rings don't cover the Verdict. You can blunt a blade, but you can't blunt a wall. Last spring a boy ran into one, and we put his collarbone back together in two pieces."

"Noted."

"Don't note it. Think about it."

He left. The door had barely closed when it opened again, and Lysette came in without knocking. She held the duel book against her chest like a shield. She was the marshal, of course. The Committee runs every duel, and she was the Committee.

"Stand up," she said.

I stood, and she looked at my boots, my belt and my glove. Then she pulled my left cuff straight with two fingers. Her hand was perfectly steady. That is how I knew she was afraid.

"If it goes badly, you yield," she said. "Don't look at me. Just say it out loud. I will ring the bell myself."

"I'll remember that."

"Don't remember it. Do it."

"Lysette."

"He is doing this on purpose." Her voice dropped. "I don't know why. But he waited until the Ball was three weeks away and the whole year was bored. I wrote it in my notes and put a star next to it."

"What does a star mean?"

"It means I don't like it."

She went out. Above us the crowd made one big sound, as if someone had lifted a lid off a pot, and I knew Valerius had arrived.

* * *

Long Court is an oval of sand, forty paces long, with a white chalk line across the middle. Benches stand on three sides. At the north end sits the judges' table, and beside it a brass bell hangs on a stand. By the sixth bell the sun had dropped behind the roof of the armory. Every post on the sand threw a shadow as long as a cart.

I took the south mark. Valerius already stood on the north, forty paces away.

Rowan sat in the front row with Clara and Felix. He waved with his whole arm. Clara folded her hands and seemed to count the exits. Felix nodded once. Lysette sat at the marshal's desk next to the bell, with her book open and her pen moving. She did not look up.

Valerius had taken off his cloak. He stood square with his feet apart and held his longsword point down with both hands on the end. He looked perfect, and I will admit it was nice to watch.

"Wren," he said.

"Sir."

"On the Ladder, I'm Valerius."

"Then I'm Wren."

"Do you know why I called you?"

"The Ladder says the first rung can call anyone within eight."

"That is why I was allowed to."

"I liked the first answer better."

Someone on the benches laughed. The old judge hissed, and it stopped. At the table, Dame Ostwald watched me with her good eye and held a flask in her lap. I decided not to think about the flask.

Someone tapped the bell once, and Valerius raised his blade.

In the first exchange I did nothing clever, which is my rule. I blocked and watched his shoulders. He came straight down the middle and cut high, then low, then high again. Each cut was clean and level, and I caught every one on the lance shaft. His body gives no warning. But before a ruling he has one habit, and his weight sinks into his back heel, as if he would say something important.

He did it now, and I was ready.

"This line is not crossed," he said.

He dragged the tip of his sword through the sand behind my boots and made one long scratch. The scratch did not stay a scratch. A pane of pale, cloudy force rose out of it, as high as my shoulder. I was already moving, but it hit me hard across the left side of my back.

A door hit me, not a spell.

My ribs went hot, then dull. I stayed on my feet, and that mattered more than the pain. I kept the lance level and my face bored.

"Sorry," said Valerius. He did not sound sorry.

"It's a good wall."

"It's a fair wall. That's the point. Everyone in this court can see it's fair."

"Then everyone is lucky."

"Court is in session," said Valerius. He drew a second line on my right, and a second wall rose. A third came up close on my left. Now I stood in a gray corridor as wide as a wardrobe, and Valerius waited at the open end.

Behind the cloudy walls, the benches were smears of faces. They could see shapes and nothing more. That was the first thing I noticed. The second was how neat it all was. He was not trying to hit me. He was making my room smaller, so that I would have one thing left to do, and he wanted to see it.

Everyone watches a trapped man the same way, because they wait for him to break out.

"Come on," said Valerius, low. "You can do better than the corner. I've seen you do better in the dining hall."

"I like the corner."

"Liar."

"I'm a very honest man. Ask anyone."

"I did. They said you nap."

He stepped in and cut. I blocked, but my feet slid back an inch, and my heel hit the wall. There it was, and I had nowhere to go.

That was when I stopped thinking of the walls as his. They were thick, and from the benches each one was only a smear. Three walls made three smears, and behind them nobody could see my hands. There was one open end, and Valerius would have to come around it. He would come straight on, the way he does everything.

I could feel my pulse in my hurt rib. Each breath caught, as if something inside would not close.

My plan was simple. I wanted him to charge, and I wanted him to think I was finished. So I let the lance drop low. I let my shoulders go loose and my breath go rough, which was not hard with the rib. I looked tired, like a man with no ideas left.

"Last chance," said Valerius.

"For what?"

"For you to stop being so polite about it."

He charged.

He took the corner at full speed, with his blade low and level. It was a good charge and would have gone through a man. I waited at the side of the wall with my back against it. When his front foot landed, I turned on it like a door on a hinge. His blade passed the place where my chest had been.

I call it the Hinge. It is only one step. But now he was past me with his sword arm out and his wrist an inch from my hand.

I turned the lance in my fingers and tapped the butt end into the inside of his wrist. It was not hard, but it was exactly in the right place, on the nerve near the bone.

His fingers opened, and they did not ask his permission.

The longsword fell and stuck upright in the sand between us, shaking.

From the benches it looked like nothing. They saw a wall's edge, a shoulder and a smear. They saw him charge, and Wren in the way with his lance across his chest. Then the sword was on the ground. Someone said, "Did he drop it?"

Okoru had stopped chewing, and I saw it over Valerius's shoulder. He sat at the judges' table with his jaw still and the licorice forgotten in his cheek, and one hand lay flat on the wood. He had seen all of it. He was the only one who could, and he was looking at the angle, not at me.

Valerius stood very straight. He opened and closed his right hand once, slowly, and looked at it. Then he looked at me, long and careful, as if I were a lock that had opened too easily.

"Again," he said, so only I could hear. "And this time, try."

He pulled the sword out of the sand with his left hand. Behind him, the bell hung on its stand, and nobody had touched the rope.
