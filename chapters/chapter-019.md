# The East Kitchen

> "The cooking club regrets nothing. The east kitchen regrets everything." - Hallowcrest incident report

The east kitchen has eleven ovens and only one of them works. The other ten are there for show, and for the mice.

I knew that because I'd read the maintenance book the first week I came to Hallowcrest. It's a good habit. Most buildings are trying to kill you in some small way, and a maintenance book tells you which way.

"Sixteen eggs," said Rowan, coming through the door with a crate on his shoulder. "And two more in my pocket. Don't tell Clara."

"Why's it a secret?" said Felix.

"It's not a secret. I'm going to eat them."

That was Wednesday afternoon, six days before the Ball, and the whole of Lance Nine stood in the east kitchen in aprons. Not by choice. Rowan had put our names down for the Ball-week bake before anyone could object. He'd written "Lance Nine: cake, probably big" on the club's sign-up sheet in letters the size of his fist.

"A cake," I said.

"A big one!"

"For who?"

"For everybody. It's the Ball. People want cake."

I looked at Clara. She was standing very still by a mixing bowl, holding a whisk like it was a small, strange animal. Clara can close a cut on Felix's arm before he's finished bleeding. She told me once that she'd never baked anything in her life. I believed her. It showed in how she held the whisk.

"I can heal burns," she said, to nobody. "I don't think I can help with a soufflé."

"Nobody said soufflé!" said Rowan.

"Seraphine did."

"Seraphine says a lot of things."

"I said *one* thing," said Seraphine from the far counter, "and it was a very good one." She had flour on her cheek and a wooden spoon behind her ear. In front of her, with an expression like a man waiting for a boat, stood Felix.

She'd dragged him in. I'd watched it happen. She'd walked up to the tack room that morning and said "I need a strong, silent type to hold a bowl," and he'd come, which proved that Felix is either very polite or very doomed.

* * *

The club president was a Year Three named Isolde with flour up to both elbows and the haunted look of someone who'd seen this before.

"Rules," she said. "One oven. One at a time. No open flame near the flour. No Sparks, no Edges, no Kindling, nothing that makes sparks. The ceiling beams are old. We've been over this."

"Is the flour dangerous?" said Rowan.

"The flour is the *most* dangerous thing in the room," said Isolde, and she walked away to cry in the pantry.

Rowan looked at the sack. He seemed delighted.

Lysette came in at four. She had an apron on over her Committee coat, tied up at the back with a very exact bow, and she carried a clipboard in one hand like a lamp. There was a green sprig in the apron pocket, and I knew where it had come from. Nobody has rosemary this late in autumn. Nobody except one roof.

"Kitchen Safety Form Twenty-Two," she said. "I'll need a signature from each of you, and I'll need to see the extinguisher."

"There's an extinguisher?" said Felix.

"There was in the Founders' Year. I'm told it's under the sink."

She went to look. Rowan wandered after her, because he follows people who are holding something official, the way a dog follows a sack.

"Is that rosemary?" he said.

Lysette stopped.

"It smells like rosemary. Where'd you get rosemary? Nobody's got rosemary this late. Not even the market."

"The Committee has a supplier."

"What's the supplier called?"

"That's confidential."

"Does he have more? Because the cake—"

"There is no supplier," said Clara, from the whisk, in the voice of a girl who'd been told the sky was blue, and she'd never once thought to check. "It smells like a garden."

Lysette went back under the sink so fast she banged her head on the pipe. I'm fairly sure she said a word the handbook forbids. When she came up, her face was very calm and she held the extinguisher out in front of her like a baby she hadn't wanted.

I watched Rowan watching her, all friendly curiosity. I could almost see him thinking: *rosemary, autumn, the roof—* and then Clara dropped the whisk and he turned around to help.

That's how close it was. It was about a whisk's worth.

* * *

I'd made a plan. I'll say that for the record, because it was a good one.

I'd come in at four. By five I was going to remember a book I'd left on the roof. By ten past, I'd be gone. I'd worked it out down to the minute, including Lysette, because she was the hard part.

At five to five I put my hand in my pocket and took out a pretend slip of paper, and looked at it with a worried face.

"Oh," I said.

"Sit down," said Lysette, without turning round.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You were going to remember something. I can tell from your forehead." She wrote on her clipboard. "It's been noted. Sit."

I sat.

Felix gave me a small, sad look from across the room. It was the look of a man watching a friend stuff himself into a very small boat.

"Eaten today?" he said.

"Technically."

"It's a cake. There's going to be a *lot* of cake."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

Seraphine pointed her spoon at him.

"Felix. Felix, listen. What do you call a soufflé that wants to be famous?"

He looked at her. His face did not change at all.

"A rising star!" said Seraphine.

A pause.

"Mm," said Felix.

"It's *rising!*"

"I got it."

"Then why didn't you laugh?"

"I did," said Felix. "On the inside."

She took a stub of charcoal from her apron and wrote a number on the back of her hand. It was a 41. I could see six earlier ones in a row above it, going up her wrist like a staircase, and I didn't want to think about the other thirty-four.

"Next time," she said, "I'm using a pun about dough."

"I'll look forward to it."

"You won't!"

He smiled a little at the bowl. It was only a corner of a smile, and he didn't think anyone saw. I saw. So did Seraphine, and she looked at the ceiling very hard and went pink.

* * *

By six, the east kitchen looked like a battlefield with butter.

Rowan had cracked eighteen eggs, five of them onto the floor. Clara had made a batter so thick the spoon stood up in it by itself. Felix had stood in front of a bowl for an hour and was holding it like a prisoner. Seraphine had gotten into the flour sack, and the flour had gotten into everything else. It hung in the air as a fine white haze, drifting slowly across the room toward the one working oven.

I watched it.

It's a strange feeling, when everything in a room starts to slope toward one thing. I'd had it on the Long Stair and in the maze, and I'd had it in the bell market. It's the feeling of a lamp being tilted. I didn't need a Tenet to know what a cloud of fine flour does when it meets a hot iron door.

"Rowan," I said. "Don't open the oven."

"It's the cake!"

"I know. But wait for it."

"It's been *forty minutes!*"

"Rowan—"

He opened the oven.

The thing about flour is that it doesn't burn. It *goes.* The whole cloud caught at once, in a soft white *whump* that I felt in my ears before I saw it. Rowan was at the oven door, and Clara was beside him, and Felix and Seraphine were ten feet back with the bowl between them.

I was already moving. I'd been moving since *wait.*

Behind the long stone table there's a shadow. In the lamp, it's a thick black rectangle, and it was about two feet from the oven door. I put one gloved hand flat against my own shadow on the floor, down by my knee, where nobody was looking, and I knocked.

It was a very small door, hung in a very big hurry.

The fire came out of the oven in a ball the size of a cart. It hit the shadow-door, and the door closed on it like a hand over a candle. The flame went dark. The blast rolled up the wall instead, and blew out the one window and every pane of the pantry, and a shelf of tins fell over with a noise like a drum solo.

Then there was silence. Flour came down very slowly, like snow.

Nobody was hurt. I checked each one. Rowan sat on the floor with his eyebrows singed and a look of complete joy. Clara had her hands over her head. Felix stood with the bowl against his chest, white from hair to boots, like a statue of himself. Seraphine was behind him, laughing helplessly.

"Is everyone alive?" said Lysette.

She was in the doorway. The extinguisher was in her hands, and she hadn't used it. Her form was on the floor, scorched at one corner.

"I think so!" said Rowan.

"That shouldn't have worked," said Clara. She meant the wall, where the fire hadn't gone. "It went the wrong way. It went *up.*"

"Luck," I said.

"That's a lot of luck."

"It's a good year for it."

I felt my rib then, a long, bright line of it down the left side, like someone had drawn it with a hot wire. I'd put weight on the arm that was holding the door, and the door had weighed more than it looked. Late bills are the worst bills. I stood up very straight, and I did not touch my side.

Lysette picked up her form. She smoothed it out on her knee, read the damage, and filled in a line. I watched over her shoulder. *Cause of fire,* she wrote, in a perfectly clear hand. *Luck.*

Then she looked up, and I could see that she didn't believe it.

"The Committee will return tomorrow," she said, "for a full inspection."

"Of the kitchen?" said Rowan.

"Of everybody."

* * *

We left the east kitchen at seven, in a file, like survivors. The corridor outside was cold, and smelled of burnt sugar.

Seraphine fell in beside Lysette. She'd washed her face. She still had a white streak in her hair, and she was swinging her wooden spoon.

"Lysette," she said, very sweetly. "I've been meaning to ask. Who are you going to the Ball with?"

There was a very short pause. Rowan was ten paces ahead, talking to Clara about eggs.

"I haven't decided," said Lysette.

"Haven't you? Not *now?*"

"No."

"Oh," said Seraphine. She sounded as if she'd been given a present. "That's interesting."

I walked behind them both, one hand pressed flat against my coat, and thought that I had never, in my life, been so glad to be a man nobody asked anything.

Then I took a step down the stairs, and my rib reminded me that it had been quiet all afternoon only because it was waiting.
