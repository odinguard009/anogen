# The East Kitchen

> "The cooking club regrets nothing. The east kitchen regrets everything." - Hallowcrest incident report

The east kitchen has eleven ovens. Only one of them works. The other ten are there for show, and for the mice.

I knew that because I read the repair book in my first week at Hallowcrest. It is a good habit. Most buildings try to kill you in some small way. The repair book tells you how.

"Sixteen eggs," said Rowan. He came through the door with a crate on his shoulder. "And two more in my pocket. Don't tell Clara."

"Why is it a secret?" said Felix.

"It's not a secret. I'm going to eat them."

It was Wednesday afternoon, six days before the Ball. All of Lance Nine stood in the east kitchen in aprons. We had not chosen this. Rowan had put our names on the list for the Ball-week bake before anyone could say no. He had written "Lance Nine: cake, probably big" on the sign-up sheet. His letters were as big as his fist.

"A cake," I said.

"A big one!"

"For who?"

"For everybody. It's the Ball. People want cake."

I looked at Clara. She stood still by a mixing bowl. She held a whisk as if it were a small, strange animal. Clara can close a cut on Felix's arm before he finishes bleeding. She once told me she had never baked anything in her life. I believed her. I could see it in how she held the whisk.

"I can heal burns," she said, to no one. "I don't think I can help with a soufflé."

"No one said soufflé!" said Rowan.

"Seraphine did."

"Seraphine says a lot of things."

"I said one thing," said Seraphine from the far counter. "And it was a good one." She had flour on her cheek. A wooden spoon stuck out from behind her ear. In front of her stood Felix. He waited, still as a post.

She had dragged him in. I had watched it happen. She had walked up to the tack room that morning and said, "I need a strong, quiet person to hold a bowl." He came. That proved Felix is either very polite or in very big trouble.

* * *

The club president was a Year Three named Isolde. She had flour up to both elbows. She looked like someone who had seen this before.

"Rules," she said. "One oven. One person at a time. No open fire near the flour. No Sparks, no Edges, no Kindling. Nothing that makes sparks. The roof beams are old. We have talked about this."

"Is flour dangerous?" said Rowan.

"Flour is the most dangerous thing in this room," said Isolde. She walked away to cry in the pantry.

Rowan looked at the sack. He seemed happy.

Lysette came in at four. She wore an apron over her Committee coat. It was tied at the back with a very exact bow. She carried her clipboard in one hand. A green sprig stuck out of her apron pocket. I knew where it came from. Nobody has rosemary this late in autumn. No one except one roof.

"Kitchen Safety Form Twenty-Two," she said. "I need a signature from each of you. And I need to see the fire extinguisher."

"There's an extinguisher?" said Felix.

"There was one in the Founders' Year. I'm told it is under the sink."

She went to look. Rowan walked after her. He follows people who hold something official, the way a dog follows a food sack.

"Is that rosemary?" he said.

Lysette stopped.

"It smells like rosemary. Where did you get rosemary? No one has rosemary this late. Not even the market."

"The Committee has a supplier."

"What's the supplier's name?"

"That is private."

"Does he have more? Because for the cake—"

"It smells like a garden," said Clara from the whisk. She did not look up. "A real one. With dirt."

Lysette went back under the sink so fast that she hit her head on the pipe. I am fairly sure she said a word the handbook does not allow. When she came up, her face was calm. She held the extinguisher out in front of her as if it were something she had not wanted.

I watched Rowan watch her. He was friendly and curious. I could almost see him think: *Rosemary, autumn, the roof—* Then Clara dropped the whisk, and he turned to help.

That was how close it was. It was about one whisk.

* * *

I had made a plan. I will say that for the record, because it was a good plan.

I came in at four. At five, I would remember a book I had left on the roof. At ten past, I would be gone. I had worked it out to the minute. That included Lysette, because she was the hard part.

At five to five, I put my hand in my pocket. I took out a pretend slip of paper and looked at it with a worried face.

"Oh," I said.

"Sit down," said Lysette. She did not turn around.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You were going to remember something. I can see it in your forehead." She wrote on her clipboard. "It's noted. Sit."

I sat.

Felix gave me a small, sad look from across the room. He looked at me the way you look at a friend in a very small boat.

"The window behind you opens," he said.

"It's painted shut."

"Then it's a cake. You'll survive a cake."

"That's what people say before the cake."

Seraphine pointed her spoon at him.

"Felix. Felix, listen. What do you call a soufflé that wants to be famous?"

He looked at her. His face did not change at all.

"A rising star!" said Seraphine.

There was a pause.

"Mm," said Felix.

"It's rising!"

"I got it."

"Then why didn't you laugh?"

"I did," said Felix. "On the inside."

She took a piece of charcoal from her apron. She wrote a number on the back of her hand. It was 41. I could see six more numbers above it, going up her wrist like stairs. I did not want to think about the other thirty-four.

"Next time," she said, "I'll use a joke about dough."

"I'll look forward to it."

"You won't!"

He smiled a little at the bowl. It was only the corner of a smile. He did not think anyone saw. I saw. So did Seraphine. She looked hard at the ceiling and went pink.

* * *

By six, the east kitchen looked like a battlefield with butter.

Rowan had cracked eighteen eggs. Five of them went on the floor. Clara had made a batter so thick that the spoon stood up in it. Felix had stood in front of a bowl for an hour. He held it tight. Seraphine had fallen into the flour sack, and the flour was now on everything. It hung in the air as a fine white cloud. It drifted slowly across the room toward the one working oven.

I watched it.

It is a strange feeling when everything in a room starts to lean toward one thing. I had felt it on the Long Stair. I had felt it in the maze. I had felt it in the bell market. I did not need a Tenet to know what a cloud of fine flour does when it meets a hot iron door.

"Rowan," I said. "Don't open the oven."

"It's the cake!"

"I know. But wait."

"It's been forty minutes!"

"Rowan—"

He opened the oven.

Flour does not burn slowly. It goes all at once. The whole cloud caught fire together with a soft white *whump*. I felt it in my ears before I saw it. Rowan stood at the oven door. Clara stood beside him. Felix and Seraphine were ten feet back, with the bowl between them.

I was already moving. I had been moving since "wait."

Behind the long stone table there is a shadow. In the lamp light it is a thick black rectangle. It was about two feet from the oven door. I dropped to one knee behind the table. I put my gloved hand flat on the edge of that shadow, where no one was looking. I knocked.

It was a very small door, made in a very big hurry.

The fire came out of the oven in a ball as big as a cart. It hit the shadow door. The door closed on it the way a hand covers a candle. The flame went dark. The blast rolled up the wall instead. It blew out the one window and every pane of glass in the pantry. A shelf of tins fell over with a noise like a drum.

Then it was quiet. The flour came down slowly, like snow.

Nobody was hurt. I checked each one. Rowan sat on the floor. His eyebrows were burned, and he looked completely happy. Clara had her hands over her head. Felix stood with the bowl against his chest. He was white from hair to boots, like a statue of himself. Seraphine stood behind him and laughed until she could not breathe.

"Is everyone alive?" said Lysette.

She stood in the doorway. She held the extinguisher in both hands. She had not used it. Her form lay on the floor. One corner was burned.

"I think so!" said Rowan.

"That should not have worked," said Clara. She meant the wall, where the fire had not gone. "It went the wrong way. It went up."

"Luck," I said.

"That's a lot of luck."

"It's a good year for it."

I felt my rib then. A long, bright line of pain ran down my left side. It felt like someone had drawn it with a hot wire. I had put weight on the arm that held the door. The door had weighed more than it looked. The bill always comes later. It always does. I stood up straight, and I did not touch my side.

Lysette picked up her form. She smoothed it on her knee. She read the damage and filled in one line. I watched over her shoulder. *Cause of fire,* she wrote, in a perfectly clear hand. *Luck.*

Then she looked up. I could see she did not believe it.

"The Committee will come back tomorrow," she said. "For a full check."

"Of the kitchen?" said Rowan.

"Of everybody."

* * *

We left the east kitchen at seven, in a line, like survivors. The hall outside was cold. It smelled of burnt sugar.

Seraphine walked up beside Lysette. She had washed her face. She still had a white streak in her hair. She swung her wooden spoon.

"Lysette," she said, very sweetly. "I've wanted to ask you. Who are you going to the Ball with?"

There was a short pause. Rowan was ten steps ahead. He was talking to Clara about eggs.

"I haven't decided," said Lysette.

"Haven't you? Not even now?"

"No."

"Oh," said Seraphine. She sounded as if someone had given her a present. "That's interesting."

I walked behind them both. I pressed one hand flat against my coat. I thought that I had never been so glad to be a man nobody asks anything.

Then I took a step down the stairs. My rib reminded me that it had been quiet all afternoon only because it was waiting.
