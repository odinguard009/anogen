# The Crooked Collar

"Say it again, but slower," said Felix.

"It's the same words, Felix," said Seraphine. "Slower won't change them."

We stood on the landing of the Long Stair. The morning crowd gets thin there. The stone is so cold that you feel it through your boots. It was five days before the Ball. Workers were hanging banners in the yard below. A first-year hung from a ladder. He was losing a fight with a lantern hook.

Seraphine had run up the steps two at a time. She stopped in front of me. Now she held her chin high and her hands behind her back, like a girl about to recite a poem.

"Eden Wren," she said. "Will you come to the Lantern Ball with me? As a joke. Mostly."

"Which part is not the joke?" I said.

"The part where you say yes."

I looked at Felix. He studied the stair rail as if it had said something rude about his family.

"I'll say no," I said.

"You haven't heard the offer!"

"I heard it. You said it twice."

"There's a second half. I get you a seat by the window. I get you a plate of the little smoked things. I stand next to you, so no one else asks you to dance." She counted on her fingers. "That's a very good offer. It's almost a rescue."

"I'll be on the roof."

"On the roof?"

"I like the roof."

"It's a ball, Eden. It's not an exam. You can't skip it and say you were studying."

"I wasn't going to say I was studying."

"What were you going to say?"

"Nothing. I would just be on the roof."

Seraphine turned slowly to Felix. "Did you tell him?"

"Tell him what?" said Felix.

"That I would ask."

"No."

"Someone told him. He isn't even surprised."

"I'm surprised," I said. "I just have a flat face."

Felix gave me a long, level look. I gave him one back. In four years he had never once sold me out. I was fairly sure this was not the start. But it is easier to check a thing than to trust it, and checking cost nothing.

He raised both hands a little. "I told her you would say no," he said. "I told her yesterday, Eden. That's all I said."

"He said it with pity," said Seraphine.

"I didn't."

"You did. You sighed."

"I don't sigh," said Felix. Then he sighed. It sounded like a dropped book.

A little way off, I heard a different sound.

I had heard it for a minute without knowing what it was. It was a thin hum, a few notes long. It went up when it should go down. Then the same notes came again, worse.

Rowan sat on the third step down, with his back to us. His elbows were on his knees. His lips were pressed together. He hummed the Ball waltz, as quietly as a man who does not want to wake a baby. His boot tapped on the stone. One, two, three. One, two, three. Every fourth bar the boot stopped. He said "ah" very softly. Then he started again.

I have seen Rowan lift a fallen beam. I have seen him argue with a goat. I had never seen him quiet.

"Rowan," said Seraphine.

His head came up. His face went red from the collar up. It was a clean, even red, like a glass being filled.

"I wasn't doing anything!"

"You were humming."

"That's not doing something."

"It was the waltz," said Felix. "The second half. You skip the turn."

"I know I skip the turn!" Rowan dropped his voice and looked down the stairs. "I asked Clara. Yesterday, after the kitchen. She said yes before I finished the question. I think she was being kind. Now I have five days to learn to walk in a square."

"You don't have to be good," said Seraphine. "You only have to be loud and brave."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

Behind me, the stair creaked.

* * *

I knew the steps before I turned. They were quick and even, a little sharp on the heel. She had come up from the yard. Her Committee coat was buttoned to the throat. She held a small black notebook in her left hand.

Lysette had heard it. I could see it on her face. It was careful and blank, like a door someone has just locked from the inside. I did not know how much she had heard. It was enough.

"Good morning," she said.

"Morning!" said Rowan, too loudly. "We weren't doing anything."

"I know. I'm not here for you." She turned. Her eyes landed on Seraphine and stayed there. "Your Highness. Your collar."

Seraphine put a hand to her neck. "What's wrong with my collar?"

"It's crooked."

"It's—" Seraphine looked down. The left point of her collar sat a finger's width higher than the right. You had to stand in the right spot, with the light behind you, to see it. "That's nothing."

"It's a Dress Code violation. Rule Fourteen. Collars must lie flat and level." Lysette had already opened the notebook. Her pencil was moving. "Name, year, offense."

"You know my name."

"For the record."

"You have never written me up!"

"I have never seen it crooked."

"You walked past me yesterday, and I had flour in my hair!"

"That was a kitchen, not a hall." The pencil stopped. It started again, a little harder. "I need your signature."

Seraphine took the notebook. She read the line. Then she looked up at Lysette. I can only call her face joy. It was the face of a child who learns that the thing under the bed is real.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, this is wonderful."

"Please sign it."

"I will. I'll put it in a frame."

"You will not put it in a frame."

"I'll hang it in the Ball hall. Next to the lanterns."

"Sign it, Seraphine."

It was the first time I had heard Lysette use her name without the title. She seemed to notice it as she said it. A very small crack ran across her face. She fixed it at once. But it had been there.

The pencil slipped out of her fingers. It rolled across the landing. Felix had waited all week for a job. He bent down, picked it up and gave it back with a short bow.

"Thank you," said Lysette. She did not look at him.

"You're welcome," said Felix. Then he said to Seraphine, "Sign it. She's going to cry if you don't."

"I am not—"

"Not you. The first-year on the ladder. He's been listening."

We all looked down. The boy on the ladder had stopped hanging the lantern. He had stopped doing anything. He held the hook in one hand. His mouth was a little open.

"Back to work," said Lysette.

He went back to work so fast that he nearly fell off.

Then the stair creaked again. Isolde came up from the yard.

I should tell you about Isolde. She is the president of the cooking club, a Year Three. Since the day of the flour, she had looked like someone coming home from a long war. She held a roll of paper under one arm. She took us all in with one tired look. She saw the Princess with the notebook. She saw the vice-chair with the pencil. She saw the Dawn Boy, red to the ears. She saw Felix, who looked at the ceiling. She saw me.

"The bill for the kitchen," she said, to no one. "From the Bursar."

"We'll pay," said Rowan.

"You can't pay." She unrolled it. It was a very long piece of paper. "It says Lance Nine owes the school four hundred and twelve crowns. That's for beams, two ovens, a mixing bowl that I'm told was historic, and—" She squinted. "'Emotional damage to the pantry.'"

"The pantry has feelings?" said Rowan.

"The pantry has me," said Isolde, with great dignity. She rolled the bill up. "It isn't due until after the Ball. The Bursar likes romance. He says no one should be sued before a dance." She looked at Seraphine. She looked at Lysette. She looked at the notebook. "Is someone being written up? Can I do it too? I'd like to write up the Bursar."

No one answered.

"No," she said. "I thought not." She went down the stairs again with the bill under her arm. I thought it was the most useful thing anyone had done all morning.

Seraphine signed the notebook. She gave it back with a flourish. Lysette tore out the page. She folded it once, then twice. She put it in her coat. I don't know why she kept it. A Committee copy lives in the Committee book.

"Five days," said Seraphine to me as she turned to go. She had recovered. Her chin was up again. "The offer stands, Wren. Roof or no roof. I'll leave you a plate."

"Of the little smoked things?"

"I'll leave you two."

She went down. Felix hesitated for a second. Then he went with her, one step behind. I heard her say, "Did you really sigh?" He said, "It was a cough." Rowan got up. For a moment I thought he would say something, because his mouth opened. He looked at Lysette. He looked at the ground. Then he ran down the stairs, humming, still wrong.

That left the two of us on the landing. There was a ladder. There was a boy who was very carefully not listening.

"Lysette," I said.

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"Good." She put the notebook in her pocket. Her hand was steady. "I have rounds."

She went up the stairs, not down. That was the wrong way for rounds. I did not mention it.

* * *

She found me on the roof that night. I had known she would.

I had been there since sunset. The wind was cold but dry. The garden was doing what it does. It stayed green in the wrong month. The rosemary had a new shoot. A thistle stood by the water barrel. I thought it was proud.

I heard the trapdoor. I did not turn around. I had learned that. If you turn, she stops climbing.

She came up slowly. She carried a watering can in one hand. She stood at the top of the ladder for a moment. Then she walked past me to the beds. She began to water the lilies that were not Clara's, in a very straight line.

"It's not dry," I said.

"I know."

"You watered it yesterday."

"Eden. Please."

I closed my mouth. She kept pouring. The can emptied, and she held it tipped over until the last drops fell. Then she put it down next to the barrel and sat on the bench. She sat at the far end. I was at the near end. There was a hand's width between us. Neither of us touched it.

"I don't have a partner," she said.

"Okay."

"I haven't had one all week. I told Seraphine I hadn't decided. That wasn't true. I knew I wouldn't ask anyone. And no one would ask me, because I'm the girl who writes up the Princess."

"Someone might."

"Who? You?"

"I'll be on the roof."

"I know," said Lysette. I could not tell if she was laughing. "I'm so sorry for you. It must be hard to be so easy to guess."

She pulled up her knees and rested her chin on them. I had never seen her do that in daylight. Far below us, one window in the east wing was lit. A thin sound came out of it. It was a violin, tuning. Someone practiced the waltz.

"Corentin took me to the Ball," she said. "Every year before he went to the Court. He hated dancing. He counted under his breath the whole time. I stepped on him on purpose. He said, 'Lysette, the beat.'" She was quiet for a while. "I haven't been since."

My left hand lay in my lap. I looked at the glove. I looked at nothing.

"That sounds like a good brother," I said.

"He was a very annoying one."

I said nothing. That was not kindness. Every sentence I had was either a lie or a knife. I had decided long ago which one I could live with. So I sat. The violin tried the same four bars again. It got them wrong. It stopped.

"You're not going to say anything useful," she said.

"No."

"That's a relief. Everyone else is useful at me." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. She did it fast, as if she were hitting a fly. "Rowan asked Clara."

"I know."

"I'm glad. I am. She's kind, and he is, and they'll walk in a square. It will be sweet and awful. I'm not sad." She took a breath. "Why do I keep saying it as if someone asked?"

I said nothing. She talked for a long time after that. It was about nothing much. The garden. The Committee's money. A girl in Year Two who cried in the office because her sister had gone to the front. Her mother, who had written twice this week about the papers. Her voice went up and down and sideways. I stayed. I did not look at her when it cracked. I did not look away either. Around the eleventh bell, she ran out, all at once, like a tap. She sat with her hands loose around her knees.

The cold had come into the bench. My rib hurt. I would have stayed another hour.

"Thank you for not being useful," said Lysette.

"Any time."

She stood. She picked up the watering can and held it against her chest. It was empty. Her jaw had changed. I knew that jaw. She wore it when she had decided something against her own good sense.

"I'm going," she said.

"To the Ball?"

"Yes. On my own. In a dress. I'll walk in, in front of everyone, and stand in the middle of the hall." She lifted her chin. "To prove I don't care."

"Prove it to who?"

"Anyone who's looking."

She went down the ladder with the can. I heard her steps on the stairs. They were quick and even. Then the door shut, and only the violin was left.

I sat there a while. The thistle had not moved. In five days, five hundred people would look at her. All of them would be inside. And I would be on the wrong side of the glass.
