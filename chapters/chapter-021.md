# The Crooked Collar

"Say it again, but slower," said Felix.

"It's the same words, Felix," said Seraphine. "Slower won't change them."

We were on the landing of the Long Stair, where the morning crowd thins out and the stone is cold enough to feel through your boots. Five days to the Ball. Banners were going up in the courtyard below. A first-year was hanging from a ladder, losing a fight with a lantern hook.

Seraphine had come up the steps two at a time and planted herself in front of me. Now she was holding her chin high, with her hands behind her back, like a girl about to recite.

"Eden Wren," she said. "Will you come to the Lantern Ball with me? As a joke. Mostly."

"Which part isn't the joke?" I said.

"The part where you say yes."

I looked at Felix. He was studying the banister as if it had said something rude about his family.

"I'm going to decline," I said.

"You haven't even heard the offer!"

"I heard it. You said it twice."

"There's a *second* half. I get you a seat by the window. I get you a plate of the little smoked things. I stand next to you so nobody else asks you to dance." She counted it on her fingers. "That's a very good offer. That's practically a rescue."

"I'll be on the roof."

"On the *roof?*"

"I like the roof."

"It's a ball, Eden. It's not an exam. You can't just skip it and say you were studying."

"I wasn't going to say I was studying."

"What were you going to say?"

"Nothing. I'd just be on the roof."

Seraphine turned to Felix, slowly. "Did you tell him?"

"Tell him what?" said Felix.

"That I was going to ask."

"No."

"Somebody told him. He's not even surprised."

"I'm surprised," I said. "I've just got a flat face."

Felix gave me a long, level look. I gave him one back. In four years he had never once sold me out, and I was fairly sure this wasn't the start of it. But a thing like that is easier to check than to trust, and checking cost nothing.

He raised both hands a little. "I said you'd say no," he told Seraphine. "I told her you'd say no yesterday, Eden. That's all I said."

"He said it with *pity*," said Seraphine.

"I didn't."

"You did. You sighed."

"I don't sigh," said Felix, and then he did, and the sound came out of him like a dropped book.

A little way off, there was a different noise.

I'd been hearing it for a minute without placing it. A thin hum, a few notes long, going up when it should have gone down. Then the same notes again, worse.

Rowan was sitting on the third step down with his back to us. He had his elbows on his knees and his lips pressed close together, and he was humming the Ball waltz at about the volume of a man trying not to wake a baby. His boot tapped it out on the stone. One, two, three. One, two, three. Every fourth bar the boot stopped, and he said "ah," very softly, and started again.

I have seen Rowan lift a fallen beam. I've seen him argue with a goat. I'd never seen him quiet.

"Rowan," said Seraphine.

His head came up. He went red from the collar upward, a clean even red, like someone filling a glass.

"I wasn't doing anything!"

"You were humming."

"That's not doing something."

"It was the waltz," said Felix. "The second half. You skip the turn."

"I know I skip the turn!" Rowan lowered his voice again, glancing down the stairs. "I asked Clara. Yesterday, after the kitchen. She said yes before I'd finished the question. I think she was being kind. Now I've got five days to learn how to walk in a square."

"You don't have to be good," said Seraphine. "You only have to be loud and committed."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

Behind me, the stair creaked.

* * *

I knew the footsteps before I turned. Quick, even, a little too sharp on the heel. She'd come up from the courtyard with her Committee coat buttoned to the throat and a small black notebook in her left hand.

Lysette had heard it. I could tell by her face, which had the careful, blank look of a door somebody has just locked from the inside. I don't know how much she'd heard. Enough.

"Good morning," she said.

"Morning!" said Rowan, too loudly. "We weren't doing anything."

"I know. I'm not here for you." She turned, and her eyes landed on Seraphine and stayed there. "Your Highness. Your collar."

Seraphine put a hand up to her neck. "What's wrong with my collar?"

"It's crooked."

"It's—" Seraphine looked down. The left point of her collar was sitting a finger's width higher than the right. You'd have needed to be standing in the right spot, with the light behind you, to notice. "That's *nothing*."

"It's a Dress Code violation. Rule Fourteen. Collars shall lie flat and level." Lysette had already opened the notebook. The pencil was moving. "Name, year, offence."

"You know my name."

"For the record."

"You've never written me up!"

"I've never seen it crooked."

"You walked past me yesterday, and I had *flour* in my hair!"

"That was a kitchen, not a corridor." The pencil stopped. It started again, with a little more force. "I'll need your signature."

Seraphine took the notebook. She read the line. Then she looked up at Lysette with an expression I can only describe as joy. It was the face of a child who has been told, finally, that the thing under the bed is real.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, this is *wonderful*."

"Please sign it."

"I will. I'll frame it."

"You will not frame it."

"I'll hang it in the Ball hall. Next to the lanterns."

"Sign it, Seraphine."

It was the first time I'd heard Lysette use her name without the title. She seemed to notice it as she said it. A very small crack went across her face, and she put it back together at once. But it had been there.

The pencil slipped out of her fingers. It rolled across the landing. Felix, moving like a man who'd been waiting all week for a task, stooped, picked it up and handed it back to her with a short bow.

"Thank you," said Lysette, not looking at him.

"You're welcome," said Felix, and then, to Seraphine, "Sign it. She's going to cry if you don't."

"I am *not*—"

"Not you. The first-year on the ladder. He's been listening."

We all looked down. The boy on the ladder had stopped hanging the lantern. He'd stopped doing anything. He was watching, with the lantern hook in one hand and his mouth slightly open.

"Back to work," said Lysette.

He went back to work so fast he nearly fell off.

At that point the stair creaked a second time, and Isolde came up from the courtyard.

I should explain Isolde. She is the president of the cooking club, a Year Three, and ever since the day of the flour she had looked like someone returning from a long war. She was carrying a roll of paper under one arm, and she took us all in with one tired glance: the Princess with the notebook, the vice-chair with the pencil, the Dawn Boy red to the ears, Felix with his eyes on the ceiling, me.

"The invoice for the kitchen," she said, to nobody. "From the Bursar."

"We'll pay," said Rowan.

"You can't pay." She unrolled it. It was a very long piece of paper. "It says here Lance Nine owes the academy four hundred and twelve crowns, for beams, two ovens, a mixing bowl that I'm told was *historic*, and—" She squinted. "'Emotional damages to the pantry.'"

"The pantry has emotions?" said Rowan.

"The pantry has *me*," said Isolde, with enormous dignity. She rolled the invoice up. "It's not due until after the Ball. The Bursar is a romantic. He says no one should be sued before a dance." She looked at Seraphine, and at Lysette, and at the notebook. "Is somebody being written up? Can I do it too? I'd like to write up the Bursar."

Nobody answered.

"No," she said. "I thought not." And she went down the stairs again, with the invoice under her arm, and I thought it was the most useful thing anyone had done all morning.

Seraphine signed the notebook. She handed it back with a flourish. Lysette tore the page out, folded it once, folded it twice, and put it in her coat. I don't know why she kept it. A Committee copy lives in the Committee book.

"Five days," said Seraphine, to me, as she turned to go. She had recovered. She had her chin up again. "The offer stands, Wren. Roof or no roof. I'll leave a plate."

"Of the little smoked things?"

"I'll leave you two."

She went down. Felix, after the smallest hesitation, went with her, a step behind, and I heard her say "Did you really *sigh?*" and him answer "It was a cough." Rowan got up, and for one moment I thought he was going to say something, because his mouth opened. He looked at Lysette. He looked at the ground. Then he took off down the stairs at a trot, humming, still wrong.

That left the two of us on the landing, and a ladder, and a boy who was very carefully not listening.

"Lysette," I said.

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"Good." She put the notebook in her pocket. Her hand was steady. "I have rounds."

She went up the stairs instead of down. That was the wrong direction for rounds. I didn't mention it.

* * *

She found me on the roof that night, as I'd known she would.

I'd been there since sunset. The wind was cold but dry, and the garden was doing the thing it does, which is to stay green in the wrong month. The rosemary had a new shoot. There was a thistle by the water barrel that I suspected of being proud.

I heard the trapdoor and didn't turn around. I'd learned that. If you turn, she stops climbing.

She came up slowly, with a watering can in one hand. She stood at the top of the ladder for a moment. Then she walked past me, to the beds, and started watering the lilies that weren't Clara's, in a very straight line.

"It's not dry," I said.

"I know."

"You watered it yesterday."

"Eden. Please."

I closed my mouth. She went on pouring. The can emptied, and she kept holding it, tipped over, until the last drops fell. Then she set it down next to the barrel and sat on the bench. It was the far end. I was at the near end. There was a hand's width of space between us, and neither of us touched it.

"I don't have a partner," she said.

"Okay."

"I haven't had one all week. I told Seraphine I hadn't decided. That wasn't true. I knew I wasn't going to ask anyone, and nobody was going to ask me, because I'm the girl who writes up the Princess."

"Somebody might."

"Who? You?"

"I'll be on the roof."

"I know," said Lysette, and I couldn't tell if she was laughing. "I'm very sorry for you. It must be hard, being so predictable."

She pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them. It was a thing I'd never seen her do in daylight. Below us, a long way down, one window in the east wing was lit, and a thin sound came up out of it. A violin, tuning. Somebody practising the waltz.

"Corentin used to take me to the Ball," she said. "Every year before he went to the Court. He hated dancing. He'd count under his breath the whole time, and I'd step on him on purpose, and he'd say 'Lysette, the *beat.*'" She was quiet for a bit. "I haven't been since."

My left hand was in my lap. I looked at the glove. I looked at nothing in particular.

"That sounds like a good brother," I said.

"He was a very annoying one."

I didn't say anything. That wasn't kindness. It was that every sentence I had was either a lie or a knife, and I had decided a long time ago which kind I could live with and which I couldn't. So I sat. The violin tried the same four bars again, got them wrong, and stopped.

"You're not going to say anything useful," she said.

"No."

"That's a relief. Everyone else is useful at me." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, quickly, as if she were swatting a fly. "Rowan asked Clara."

"I know."

"I'm glad. I am. She's kind, and he is, and they'll walk in a square, and it'll be sweet and awful. I'm not *sad.*" She drew a breath. "Why do I keep saying it as if somebody asked?"

I said nothing. She talked for a long time after that, about nothing much. The garden. The Committee's budget. A girl in Year Two who had cried in the registry because her sister had been sent to the front. Her mother, who had written twice this week about the papers. Her voice went up and down and sideways. I stayed. I didn't look at her when it cracked, and I didn't look away either. Somewhere around the eleventh bell she ran out, all at once, the way a tap does, and sat there with her hands loose around her knees.

The cold had got into the bench. My rib hurt. I'd have stayed another hour.

"Thank you for not being useful," said Lysette.

"Any time."

She stood. She picked up the watering can and held it against her chest, empty. Her jaw had changed. I knew that jaw. It was the one she wore when she had decided something against her own better judgment.

"I'm going," she said.

"To the Ball?"

"Yes. On my own. In a *dress.* I'm going to walk in, in front of everyone, and stand in the middle of the hall." She lifted her chin. "To prove I don't care."

"Prove it to who?"

"Anyone who's looking."

She went down the ladder with the can. I heard her footsteps on the stairs, quick, even, until the door shut and there was only the violin again.

I sat there a while. The thistle hadn't moved. In five days five hundred people would be looking at her, and all of them would be inside, and I would be on the wrong side of the glass.
