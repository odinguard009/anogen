# Blue Hour at the Dye-Works

"How many vats?"

The foreman asked it the same way every evening. I knew because I'd lain on a rafter above him since the second bell and heard him ask it twice already, to two different shifts. He was a heavy man with blue arms up to the elbow. He had a tally stick in one hand and no smile at all.

"Fourteen," said the line of workers below.

"How many are lit?"

"Nine."

"How many are not?"

"Five."

"Good," said the foreman. "Back to it."

That was the whole ceremony. Fourteen answers, one stick, and a bell that rang for tea. No one looked up. A woman near the door yawned. A boy of about twelve scratched his nose with a wrist stained the color of a bruise.

It looked like a dye-works. It smelled like one: wet wool, alum, a sour sweetness from the vats. I'd been watching the place for an hour, and that was what bothered me. Everything was too ordinary. No one in the room was nervous, and that is not normal when three armed men are standing at the doors.

"Your face is doing the thing," said Vesk, from the dark of my shadow.

"I'm wearing a mask."

"Your *mask* is doing the thing. It's got that look. Like a closed door that's thinking."

I let that go. The Mantle's hood and mask were all I'd worn tonight. Full plate would have rung on every beam. This was the Black Order's face, a black cloth with nothing on it, and I'd put it on under the roof of the Duskwater Dyeworks four days before the Ball because Ysolde's note had been short.

*Tannery cell moved. Lower city, dye-works, east of the bell market. Break it before the Ball. No kills.*

The second line was underlined. She rarely does that. She thinks it makes her look emotional.

"Count," I whispered.

"Twenty-two Cinders on the floor," said Vesk. "Eleven of them kids. The big one at the vats is the foreman, Theobald, an Ember. Two more at the doors with short blades, one in the loft room behind the stair. That's four with weapons. And a door no one goes through."

"Where?"

"Back wall, behind the red vat. They walk around it. Even the kids. They've stepped around a spot in the floor for an hour as if there's a hole there."

"Is there?"

"No. Just a floor." He sounded almost offended. "That's the part I don't like."

I didn't like it either. Whoever ran this place had a rule, and I didn't know what it was. A cell with a rule you can't see is a trap with the lid shut.

Below, the bell rang for tea. The Cinders drifted toward the long table, and I watched what they did with their feet. Every one of them crossed the floor in a curve. They kept their eyes down, and none of them spoke while they were curving.

"Fine," I said. "Wear the second."

"Wear the *second*?" The shadow rippled. "Eden. You know what he's like. Dietrich. He's the worst. He hums."

"He's in the loft room?"

"He's eating a pie in the loft room with his boots on the desk."

"Then you've got a perfect view."

Vesk muttered something unkind about my family. Then the shadow on the rafter beside me stretched down the beam, thin as a rope, and dropped.

* * *

A shadow is a strange thing to wear.

I've seen Vesk do it nine times, and each time it makes the back of my neck itch. He slides into a man's shadow on the wall, and for as long as the light holds, whoever looks at that shadow sees him. His voice comes out of the shape. His walk is the shape's walk. He can't touch a thing. He can only be seen and heard.

Dietrich's shadow, thrown by a lamp in the loft room, stood up on the wall behind the long table. It was a heavy shadow with a square head and one hand on its hip. It had Dietrich's slouch exactly. I didn't know how Vesk had got the slouch.

"Blue Hour," said Dietrich's shadow, in Dietrich's voice, from forty feet away.

Every head turned.

Theobald was already looking at the wall. His tally stick stopped halfway to his belt. "Blue Hour? Now?"

"The Seal's two streets off," said the shadow. "Cart yard. Everyone. Leave the tea."

"Where's the signal?" said Theobald slowly.

I felt Vesk freeze, one beat, in the dark beside me. Then the shadow raised a hand and rubbed its chin, and I understood. He was buying time.

"The signal," said Dietrich's shadow. "How many vats?"

Theobald's eyes narrowed. "Fourteen," he said.

"How many lit?"

"Nine."

"Wrong," said the shadow gently. "It's ten. You're losing your sums, Foreman. There's an Ember in the street who will want to hear how."

The room held its breath. For a moment I thought Theobald would see it. He had the type of face that doesn't miss a thing. But he'd spent three years in a room where everyone said the right numbers, and he'd built his whole day on it. His own man had told him he was wrong. He looked at his stick.

Then he looked at the red vat, the one they all walked around, and I saw his shoulders drop.

"Out," he said. "Cart yard. Everyone. Walk, don't run. Children first."

It was the wrong thing to say, and the right one. The Cinders moved fast. They carried their cups with them, which shows you how a day like that has trained a person. A girl of about fourteen held a smaller girl by the wrist. The twelve-year-old with the stained arm walked to the side door with his head up, trying to look like a veteran.

I dropped from the rafter into the dark behind the red vat.

My boots made no sound. The Mantle's hood was cool against my forehead. I could hear Mirelle's silence somewhere out in the yard, which is a sound if you've lived with it long enough. She would be on the cart. She'd count every head and pass them to Ysolde's wagon at the corner. Eleven kids to a church school in the upper town, with a woman whose job was not to ask them anything.

That left four men.

* * *

The guard at the nearest door turned first. He had the instincts. He heard something in the dark at his back, and he spun with the short blade low and quick, the way you'd gut a fish.

I stepped inside it.

Hinge is a simple form. You pivot on the lead foot like a door, and the attack goes past the space you were in. The lance butt came up under his wrist. The blade hit the floor. I turned him by the shoulder and sat him down against the vat, and he opened his mouth to shout. I closed the shield-door across it. It's a clean sort of thing. A door where there wasn't one, and a sound that doesn't get out.

"One," said Vesk, who'd returned to my shadow. He was out of breath, which isn't possible. "Dietrich's shadow is going to want an apology. He was so *patient*."

"Where's Dietrich?"

"Coming down the stair. He's heard the bell."

He had. I could hear him on the steps, a heavy man in a hurry with a pie still in one hand. He came out into the long room and stopped, because the room was empty. He saw me. He saw the mask.

"Black Order," he said. He wasn't scared. He was almost relieved. "Oh. It's you."

He threw the pie. It wasn't a bad idea. I leaned out of the way, and it hit the wall with a wet crack, and he pulled his blade and charged while I was still watching the pie slide.

I gave him the first exchange for free. I always do. I held the lance at my hip and let his edge slam against it, shoulders first. Big man, wild guard. He swung the second time to the left and the third to the right, and there was a gap in the middle the width of a hand. I put the lance through it.

It's called Draught, because it goes through the narrow place like wind under a door. The point stopped a finger from his throat. His sword stayed up in the air, and he looked at the lance, and he stopped.

"Down," I said.

He went down. I took the sword. "Two," said Vesk. "Number three ran out with the kids. Mirelle says hello."

That left the foreman.

* * *

The foreman didn't run.

I'd expected it. He stood by the red vat with his tally stick in his right hand and his left hand wrapped in a rag to the wrist. The rag was wet. His left forearm, where it showed, was grey.

Not stained. Grey. Fine lines had spread under the skin, pale and branching, like frost on the inside of a window.

Candor, at my hip, did not hum. It went cold. And behind the sour sweetness of the vats there was another smell. Dry. Papery. A grave opened a long time ago. I had smelled it twice in my life, both times on the same kind of thing.

*Veil dust.*

"You know this," said Theobald. He'd seen me stop. He smiled, a tired one. "I thought you might. The Black Order always knows. Is that why you came? You know what they put in us?"

"Put the stick down."

"It's only a stick." He looked at it. "I counted the kids on it. Eleven. I put a notch every time we got one out of the south. I've got the sum to the day." He laughed, softly, without any heat. "Funny. I never let myself count how many I'd lose."

"They're safe. They're in a wagon."

"Are they?" He swayed. The grey on his arm had crept up toward the elbow while we talked. His face had no color at all. "You're the first one that never raised his voice. They said you'd be loud."

"Who said?"

"No one says. It's in the walls." He was holding himself up on the vat now. His breath came short. "I'll tell you the rule. You never asked. Nobody walks over the spot behind the red vat. They put it there on the first day. It's where my brother's buried."

I looked at the floor. Just a floor. He'd made a grave in the only spot no one would cross, and made twenty-two people walk around it, every day, so he'd never have to say so.

I didn't know what to do with that. So I did nothing, and let it be true.

"Sit," I said.

He sat. His knees folded, and he went down slowly with his back against the vat. I stepped nearer, careful, and crouched. His eyes were bright and wrong. I checked his pulse in the neck. Still beating, fast and thin, but it was beating. He wasn't going to die tonight. Not of me.

"Who gave you the graft?" I said.

"A man with a kind voice." He closed his eyes. "I never saw his face. He said he'd fix my arm."

I felt Vesk go still in my shadow.

"Where is he now?"

"Where do you think?" said Theobald, and he began to laugh again, small and wet and delighted. His grey fingers curled around the stick. "You're so clever, Black Order. You came on a quiet night, and you broke us up with a joke about a hum. It's good work. It really is." He opened his eyes and looked straight at the mask, and I saw there was nothing frightened in them at all. "But you're late. The Crown already has the hall."
