# Blue Hour at the Dye-Works

"How many vats?"

The foreman asked it the same way every evening. I knew because I had lain on a rafter above him since the second bell, and I had already heard him ask it twice, to two different shifts. He was a heavy man with blue arms up to the elbow, and he held a tally stick in one hand. He did not smile.

"Fourteen," said the line of workers below.

"How many are lit?"

"Nine."

"How many are not?"

"Five."

"Good," said the foreman. "Back to work."

That was the whole ceremony. There were fourteen answers, one stick and a bell for tea. No one looked up. A woman near the door yawned, and a boy of about twelve scratched his nose with a wrist the color of a bruise.

It looked like a dye-works, and it smelled like one, of wet wool and alum and a sour sweetness from the vats. The light was the last of the evening, gray-blue through high dirty windows. I had watched the place for an hour, and that was what bothered me. Everything was too ordinary. No one in the room was nervous, and that is not normal when three armed men stand at the doors.

"Your face is doing the thing," said Vesk, from the dark of my shadow.

"I'm wearing a mask."

"Your mask is doing the thing. It looks like a closed door that is thinking."

I let that go. Tonight I wore only the black hood and mask, because full plate would ring on every beam. It was the face of the Black Order, a black cloth with nothing on it. I had put it on four days before the Ball, under the roof of the dye-works in the lower city. Ysolde's note had been short.

*Tannery group moved. Lower city, dye-works, east of the bell market. Break it before the Ball. No kills.*

The second line was underlined. She rarely does that, because she thinks it makes her look emotional.

"Count," I whispered.

"Twenty-two Cinders on the floor," said Vesk. "Eleven of them are kids. The big man at the vats is the foreman, Theobald, and he's an Ember. Two more men stand at the doors with short blades, and one is in the loft room behind the stair. That makes four with weapons. And there's one place nobody walks."

"Where?"

"Behind the red vat, on the back wall. For an hour they've all stepped around one spot in the floor, even the kids, as if there were a hole."

"Is there?"

"No. It's just floor." He sounded almost angry. "That's the part I don't like."

I didn't like it either. Whoever ran this place had a rule, and I didn't know the rule. A group with a rule you can't see is a trap with the lid shut.

Below us the bell rang for tea, and the Cinders walked toward the long table. I watched their feet. Every one of them crossed the floor in a curve with their eyes down, and no one spoke while they curved.

"Fine," I said. "Wear the second."

"Wear the second?" The shadow shook. "Eden. You know what he's like. Dietrich is the worst one. He hums."

"Is he in the loft room?"

"He's eating a pie in the loft room with his boots on the desk."

"Then you have a perfect view."

Vesk said something unkind about my family. Then the shadow on the rafter beside me stretched down the beam, thin as a rope, and dropped.

* * *

A shadow is a strange thing to wear.

I have seen Vesk do it nine times, and each time the back of my neck itches. He slides into a man's shadow on the wall, and as long as the light holds, anyone who looks at that shadow sees him. His voice comes out of the shape and he walks the shape's walk. He can't touch anything. He can only be seen and heard.

A lamp in the loft room threw Dietrich's shadow on the wall behind the long table, and it stood up. It was heavy, with a square head and one hand on its hip. It had Dietrich's slouch exactly, and I don't know how Vesk got the slouch.

"Blue Hour," said Dietrich's shadow, in Dietrich's voice, from forty feet away.

Every head turned.

Theobald was already looking at the wall, and his tally stick stopped halfway to his belt. "Blue Hour? Now?"

"The Seal is two streets away," said the shadow. "Go to the cart yard, everyone. Leave the tea."

"What is the signal?" said Theobald slowly.

I felt Vesk freeze for one beat in the dark beside me. Then the shadow raised a hand and rubbed its chin, and I understood that he was buying time.

"The signal," said Dietrich's shadow. "How many vats?"

Theobald's eyes went narrow. "Fourteen," he said.

"How many lit?"

"Nine."

"Wrong," said the shadow gently. "It is ten. You are losing your count, Theobald. There is an Ember in the street, and he will want to hear how."

The room held its breath. For a moment I thought Theobald would see through it, because he had a face that misses nothing. But he had spent three years in a room where everyone said the right numbers, and he had built his whole day on that. Now his own man said he was wrong, and he looked at his stick.

Then he looked at the red vat, the one they all walked around, and I saw his shoulders drop.

"Out," he said. "Cart yard. Everyone. Walk, don't run. Children first."

It was the wrong thing to say, and the right one. The Cinders moved fast, and they took their cups with them, which shows what a day like theirs does to a person. A girl of about fourteen held a smaller girl by the wrist. The boy with the stained arm walked to the side door with his head up, trying to look like an old soldier.

I dropped from the rafter into the dark behind the red vat.

My boots made no sound, and the hood was cool against my forehead. Somewhere out in the yard I could hear Mirelle's silence. It is a sound, if you have lived with it long enough. She would be on the cart, counting every head, and then she would pass them to Ysolde's wagon at the corner. The eleven kids would go to a church school in the upper town, where a woman would take them and ask them nothing.

That left four men.

* * *

The guard at the nearest door turned first. He had good instincts. He heard something in the dark behind him, and he spun with his short blade low and fast, like a man gutting a fish.

I stepped inside the cut.

The Hinge is a simple move. You turn on your front foot like a door, and the attack goes past the place where you were. My lance butt came up under his wrist, and his blade hit the floor. I turned him by the shoulder and sat him down against the vat. He opened his mouth to shout, and I closed the shield door across it. It is a clean trick, a door where there was no door, and a sound that cannot get out.

"One," said Vesk. He was back in my shadow and out of breath, which is not possible. "Dietrich's shadow will want an apology. He was so patient."

"Where is Dietrich?"

"Coming down the stair. He heard the bell."

He had. I could hear him on the steps, a heavy man in a hurry with a pie still in one hand. He came into the long room and stopped, because the room was empty. Then he saw me, and he saw the mask.

"Black Order," he said. He wasn't scared. He was almost glad. "Oh. It's you."

He threw the pie. It was not a bad idea, and I leaned away, and the pie hit the wall with a wet crack. He pulled his blade and charged while I was still watching the pie slide down.

I gave him the first exchange for free, as I always do. I held the lance at my hip, and his blade slammed into it, shoulder first. He was a big man with a wild guard. He swung left the second time and right the third, and that left a gap in the middle as wide as a hand. I put the lance through it.

That move is called the Draught, because it goes through the narrow place like wind under a door. The point stopped a finger from his throat, and his sword hung up in the air. He looked at the lance. Then he stopped.

"Down," I said.

He went down, and I took the sword.

"Two," said Vesk. "Number three ran out with the kids. Mirelle says hello."

That left the foreman.

* * *

The foreman did not run.

I had expected it. He stood by the red vat with his tally stick in his right hand. His left hand was wrapped in a wet rag up to the wrist, and his left forearm, where I could see it, was gray.

It wasn't stained. It was gray. Fine lines had spread under the skin, pale and branching, like frost on the inside of a window.

Candor, at my hip, did not hum. It went cold. Behind the sour sweetness of the vats there was another smell, dry and papery, like a grave opened long ago. I had smelled it twice in my life, and both times it came from the same kind of thing.

*Veil dust.*

"You know this," said Theobald. He had seen me stop, and he smiled a tired smile. "I thought you might. The Black Order always knows. Is that why you came? Do you know what they put in us?"

"Put the stick down."

"It's only a stick." He looked at it. "I counted the kids on it. Eleven. I cut a notch every time we got one out of the south, so I know the number to the day." He laughed softly, with no heat. "Funny. I never let myself count how many I would lose."

"They're safe. They're in a wagon."

"Are they?" He swayed. The gray on his arm had crept toward his elbow while we talked, and his face had no color at all. "You're the first one who never raised his voice. They said you would be loud."

"Who said?"

"No one says. It's in the walls." He held himself up on the vat now, and his breath came short. "I'll tell you the rule, since you never asked. Nobody walks over the spot behind the red vat. We made the rule on the first day. It's where my brother is buried."

I looked at the floor. It was just floor. He had made a grave in the one spot nobody would cross, and twenty-two people walked around it every day, so he never had to say it out loud.

I didn't know what to do with that, so I did nothing. I let it be true.

"Sit," I said.

He sat. His knees folded, and he went down slowly with his back against the vat. I stepped closer and crouched. His eyes were bright and wrong, but the pulse in his neck was still beating, fast and thin. He would not die tonight, and not because of me.

"Who gave you the graft?" I said.

"A man with a kind voice." He closed his eyes. "I never saw his face. He said he would fix my arm."

I felt Vesk go still in my shadow.

"Where is he now?"

"Where do you think?" said Theobald. He began to laugh again, small and wet and happy, with his gray fingers curled around the stick. "You are so clever, Black Order. You came on a quiet night. You broke us up with a joke about a hum. It's good work. It really is." He opened his eyes and looked straight at the mask, and I saw nothing in them that was afraid. "But you're late. The Crown already has the hall."
