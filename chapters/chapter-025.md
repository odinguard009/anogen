# The Glass Floor

I had just caught a falling chandelier, and no one had thanked me. That is the trouble with doing it right. Nothing happens, and so nothing is seen.

Now the hall below was full of gray lamps and the sound of people crying quietly. Forty guests knelt in the middle of the dance floor with their hands on their heads. Around them stood twelve men in ash-gray coats, each with a crossbow pointed at the guests. Six more stood back with their sleeves rolled. I could see gray veins under the skin of their arms, like cracks in old ice.

Lysette had my wrist in both hands. Her nails were in it.

"Why do they know your name?" she said.

"Thornmere is a small place. I am a scholarship boy from there. Maybe they have a list."

"A list."

"The Crowns have lists of everything. I am sure I am on one."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer I have." I was angry, and I kept my voice flat, which she can always tell. I had wanted one quiet night on a roof, and now my cover had a hole in it the size of a house. "Listen to me. There are twelve crossbows, and forty hostages. If your people charge the doors, the bows go off. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Go down the east ladder, out of the yard, round to the north gate. Find the Golden Seal captain. Tell him to hold the doors and not to storm in. Say a man on the roof asked for ten minutes."

"A man on the roof." Her voice cracked on it. "And what will you do in ten minutes?"

"Finish."

She looked at me for a breath. She was shaking, but she was also the vice-chair of the Discipline Committee, and she knew an order when she heard one.

"You will tell me tomorrow," she said. "Everything. All of it."

"One question."

"Everything."

"One question, and I will answer it truthfully."

She stared. Then she picked up her gown and ran for the ladder, and I heard her heels ring on the iron all the way down.

* * *

I stepped back into my shadow.

The cold came up through my boots, then the plates. They closed from my feet to my shoulders, black and flat, and I listened for the sound they did not make. The gauntlets came last. When the left one locked, my fingers were already white to the elbow. The helm came down, and the white thread across the eye slit caught the moon and shone for one second.

That was the price for tonight. It was a big one, and I did not argue with it.

"Roles," said Vesk, from my shadow. He sounded cheerful, and I wanted to hit him. "Gorrath, you take the north doors, from outside. Mirelle takes the rafters. I take the key. Eden does the dramatic part."

"I take the woman in charge," I said.

"That is what I said."

"It is not. You said dramatic."

"Same thing. Wear the mask, walk slowly, look tragic."

"I always look tragic."

"Today I want it bigger," said Vesk.

Gorrath answered from a dark corner, in his own deep voice. "I will say something brave at the doors. I have been practicing."

"Do not practice," I said. "Just open them."

Mirelle came down out of the dark beside me in her own black plate, thin and long-armed, with one narrow blade. She did not say a word. She never does. She pointed twice, once at the bows and once at the lattice above them, and then she was gone along the iron like a drop of oil.

I looked down through the pane again. The moon had made a grid of bars across the floor, dark lines from the iron frame of the roof. Every Ember with a crossbow stood on one. They had not planned it. Men just stand where there is room.

I needed a way to move six hurt men without touching them. I needed a plan. The moon made the bars, the bars were shadows, and shadows were doors.

I knocked on the pane, six times, one for each of the grafted men.

Six black doors opened under their boots. They did not even have time to shout. Their feet went through, and then their knees, and they dropped out of the hall like stones through a trapdoor. The far end of each door was the straw room behind the school kitchen, two floors down. I heard nothing. Later I would find out they had landed in a heap and fallen asleep from shock.

At the same moment, along the rafters, all twelve bowstrings went slack.

Mirelle's work. No one saw her. Twelve Embers looked down at their dead bows and then at each other, and one of them said a word I will not write.

"Who cut them?" he hissed. "Who cut them?"

"No one is up there," said the man beside him. "I looked."

"Then who did it?"

Then the door by the north wall rattled, and Vesk had the bar off in a blink, with Gorrath outside shouting in a very large, very bad voice, "Run! Everyone! Go! Please do not step on me!"

The crowd broke. Forty guests ran for the open doors and out, with Gorrath's huge arms scooping them past like a hedge. A man in a brown coat fell and was pulled up by a woman in a blue dress, and the two of them ran together with their hands clasped, screaming.

That left the middle of the floor, and the man in the center of it.

I broke the pane at my feet with the heel of my boot.

I dropped through the roof into the light. Glass rained down around me, as bright as snow in the gray lamps, and I came down in a crouch on the dance floor on top of a hundred pieces of it. I did not make a sound. This armor never does. I stood up, and the glass under my boots made no noise either, though it must have been crunching a hundred ways. It was like walking in a dream.

I started across.

"Hey," said a young man near the front, in a voice that tried to be hard. "Stop there. Stop, or the girl in green gets a bolt."

"Your bow has no string," I said.

He looked at it. He looked at me. "That is not fair," he said.

Six steps. The Embers near me went still. Seven, eight, and one of them stepped back without knowing he had done it. Then another. A man with a dead crossbow let it fall out of his hand and put his palm against his chest, and his knees looked weak. The gray lamps shook in their holders for no reason that I could see. They were a long way from being brave.

"That is him," whispered someone. "The Black Order. He's alone."

"He's one man!"

"Then why am I cold?" said another.

I kept walking.

On the far side of the circle a girl in pale green stood among the last hostages, with one hand held tight against her chest. It was Seraphine. Her fingers had been shaking all night, and I could see it from ten steps. As I went by her, her hand opened and lay still.

"It stopped," she whispered, to no one, to the glass. "Why did it stop?"

"Just breathe," said the boy beside her, the one who had danced with her all night. He held her sleeve and kept his eyes on the floor. "Do not look up."

Near the far wall, hung with a long old wall cloth, a gray-haired man stood a little apart from the rest. He was not kneeling. Merrow had his head tilted back, like a man in a museum, and he was admiring the weaving. The cloth showed a battle, with a dragon in red thread.

"Ostrevaal," he said, quietly, to no one. "They never got the dragon's eye right. Such a pity."

"Sir, kneel," said a frightened guard beside him.

"In a moment, young man," said Merrow. "I am looking."

I did not stop. I put it somewhere in my head and marked it, and it sat there like a splinter.

* * *

The woman waited for me in the middle of the floor.

She was tall and thin, with a pointed chin and gray-brown hair cropped to her jaw. She wore a long coat of pale metal plates, the color of a window in winter. Veins of frost ran through the plates like cracks, and they glowed faintly along the joints. Where she stood, the room had no sound at all. It was as if a hand had closed around her.

"I am Ilsabet Rorke, Crown of Kaldmark," she said. I saw her mouth move, and I did not hear a word, but I could read it. "You are not the one I called for."

"I was busy."

I could not hear myself either, and I lost a half second to it. I had not known that I could be silent inside her silence.

She smiled, a thin, bitter smile. She raised a long, plain sword in both hands, and frost raced up the blade.

"Where are the rest of you?" she asked. "The Black Order sends a hundred."

"Tonight I am short," I said.

She came in fast.

I did what I always do. I turned side-on, left foot back, with the lance low at my hip, and I let her come. Her first cut hit the knocker ring on my shield and slid. Her second cut went high, and I took it again. She was good, quick on her feet and angry, and she hit like someone who had practiced alone in an empty room for years. Each blow chilled my arm a little more. The Frost that had started in my hand climbed.

"You do not strike back," she said. I read it off her lips. "Are you afraid?"

"No," I said, and she read that off mine. "I am watching."

"For what?"

"Your shoulder."

I did not need to. I had already seen how she moved. Her pale armor pulled her swing high every time, because the veins at her shoulder tightened when she rose. Wherever the glow was strongest, the plate was weakest, and her swing always went through that place.

She went for my head.

I stopped waiting. I lifted the shield over my head, flat, like a door over a doorway, and her blade came down on it with a flash and a hard, ringing bite that I felt in my teeth. Under the shield, my right hand was already moving. my short bright sword drew across the glowing vein at her collar.

It was a clean cut. No wasted turn of the wrist. This was the form I call the Lintel, and it ends a fight in one motion.

The frost went out of her plates. They cracked with a sound like a pond giving way, and the sound came all at once, loud as a slammed gate, because the silence broke with them. I heard the whole hall. I heard the Embers drop their weapons. I heard my own breath in the helm.

Ilsabet went to her knees. Without the plates she was a small, pale woman of twenty-two or so, in a torn gray shirt. Her arms were covered in gray veins. They were fading, and she did not know it.

"Do it," she said. It was her real voice now, rough and low. "Do it. Someone will, and they will not have your manners."

I held my sword at her throat, and the blade made a little white glow on her face.

"No," I said.

"You think this is kind."

"I think you are twenty-two, and I do not need to." That was not the whole truth. I was thinking about a man in a dye-works who said a man with a kind voice had given him a graft. "Who gave you the armor?"

She laughed once, very softly, almost happy. "A man with a kind voice."

"Of course."

The great doors burst open. The Golden Seal came in with lamps and swords, forty guards, with Lysette at the front and her gown dragging.

"Drop it! Everyone down!" shouted the captain.

"We are down!" shouted a guest.

"Not you! The others!" In the noise, the surviving Embers dropped their coats and let themselves be tied.

Ilsabet looked up at me, and for a moment she was just frightened.

"They told me the Black Order never shows mercy," she said.

"They say a lot of things."

"Why spare me?"

"Ask me when you are not tied up."

I stepped back into the shadow of the broken glass, and as I did, I saw him.

Valerius stood among the hostages near the wall. His sleeve was torn from shoulder to wrist, and blood ran down his hand in a thin line and dripped off his fingers onto the floor. He had tried to stop them, I guessed, and had lost. He did not call out. He did not point. He did not say a word to the captain, or to Lysette, or to the soldiers who were already running to him.

He just looked straight at me.
