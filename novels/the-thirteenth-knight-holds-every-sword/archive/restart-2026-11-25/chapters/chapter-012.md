# Chapter 12: Perrin

A wheelbarrow is the best disguise on the plateau. No one has ever looked at a person's face over one.

I had pushed this one thirty yards across the stable yard with a pitchfork laid on top and my hood down. A boy who moves dirty straw is someone else's problem, and that was my whole idea. It was working, too. Two juniors in yellow armbands walked past with a list of names. The shorter one kept saying "Wren, Wren, Wren" like a man who has lost his keys. He went right by the barrow.

I tipped it out at the muck heap and leaned the fork on the wall. Then I went around the back of the tack room, where the roof hangs out over a stack of feed sacks. No one goes near it because of the smell.

Felix was sitting on a sack with his bow case across his knees and a napkin in his lap. He did not look up.

"You're late," he said. "And you smell like a horse."

"The barrow isn't mine."

"I know. It belongs to the stable hand, and he's been looking for it since breakfast." He moved over. "Sit."

I sat on a sack that was lumpy and smelled of old oats. It was the fourth place on the plateau where no one thought to look for me. It was the only one where someone waited. Felix had found it in Year Three and never said how. We ate here on the days we ate. Dust fell from the loft like slow brown rain, and a yellow cat with one bent ear sat nearby and had opinions about us.

He unfolded the napkin, and inside were two dark buns from the north ovens with caraway seeds baked into the crust. They were still a little warm, and the smell was better than anything else in that yard.

"Did you eat today?" said Felix.

"Technically."

"Mm." He gave me the bigger one. Then he turned up his sleeve and showed me where Clara had closed the cut, and there was no line and no pink thread at all. He turned his arm over in the light twice, like a man checking a coin he found in his own pocket.

"I've had cuts that took a month," he said. "I have one on my knee that I'll keep forever. Hers took as long as a sneeze."

"She's a strong healer."

"Is that what you'd call it?" He ate a piece of bun. "Seraphine told me joke number four last night. A horse walks into a hospital."

"Was it good?"

"The horse had a limp. That was the joke. The limp." He thought about it. "I laughed, because that's what you do. She wrote a four on her hand and bowed. I think I did something wrong."

"You're keeping count."

"I'm told it's a scoring system." He picked a seed off the crust and looked at it. "Yesterday, in the maze, my Trail said right. A hundred boots said right. You said left because it felt deeper."

"It was a guess."

"I have been wrong in front of Okoru before, but he never looked at me like that on the way out." He flicked the seed at the cat, and the cat ignored it. "I don't mind being wrong. But my feet said right, you said left, and you won."

"Then I was lucky."

"You're a lot of things. I have never once seen you lucky."

I had no answer to that, and the next thing he said was worse.

The cat came and sat in the square of sun between us, and it washed one paw as if nothing important were happening. Far away, the bell of the low chapel rang once for the half hour, and the stable hand started shouting about his barrow. It would have been a very good lunch. Felix put down his bun the way a man puts down a drink before he gives a toast.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You're going to."

"I tried to think of a way you wouldn't dodge. I couldn't. So." He looked at his hands, not at me. They were long, pale, careful hands, the kind that tie fishing flies. "Why won't you let anyone near you? I don't mean only me. Rowan has tried since the first week, with food and questions and all his little tricks. And you step away from him like he's a roof about to fall."

"Felix."

"Let me finish. Lysette has a timetable. I think you've been counting how many days she tried to catch you. I saw you counting." He was quiet for a second. "I have never asked you anything in three years. Have I?"

"No."

"Do you know why?"

I did not answer, because I did not think it was a real question, and I was busy looking at my own boots.

"Because you never told me there was a locked door," said Felix gently. "You just stood in front of it."

* * *

I did not sleep that afternoon. I tried, and that is worse.

I lay on the roof of the Old Observatory with my coat over my face, while the sun came hot through the wool and the thyme smelled sharp. The war came anyway, the way it always does, at the edge of the blanket. There were banners somewhere and a river that was the wrong color, and a row of men in black armor stood three deep in a field. They had stood there for a very long time, and they were waiting for me to come and say what for.

My old teacher was far away and busy in the half-dream, and he did not look around. The Dragon did.

*You didn't answer the boy,* said Sepiroth. It was the dry voice he used when he wanted to seem above it all.

*Leave it.*

*I heard the whole thing. A landlord hears things. Very nice young man, like a sheepdog. And you sat there with a bun in your hand for as long as it takes to boil an egg. I counted.*

*Leave it,* I said again.

*I am only a tenant here,* said Sepiroth. *I only say what I see.*

*Then see something else.*

He did, which was rare. The afternoon slid into evening, and I never found one thing to say.

I had once had a speech for this. I was fourteen and new, and I had a list of rules written inside my wrist. Never be at the center of anything. Never be at the edge either, because people look at edges. Never let one person know three things about you.

I had already used up everyone I let close. The king had a spy who kept a locked cabinet, and I had a family in a house I could not even visit without a letter. A scholarship boy was not supposed to have a family, so every road to that house ran past that fact.

The speech did not cover a boy with a bun.

I got up at dusk, stiff, with caraway still in my teeth. Under the apple tree, Lysette's garden had been watered. She had come while I slept and gone away again without a note. She never left notes.

* * *

The next day I was at the sacks before him.

It was gray and cool that morning, and the yard smelled of wet straw. He came around the corner carrying a cloth bundle and stopped when he saw me, and he said nothing, which was Felix's best talent. He sat down and opened the cloth. Two buns.

"Eaten today?" he said.

"No."

He looked at me. I had never said no before.

"I'm going to answer what you asked," I said. "It will be a bad answer. Let me finish."

"All right."

"I can't give you mine." It came out flat, and I did not want that. I tried again, and it came out flat again. "You asked why I don't let people close. I would like to. That's the first half. The second half is that some things about me are not safe for you to hold. Not because you would tell. Because you would have to *carry* them. I've watched what that does. It makes people careful, and then it makes them sad."

Felix looked at the cat. The cat had come back.

"I'm not going to tell you what," I said. "And I won't make up something that sounds close enough. That would be worse."

"That's it?"

"That's it. I'm sorry."

"Mm." He broke his bun in half and held out the bigger piece. "Here."

"Felix."

"Perrin."

"What?"

"It's my middle name. Felix Perrin Ashgrove. It was my mother's grandfather's name, and he was a very bad poet. Don't use it. I have never said it to anyone who wasn't giving me a blessing or a bill." He pushed the bread another inch. "Now you know all of mine. I thought someone should."

I took the bun. It was an ordinary bun, but my hand was not steady, and he pretended not to see. That was another of his talents.

In my kind of work, a name is not just a name. If someone gives you the middle one, he has given you the house, the key and whatever is in the cellar. If I were a man who used people, that name could be a rope to tie up half a battlefield. He did not know that. He only knew it was a poet and a bun.

"I can't give you mine back," I said.

"I know."

"I want to."

"I know that too." He ate. "It was never a trade."

The yard was quiet now. Even the stable hand had stopped shouting, so I could hear the cat chewing on something it should not have found.

"Did he find his barrow?" I asked.

"I think so," said Felix. "He's yelling at someone else now."

I ate the bun. I am not sure what else I did.

It could have ended there, with the cat and the caraway and Felix not talking. It would have been the best ending of anything in a year. It did not end there. Around the corner of the tack room came Lysette Hollen at a fast Committee pace, in her full uniform, with her clipboard under one arm. In the other hand she held two wrapped lunches.

She stopped when she saw us. Felix lifted one hand off his knee, politely, and I think that was his whole greeting.

"Ashgrove," said Lysette.

"Hollen."

That was all they said to each other. It was more than they had planned.

"Wren," she said. Her cheeks were pink from the fast walk, and a strand of hair had come loose under her cap.

"Hollen."

"A stable boy told me where you are. For eleven days I tried to work out where you eat. On the eleventh day, I thought of *asking.*" She held out one of the parcels, which were wrapped in brown paper and string and smelled of onions. She did not look at me. She looked at a spot six inches above the cat. "It's Committee efficiency. If I know you are fed, I don't have to count you. It saves a form."

"Lysette."

"You are the kind who runs away," she said.

She put the parcel on the sack next to me and tucked the other under her arm. That one was hers, so she had planned to eat lunch too, just not with us. She moved the clipboard to her other arm, and that took longer than it should have.

"There's a rota," she told the cat. "I put you on it. Wherever you are."

Then she walked away around the corner, with her back so straight it must have hurt.

Felix watched the corner for a while.

"Rota," he said.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"Mm," said Felix. He ate the last of his bun, and for the first time since Year Three I saw him smile at something that was not a joke at all.
