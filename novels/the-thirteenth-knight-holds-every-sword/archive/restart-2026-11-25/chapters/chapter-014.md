# Chapter 14: Seven for Seven

> "An absence is a debt. Debts are collected." - Discipline Committee motto, unofficial

"That's the fourth runner," said Felix, from the apple tree.

My eyes stayed shut. I lay on my back in the thyme by the dome, with my coat folded under my head and my boots crossed. It was warm, and the herbs smelled like a kitchen. It was the ninety-first minute of what would be my seventh nap in seven days, if no one ruined it. I could hear Felix fine. He had the voice of a man reading a very short menu.

"Where?"

"The bell tower. He's on the roof with a spyglass, looking at the dormitory."

"Good."

"He has a little flag. It says *Committee.*" Leaves rustled. "I think someone told him to wave it."

"Good."

"I'm going to stop telling you these if you only say *good.*"

That sounded like a good sign, so my eyes stayed shut.

No one tells you that a perfect record is this tiring. On day one, I lay down at noon under a table in the library annex with a sign on my chest that said *Studying*. Lysette walked in at six minutes past twelve with a runner on each arm and a warrant in her hand. On day two, the warrant became two warrants. On day three, there was a wire across the gap in the boiler-room wall, and I had to start taking notes. It was the only time in my life I was glad to have a notebook. *Nap 3. Boiler room. Thirty-eight minutes. Not disturbed, except by a bucket.* It was signed *E. Wren*, and the bucket was the witness, because there was no one else.

Six in a row. One more, and I would hold the school record for naps under Committee attack. It was not on the Ladder, so there was no pin for it. There was also a bet with Felix, and the prize was a month of carrying his Strategy book. I would rather not talk about that.

"Seraphine's joke," said Felix. "Number nineteen. Do you want it?"

"No."

"A man goes to a baker and asks for a loaf. The baker says, 'I'll see what I can rise to.'" He thought about it. "I laughed. Then I knew I should not laugh until I meant it, and I could not say that without sounding sad. So I told her I *chuckled.* She wrote nineteen on her hand and drew a little box around it."

"You're a double agent."

"I'm a man with a lunch plan." He did not sound angry. "Two lunches go to the roof door at noon, in a basket on a hook. One is for you. I don't know who the other is for, and I was told not to ask. In return, I tell the Committee you're probably in the library. That is a lie. And I tell you the Committee is probably in the library. That one is true. It's a good deal."

"For who?"

"For everyone who wants to eat."

"What's in the basket today?"

"Cheese, bread and one pear."

"Which of us is the pear for?"

"I don't know," said Felix. "I was told not to ask."

I opened my eyes a little and looked up through the apple leaves. The roof of the Old Observatory was mostly sky. A dome of old green copper stood at one end, with a broken telescope sticking out of it like a bored finger. The herb beds made a rough cross of raised boards around the apple tree, which was small and bent by the wind and had about nine apples. Lysette had told someone she was saving them for jam. Felix had one foot on a branch and his back against the trunk, and the sky behind him was hard and pale, with one cloud in it. It was a very good day for sleeping.

"Fifth runner," said Felix.

I shut my eyes again. "Count the week with me."

"Do I have to?"

"Day four," I said. "The dormitory."

"A second-year under your bed for six hours," said Felix. "With a whistle."

"Day five, a cellar. Day six, the boiler room again. And a runner on the Long Stair with a coil of *rope.* No one ever told me about the rope."

"She has seven officers on the list," said Felix. "She has one in real life, and she's throwing that one at the whole plateau."

The week had been like a bad bridge, and Felix's lunches were the only good part. I had stopped asking what he told her. Only this morning had I noticed something else. She had never once asked him about the roof.

In seven days, not one runner had climbed the roof stair.

I lay there and let that sink in. It was a narrow stair with forty-one steps up the east side of the Old Observatory, behind a door with a rusty latch. Only three people had ever used it. One was a headmistress with a bad eye, one was a girl who cried, and one was me. The door had a notice on it, and on day one I had walked past and read it without reading it, the way you do. *CLOSED FOR REPAIR. Masonry. By order.* It was in a square, neat hand, in Committee ink, a red-brown like dried blood. It was the same ink and the same hand as warrants one through nine.

The notice looked like the bursar's, but the bursar never repaired anything. He wrote notes about repairing things, in pencil, and then he lost the pencil.

"Felix," I said.

"Mm."

"The sign on the stair."

"Which sign?"

"The one that says the roof is being repaired."

"I didn't know there was one." He was quiet a moment. "I would say the roof is in good repair. Except the telescope."

"It's been there since day one. I thought she was hunting me, with the warrants and the runners and the whole war." I looked at the sky. "She's been keeping them off the stair."

"Wren," said Felix slowly. "Think about it. That's either the nicest thing anyone has done for you, or the best trap in history."

"It can be both."

"Ah," said Felix.

"For seven days."

"Ah," said Felix again, in a different voice. Then he spoke much more quietly. "Eden. Someone is on the stair."

"Don't move," I whispered. "Don't breathe. Be a tree."

"I'm already a tree," he whispered back. "I'm a very good tree."

My body was already still, because that was the first thing it did. My breath slowed and my face looked like a sleeping face. I heard the latch lift on the old iron, and I heard the hinge say what it always said. Then there was nothing for as long as it takes a person to stand in a doorway and not decide.

Then I heard light, slow steps on the boards. Felix said nothing at all, and I think the cloud went away. She went past the dome and across the boards of the first bed. Not a yard from my head, I heard the clunk of the little tin watering can. I had knocked it over three weeks ago and put it back carefully, but a thumb's width from where she kept it.

She stood over me. I knew because her shadow turned the red behind my eyelids a dull brown.

I had had the right line ready for a month. It was a small apology, half a joke. *I was only resting here, I didn't know it was yours.* It was a good line, and I had even practiced my surprise.

"You're in the rosemary," said Lysette.

That was not what I expected. She said it very low, to the plants. I had thought I was lying in the thyme, so I must have rolled. My left hand rested in the herb bed. One finger touched a wooden plant label that had been broken in half and had a year of weather on it. Someone had mended it with a twist of copper wire and a short stick, three weeks ago, and no one had asked me to.

She was looking at the label, I thought, and then at my hand. I did not open my eyes. The quiet went on. I heard her crouch, and I heard a fingernail tick once on the copper wire. Then I heard the can move, a thumb's width, back to where it belonged.

"Seven days," she said, to no one. "I sent runners to the boiler room *twice.*"

Above me the leaves moved, and I counted them. The apple tree leaned over us politely, with Felix not breathing in it. If she said my name, I would have to answer, and then my record would be spoiled. I would carry the book for a month, and probably more.

She did not say my name.

Something heavy and warm came down over my shoulders. It was her coat, dark wool, still warm from her, with a lining of Committee yellow. It smelled of ink and chalk and a little mint. She tucked the collar around my neck, fast, the way you do something you wish you were not doing. Then her steps went away toward the dome.

From the tree, Felix made a tiny sound. It was the sound of a man trying very hard not to say anything.

The can scraped. She was watering the beds from left to right, like someone who could do it in the dark. Under her coat, with my eyes shut, I lay still. My heart made a noise I hoped the soil would soak up. Nine splashes of water fell, then a pause, and then the sound of paper.

One eye opened a little. I had earned it.

She knelt by the herb bed in her shirt sleeves, with her back to me. She held a folded sheet with a stamp at the top. It was today's warrant, with a number and a clause and a name, every line filled in and ready to go, in a hand that did not shake.

She tore it across, and then she tore it across again. Four pieces. She held them for a moment in her flat hand, as if she were weighing them. Then she pushed each piece into the soil with her thumb, one at a time, in a row, the way you plant seed.

She wiped her hand on her skirt and sat on the edge of the herb bed with her knees up. Her face was toward the dome and the sky and the long gold slope of Solmarch going down to the river. She did not go. She stayed there with her chin on her arms and the little tin can beside her. She looked like a person who had come to sit with something and had decided it would do.

After a while, she spoke to the dome.

"Three weeks."

I did not move.

"And you never told anyone." It was not a question, and I am not a fool, so I did not answer it.

Behind me, in the tree, very softly, Felix moved his weight onto his other foot.

I stayed where I was, and her coat kept me warm. It was the best nap of my life. I put it in the book, and I wrote not one word about why.
