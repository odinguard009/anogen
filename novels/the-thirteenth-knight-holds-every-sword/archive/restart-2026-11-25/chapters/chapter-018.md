# Chapter 18: What the Bell Market Sells

Everyone in the bell market points with their whole arm, and after an hour I knew why. The place is so loud that half the sellers have gone a little deaf, so they talk with their elbows.

It is the oldest square in the city, with two hundred stalls under red and yellow awnings. They sell lanterns, chimes and hand bells, and small brass clappers lie in tubs like nails. A hundred different sounds ring at once, and under them someone always shouts a price. It was a free Sunday, nine days before the Lantern Ball, and half the school had come to buy something shiny.

One of them was Rowan, in his blue school coat, with his hair sticking up at the back because he never remembers to comb it.

*Your boy has stopped again,* said a voice at my boot.

That was Vesk. He lives in my shadow when I need him, and he talks the way other people sneeze. It comes from nowhere, and it is about everything.

*Third stall. A bell as big as his head. He is bargaining in a language I do not know. I think it is cheerfulness.*

"He's buying a lantern for Clara," I said, very quietly, to the back of my own hand.

*He is buying everything for Clara. I counted four things and a pastry. The pastry is for him.*

Ysolde's order had been four words long. *Watch the boy. Quietly.* So there I was in a brown coat and a flat cap, a boy eating chestnuts and watching a boy who watched everything else.

That was the trouble with Rowan: he notices everything except danger.

He saw a pickpocket working the crowd and kicked a barrel into the man's path without even looking. He saw a woman with her shoes on the wrong feet.

"Excuse me, madam, your shoes are swapped!" he called, kindly, and she thanked him with a face like thunder.

Then he saw that the lantern seller's cat had one blue eye and one green.

"You're a very special cat," he told it, and the cat ignored him.

He did not see the man in the gray coat, who had walked three stalls behind him since the clock tower.

*Tannin thumb,* said Vesk. *Left hand, in his pocket. Same walk as the maze gallery.*

I had seen him too, standing at the back of the gallery on the day of the maze, before he stopped being there.

"Where's Gorrath?"

*Behind the pretzel stand. He is pretending to be a pretzel stand.*

"Mirelle?"

Vesk did not answer, because she never needs to be asked. I looked up and found a chimney on the east side of the square. A long, thin shadow lay along the tiles, and it was not the chimney's shadow. That was my answer.

"Can you take him?" I asked.

*Can I? Yes. Should I, in front of two hundred stalls? Ask me something harder.*

Three of my people against one man looks like a lot, but in a crowd it is not. If I scared him off, he would come back tomorrow with a friend. I did not want to catch him. I wanted to make him go where I chose, and for that I needed to turn the tail around.

* * *

I found the child by the cider barrels, where the air smelled of apples and hot sugar.

She was about five, and her brown braid was coming loose and one boot was untied. She stood perfectly still in the middle of the crowd with a small bell in her fist, as if someone had told her to hold it. She was crying without any sound, because kids do that when they decide that noise would make it real.

The grown-ups walked around her like water around a post, and nobody stopped to look down.

I crouched down so my face was level with hers, and I kept my voice low. "Are you lost?"

She nodded and said nothing else.

"What's your name?"

"Odile."

"Odile, I'll help you find your mother. First I need you to do one small job. Can you do it?"

*Eden,* said Vesk, softly.

*I know.*

Here is my problem, and I will say it plainly because I don't like to leave it out. The plan was good and clean, because a lost child grabs a stranger's coat and cries, "Papa, there you are!" and the crowd does the rest. Wardens come, questions come, and the stranger cannot slip away. It worked on me once when I was nine, and it works on everyone.

But I would be using her. I would send a scared five-year-old at a man with a knife in his belt, and I would tell her it was a game. The other choice was to let him follow Rowan.

*I can do it more quietly,* said Vesk. *Knife, alley, five minutes. You will never even see her cry.*

I did not answer, because we both knew what Vesk meant.

*Eden?*

"No," I said. "Not her. Not like that."

*Then the child it is,* said Vesk. *I only wanted you to say it.*

"Odile," I said. "Do you see the big man at the pretzel stand? He's my friend. His name is Gorrath, and he'll stand right behind you the whole time. If anyone scares you, run to him. Okay?"

She looked at the pretzel stand. Gorrath is seven feet tall and the gentlest man alive. He gave one tiny wave and nearly knocked the whole stand over.

"Okay," said Odile.

"You see the man in the gray coat? The one with the dark thumb? When I say, pull his sleeve and say you lost your mama and he looks nice. Then hold on tight, like it's a rope. Can you do that?"

"Is it a game?"

I thought about lying, because it would have been so easy.

"It's a job," I said. "An important one. And after, I'll buy you a pretzel as big as your head."

"With salt?"

"With all the salt."

She wiped her nose on her sleeve and nodded once, like a small soldier.

* * *

It took less than a minute, and she was good. She ran through the crowd, small and angry, and caught the gray coat by the sleeve with both hands. Then she howled.

"Mama! I lost my mama! You look nice! Don't go!"

The man jerked his arm, but she held on. Heads turned, a baker's wife said, "Oh, the poor thing," and a warden at the clock tower started to walk. The man had maybe four seconds to decide. He could make a scene or he could run, and he ran, which was his mistake.

He ran for the nearest alley, and Gorrath's cart was parked across the end of it. A woman's voice came out of the dark under the cart, high and angry. "There you are! You said you'd take me home!" That was Vesk, and it is his best voice. It is a terrible voice, but it works on guilty people.

The man spun around. Then a thin blade flew down from the chimney, forty feet up, and made no sound at all. It pinned the edge of his coat to a post.

He drew a knife. I was already there, two steps away, with the cart at my back.

"Don't," I said.

He cut at me anyway. I put the lance shaft in the way, and the knife slid off it. Then my other hand, the burned one, closed on his wrist, and I turned it until his fingers opened. The knife fell and rang once on the stones, like a bell.

The crowd had not noticed anything yet, because all the heads were turned toward the crying child. Mirelle dropped off the wall behind him, light as a cat, and said nothing, because she never does. She laid one finger on his throat, under the jaw, and he went down slowly. He was not dead.

*Perfect,* said Vesk. *We are all very proud. Nobody has praised me.*

"Good work, Vesk."

*Thank you. It's about time.*

We had less than a minute before the warden reached the alley, so I pulled the man's coat free from the post and Gorrath lifted him into the cart with one arm.

* * *

"Did I do the job?" Odile's voice came through the curtain, loud enough for the whole street.

"You did the job," I said.

"Do I still get the pretzel?"

"Two. Gorrath is paying."

Outside, Gorrath made a small, worried sound. It was the sound of a man counting coins in his head.

The back room of the cart is very small, and it smelled of old canvas and cold tea, with one lamp on a nail and one bench. We sat the man on the bench with his coat still pinned. I kept my hood up and my face in the dark. He woke up slowly and looked at me, and then at Gorrath, who filled the doorway. Then he laughed, a short, dry sound.

"You're not Seal," he said.

"No."

"You're not Order either. They would have just killed me." He rubbed his throat. "So who sends a five-year-old after me?"

"I did. She's getting a pretzel."

"That's terrible. She's five."

"She was very good at it. Better than you."

"That's terrible."

"It's cheaper than the other choice." I sat down across from him. "What's your name?"

"Corwen."

"That's not a name. That's a kingdom."

"It's the name I was born with." He said it flatly. Corwen was one of the kingdoms the Iron King took, and he had called it a gift. I let that sit in the room. It was the first honest thing either of us had said.

"Why do you follow the boy?"

"I don't."

"Corwen."

"I carry a bag. They pay me to carry it." He looked at the lamp. "I have a daughter. Did you know that? She's seven. She lives in Thornmere with her aunt. She has never seen the sea."

I did not answer. A man will tell you everything if you let him choose when.

"They want him alive," said Corwen at last. "The boy. They say he is the one who can't be touched. They want him alive on the night of lights, and not before. If anyone hurts one hair on him before that, none of us gets paid." He shrugged. "That's the whole job. Walk behind him, count his steps, and be somewhere else when it starts."

"When what starts?"

"I don't know. I'm a bag man."

It was true, and I could hear it in how he said it, because he was not clever enough to lie about this.

"There's a stall," I said. "Where you hand in your counts."

His eyes jumped to the door, and that was enough.

* * *

"Where do I take him?" asked Gorrath.

"The bookbinder's. Ysolde will want to talk to him."

"And if he wakes up?"

"Then he'll talk to you. Be nice."

"Nicer than that."

"I am always nice," said Gorrath. He rolled Corwen in a carpet and carried him off. I asked him to pass on one request, about a girl in Thornmere and the sea. He cannot lie, so he promised to try.

The lantern maker's stall was the blue one on the north row, run by a thin woman who sold paper stars and said her prices like a prayer. Now she was gone. She had left in a hurry, and her till stood open. A long book lay on the counter under a glass bell, the way people keep something they are proud of.

I turned the pages slowly, one at a time, with the stall's paper stars turning above my head.

There were dozens of names in a neat, slanting hand, and each had a number beside it. One column held lantern numbers and one held dates, and next to every name was a small note. *Tall. Quiet. Left-handed. Dances badly.* I knew that hand because I had seen it once, in a dead clerk's book, in the back room of a bookbinder's shop.

*Well?* said Vesk.

"Wait."

*You have gone very still. You only go still when it is bad.*

"I said wait."

I knew the names, too. Every one was a Year Five student, all forty-one of them. In the clerk's book they had looked like a list of people someone wanted to watch. In this book, with the numbers beside them, they looked like something else.

They were places.

It was a guest list, with one lantern for each name at the Ball and a spot where each would hang. In the margin of the very last page, in small writing, were three words I had already read once in the dark.

*Before the last dance.*

Rowan's lantern was number one. It hung nearest the glass door.

I closed the book and put it back under its glass bell exactly as I had found it, and my hands were quite steady. Behind me in the street a bell rang and a child laughed, and Gorrath was trying very hard to buy a pretzel without letting go of the little girl's hand.

Nine days, and a list of forty-one people who would all be there.
