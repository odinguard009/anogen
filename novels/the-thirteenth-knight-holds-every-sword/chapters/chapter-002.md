# Chapter 2: The Timetable

The board stood in a white room with no walls near the edge. The floor ran forty steps in every direction and then stopped and past the edge there was nothing and not dark. Nothing and Eden had learned not to look at it for long.

The Tutor sat across the board with his back half turned and he had taught Eden since Eden was ten. Eden still could not say what his nose looked like. He was tall and thin, and he held the game pieces with two fingers.

"Your tower," he said.

The tower was a small stone piece with nine smaller pieces around it. The east road had held for an hour of dream time and the west road was gone.

"I see it," Eden said.

"You saw it four moves ago. You moved toward the noise anyway."

"The noise was a knight."

"It was a knight that could not reach you." The Tutor set a thin black piece behind the tower. "You guard a place and think the place is the point. It never is. Again."

A long sigh came from behind Eden and it came from a door he did not look at straight.

"Some of us," said the Dragon, "are trying to sleep."

"You live in me," Eden said. "Pay rent."

"I am the rent. Every morning you wake up breathing, that is the rent." The voice was deep and fussy. "And that was good cloth. You gave it to a crying stranger."

"She was using her armband."

"Whatever that means."

The Tutor did not look up and the two of them never spoke to each other. They spoke past each other, through Eden.

Eden set the pieces up again.

"You are slow tonight," the Tutor said.

"I am tired."

"You are always tired. Look at the edge instead."

That was when Eden saw the doors. They stood along the edge of the board, where the white met the nothing. Their number changed, so he had stopped counting them and tonight one was locked. A thin silver hairpin sat in the keyhole and its flat head was shaped like a seed. Someone had started to open the lock and then been called away.

"Tutor," Eden said. "Who was here?"

No answer. Eden stood to look closer, and the floor under his left foot went soft.

The edge had moved while he was not looking and the white under his heel was thin. Past it was the nothing, and the nothing pulled and it did not feel like falling. It felt being rubbed out, starting at his feet. Cold climbed from his ankle to his knee before he could turn.

He threw his weight back and caught the doorframe beside the hairpin. The silver head was an inch from his knuckles, and he could not look away from it.

"Not that one," said the Tutor.

He had not moved. He set a white piece on the board, and the floor under Eden was hard again.

"Not yet," he said. "Again."

Eden woke with his left leg asleep to the hip and a sheet of paper lay on the floor by the door.

* * *

Lysette knocked an hour before the first bell and three knocks, evenly spaced. The hall outside his room was grey and cold, and the stone smelled wet. She stood there with a clean red band on her sleeve and her white hair was pinned tighter than yesterday.

"I put a timetable under your door," she said. "Did you read it?"

"Twice."

"Good. I brought another one, in case you lost yours in the last forty minutes."

She held it out, then looked past him into his room and bed, desk, chair, nothing on the walls.

"You're dressed," she said.

"I sleep dressed."

"That's—" She let it go. "South tables. Walk."

They walked with her half a step ahead. She had to look back every eleven steps to check he was still there. Two older students saw them coming and pressed themselves against the wall.

"Do they always do that?" Eden asked.

"Do what?"

"Never mind."

She did not mention the small cloth and her left jacket pocket stayed buttoned.

"Seven steps," she said, without turning. "You stopped to look at a pigeon."

"It looked at me first."

"Walk."

* * *

The dining hall was as loud as a workshop. Plates banged, benches scraped, and the room smelled of hot porridge and burnt bread. Lysette took Eden to the south tables, looked at him once more, then went to the Committee bench by the windows.

"Two rolls," she called across the hall. "At least."

"Yes," Eden said, too quietly for her to hear.

Felix sat at the end of the usual table, a round-faced boy with brown hair that never lay flat. He had porridge in front of him and a book propped against the salt. He did not look up when Eden sat down and that was how he said hello.

"Eaten today?" he said.

"Technically."

He turned a page. "What does technically mean today?"

"A peppermint."

"That's not food."

"It is in the food family."

Felix pushed the bread basket two inches toward him, his eyes still on the book. Then he looked over Eden's shoulder at the Committee bench.

"Ah," he said. "She's watching you eat."

"She's making sure I eat."

"Eden. What did you do?"

"I offered someone a cloth."

Felix put the book down and he only did that for fires and test results. He had sat next to Eden since their first week, when he walked up to the only empty seat and asked if it was taken. Then he sat before Eden could answer. In five years he had never asked where Eden went at night. He just pushed the bread over.

His tea had gone cold while he read, so Eden took the pot and filled the cup. He added half a spoon of honey and pushed it back and felix drank it too sweet and was shy about it.

"Thanks," Felix said, and picked up the book again. He did not ask about the cloth.

"You're eating," he said after a while.

"She's watching."

"Keep going, then."

That was the whole talk, and Eden ate two rolls.

The doors at the north end banged open. Rowan came in with Clara beside them, both still in drill coats. Clay marked Rowan's sleeves, and a red practice-blade mark crossed the back of his hand. Clara had a ribbon on her collar and a grey streak on her cheek. She walked the way people walk when they have already been awake for three hours.

"Wren," Rowan Tallis said. He saw the table and came over, because he came over to every table.

 "You're awake."

"It happens."

"Clara, this is Wren. He's eighth."

"I know who he is," Clara said, and sat down across from Felix.

Rowan took the bench beside her, and the bench complained. He put three rolls on his plate before he put down his bag. "Morning drill. Okoru made us run the east stairs with shields. My arms are dead."

"You said that yesterday," Clara said.

"Yesterday it was the west stairs."

Lysette's voice came across the hall. "Tallis. Sit down or leave."

Rowan sat down so fast that his knee hit the table. Clara's mouth moved, almost a smile, and she pulled her plate out of the way.

Eden watched the room instead of the table. Three tables over, a boy in a grey coat watched Rowan and then looked away. An older student by the door counted heads with a pencil. Lysette wrote something in her black book without looking at the page.

* * *

Grammar class was held in a steep half-bowl of benches facing a big slate wall. Professor Brandmeier stood at the bottom, a short woman with grey hair in a tight knot.

Eden had planned to sit at the back. Lysette had a seat ready in the front half, exactly as written. Her bag was on it, and she moved the bag when he came, without looking at him.

Brandmeier called the names. When she reached his, Eden said, "Here." The room went quiet in a way he did not like.

"Who?" someone said behind him, much too loud.

Brandmeier took off her glasses and looked at him over the place where they had been. Then she put them back on and made a mark.

"How nice," she said. "A sighting."

The lesson was about wards and a ward was a spell that guarded a place. It was a lock made of words and she wrote a sentence on the slate as she talked. It had a subject, a verb, and a long part that named what the ward protected. Her chalk was mixed with stone dust, so each finished line pulled a little mana out of the air and lifted off the slate with a faint blue glow. Mana was the power that ran spells.

"Listen," she said. "Wards are sentences. A sentence with a mistake is a lie. A gate built on a lie falls on the people inside." She drew the last line. "This one has a mistake. A small one."

The letters began to hum, a low sound felt in the back teeth. A thin curl of smoke rose from the third line. The front row leaned back together, and someone's inkpot slid an inch along the bench by itself.

Brandmeier did not turn. She looked up into the benches, all the way to the top. Then she found Eden in the front half, where he had never sat.

"Mr. Wren," she said. "You have been away so often this year that the office asked if you had died. So tell me, where is the mistake?"

Eden said nothing, and the smoke kept rising.

"Is he asleep?" someone whispered. "He looks asleep."

"Mr. Wren," said Brandmeier, louder. "I am waiting."

She knew what she was doing. She would let it run until he was frightened, then fix it with one stroke and let the room enjoy him. A safe answer sat ready in his mouth and the verb tense, Professor. Nobody remembers a safe answer.

Next to him, Lysette had gone still, her pencil above her page.

He looked at the ward again. The sentence said keep, and guard, and hold, but it never said what. A ward like that had nothing to point at, so it pointed at itself. He did not want to say so. But if he stayed quiet, the smoke would reach the second line and the front row would be hurt.

"The ward has no subject, Professor."

Six words, and they were true and brandmeier stared at him.

"Who taught you that?" she asked.

"A book."

"Which book?"

"I don't remember. It was boring."

She did not answer that. Without turning, she reached back and drew one short word into the gap at the front of the sentence. The hum stopped, the smoke thinned and was gone, and the inkpot stayed where it was.

"Yes," she said, and she sounded annoyed. "Write that down, all of you. Mr. Wren will not say it again."

A room can decide it dislikes you and the air changes. For five years Eden had never been the boy in class who knew the answer, and in four seconds he became him. Nobody near him looked his way for the rest of the hour.

Lysette underlined something with her ruler, so hard that the page tore.

"You knew that," she whispered.

"I guessed."

"You do not guess. You sleep."

"Both."

* * *

After the last class, the hall outside the west classrooms was full, as it always was at that hour. Eden's bag hung off one shoulder. Lysette walked half a step ahead and a little to the left, pulling him toward the stairs.

Valerius stood at the top of them and he was not waiting for Eden. He was talking to two students from the council, with a stack of papers in his hand. He was tall, with fair hair and a straight back, and a small bronze pin on his collar. Nothing else on him said he was the king's son, except the empty space people kept around him without being told.

He heard about the class. Eden saw it land when Valerius turned his head and found him. He sent the two students away with a nod, and they went.

"Lysette," he said, politely. She answered with a small bow.

Then he looked at Eden, the way he always did, and he read the look on Eden's face and found the bad line.

"Brandmeier," he said. "A ward with no subject."

"It was smoking."

"I know it was smoking. Half the year is talking about it." He stepped closer, but it was not a threat, because he did not make threats. "Six words, in front of a hundred and twenty people."

"You two know each other?" Lysette asked.

"No," Eden said.

"Yes," said Valerius, at the same time.

Eden could feel Lysette looking from him to Valerius and back. She was doing a sum, and she did not have all the numbers.

"So," Valerius said quietly, so only the three of them could hear. "You have finally stopped hiding?"

There were a few honest answers. Each one cost something he would not pay in a crowded hall, with the Committee vice-chair at his elbow and her pencil already out.

"I'm on a timetable," Eden said.

Something closed in Valerius's face, and he looked tired and he held Eden's eyes one more second.

"Pity," he said.

He stepped aside to let them pass, and he was back to his papers before they reached the second step.

"What was that about?" Lysette asked on the stairs.

"He's the king's son. He talks to everyone."

"He does not talk to everyone."

Eden kept walking. At the bottom she stopped and opened her black book. She wrote one line and tilted the book so he could not read it, though not quite enough.

Why does the Prince know him?
