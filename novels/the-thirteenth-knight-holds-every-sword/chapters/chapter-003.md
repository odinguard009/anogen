# Chapter 3: The Night Supervisor

The exchange on Bell Lane had one window lit, on the second floor. The rest of the building was dark grey stone, four floors tall and narrow. A brass plate hung by the door, and the smell of wet coal came up from the cellar.

Eden stood across the street under the awning of a closed shop. Vesk came out of his shadow and stood beside him.

"Nine calls," Vesk said. "Three nights. All to this exchange."

"How many did you hear?"

"One. The rest I read in the book." Vesk looked at the lit window. "It said my mother rang."

Eden looked at the window too. "Did she?"

"She has been dead eleven years." Vesk said it without any weight, the way he said everything.

Vesk was a small man in grey, with eyes the yellow of a lamp wick. He wore black plate the way other men wore coats, and he spoke like a man who had already decided that the night would be long.

"Who called?" Eden asked.

"People with dead relatives. A widow, two clerks, a baker, a boy of nine." Vesk counted on his fingers, slowly, as if the numbers were heavy. "The voice on the line knows things. Names. Birthmarks. What a man said to his wife before he died."

"And the supervisor?"

"Works nights alone. The day staff swear she hired them eight years ago and has not aged a day." Vesk showed his teeth. "She is not the same woman."

Eden looked at the lit window. One room. One kill. No trace. That was the rule, and the rule was the only reason the Thirteenth Vigil was still a story that people told badly.

"Two went to ask about the calls," Vesk said. "A baker and a clerk. Neither came home."

"The bodies?"

"Nothing. Not a shoe." Vesk stepped back toward the shadow. "I cannot go in with you. Too much copper in the walls, and the lamps are low. I would be a man in a dark room with no way out."

"Stay on the street. Whistle once if someone comes."

"Whistle. Like a bird." Vesk said it the way a man says a word he does not trust. Then he was gone, and the shadow under the awning was only a shadow.

* * *

The front door was locked. Eden went around the back, through a yard with three bins and a cat that did not move. The coal hatch was loose on one hinge. He went down the chute feet first, and the coal broke his fall with a sound like dry rain.

The cellar was black. He stood still and let his eyes learn it: the furnace, cold; the coal heap; a service stair at the far end with a strip of light under the door. Above him the building hummed. Not a machine hum. A voice hum, many voices at once, too low to make out.

Then one voice came clear through the floor.

"Your mother rang. Come to the exchange."

A woman's voice, warm, tired, kind. Then the same words in a man's voice, then a child's, all with the same slow patience, as if the mouth had practised.

Eden took off his coat and folded it on the coal. He stood where his shadow was long.

The lamp above the stair dimmed, as if it were holding its breath. His shadow spread under him in a clean round pool, edge sharp as a drawn line. He stepped backward into it, and the cold came up through his boots to his knees.

The plates rose from the pool from the feet up. Sabatons first, a soft click. Greaves, knee wings, thigh plates, the faulds. Each one closed cold for one heartbeat and then turned warm. The breastplate sealed with a deep sound, the way a vault door seals. Pauldrons, one and then the other. Vambraces. Gauntlets last, the fingers closing one by one.

The helm came up smooth and black, with no face and one narrow slit of white light. It closed over his head, and every sound in the world dropped away except his own breath.

A long plain coat unrolled from his shoulders and settled without a sound.

**[Umra]** had no weight. It made no noise. The cellar turned grey-blue, and every edge grew sharp: the chute, the stair, the line where the light stopped under the door.

Eden took one step. The coal did not move under his boot.

* * *

The service stair had eleven steps. He counted them with his hand on the wall. At the top, the door opened onto a corridor with a red carpet worn to threads down the middle.

The hum was louder here. It came from the room at the end, where the strip of light was.

Eden stopped three steps short of the door and listened.

"...your mother rang," the voice said. "She asked for you. Come to the exchange."

A pause. A click. Then a different voice, a man's: "Your mother rang. She asked for you. Come to the exchange."

Another click. A child: "Your mother rang."

The room smelled of hot copper and cold sweat. Eden put one gloved finger against the door and pushed.

The switchroom was long and narrow. One lamp hung from the ceiling on a cord, and under it stood the board: a wall of dark wood, twelve steps wide, full of holes and brass pins and little shutters. Copper cables came out of the top and ran along the ceiling like roots. A woman sat at the board with her back to the door, a headset over her ears, her grey coat hung on the chair.

Eden counted the room. One door behind him. One window, painted shut. The lamp. The board's shadow on the wall, black and square, big enough.

"Your mother rang," the woman said into the headset. "Come to the exchange. Bring a coat. It is cold."

She pulled a plug from the board and pushed it into another hole. Somewhere in the city, a telephone rang in an empty room.

Eden crossed the carpet in six steps. He put his palm against the wall inside the board's shadow and knocked twice.

The sound was small, like a knuckle on a door. Then the shadow opened. A black rectangle, taller than a man, stood in the wall, and its edges were sharp as paper. The cables' shadows leaned toward it and fell in. The hum stopped. The lamp steadied.

The woman turned around.

Her face was a supervisor's face: about fifty, tired, kind, with a small mole by her mouth. Her smile came a beat after her eyes. Her shadow stayed where it was for one breath before it followed her.

"The Thirteenth Vigil," she said. Her voice was not one voice now. It was a chord, low and layered, and every note was somebody's mother. "You are early."

Eden kept his eyes on her hands. He had one clear thought, the way he always did before a fight. The board was dead, but her mouth was not. Stop the mouth.

She stood up. Her coat fell off the chair. Under it she wore the exchange's grey uniform, and her hands were too long for her sleeves.

"Your mother rang," she said. "She asked for you. She said you would come."

Eden drew **[Exa]** from his own shadow.

It came out of the black under his hand: a short lance, a little longer than his arm, matte black, cold even through the glove. It made no shine under the lamp. He held it at his hip, point forward, the way he had held it a thousand times in the dream Vault.

The Veil moved first. It took the copper bar off the wall by the board, a heavy thing as long as a walking stick, and came at him in three long steps. The bar hit the air where his head had been. He turned on his front foot, and the second swing caught the lance.

He knocked once on the iron ring at the lance's guard, and **[Exa]** folded. The lance shortened into a black shield the size of a tea tray, with the iron ring on the outside. The copper bar struck the ring and rang like a bell. The shock went up Eden's arm to the elbow, and his fingers went cold.

"Your mother," the Veil said, and this time the voice was a woman's, low and fond, "used to sing to you."

Eden's shield did not move. Behind the slit of his helm, his eyes stayed on her shoulders, not her mouth.

"Say my middle name," he said.

The smile stayed where it was. The mouth moved a beat later. "Your mother—"

"Say it."

The Veil said a name. It was not his.

He did remember a song. That was the trouble with Veils. They pulled the words out of the air and the air out of your chest. But a name had to be given, and nobody had given her his.

Eden took one step back. The Veil came after him, fast and wrong-jointed, the copper bar low. He caught it on the shield again, let it slide, and put the shield's edge into her wrist. The bar dropped.

She did not stop. She reached past him for the lamp cord.

Eden needed light. Light makes edges. Edges make doors. He put his shoulder into her chest and drove her back against the board. The lamp swung, and the whole room moved its shadows.

Then he drew **[Inead]**.

It came into his right hand as a thin cold line first, then as gold light made solid: a hand-and-a-half sword with a plain grey grip, shining like a low sun in a cellar. It rang once when it left the air, one clear note, and the room turned honest. A small ache settled behind his eyes. The sword was cheap to hold, but it was not free.

The supervisor's face went to glass. Under the skin, the grey body showed: long, thin, folded, with too many joints and a mouth that ran from ear to ear.

The Veil said something in the chord of the dead. Every voice stopped in the middle of a word.

Eden made one cut, from the shoulder down through the core.

The grey body came apart like a coat falling off a hook. The voices stopped one by one: the man, the widow, the boy of nine, and last the woman's warm tired voice, which said, quite clearly, "You smell like the bottom of the world."

Then there was grey dust on the red carpet, and a headset swinging on its cord, and nothing else.

* * *

Far off, the tower clock began to ring. Three tones, slow and even.

Eden stood still and counted. The third tone faded. Then a fourth came, so faint that he thought the bell had moved in its frame.

"That was not the hour," said Vesk from the doorway. He had come up the service stair without a sound, and his yellow eyes went from the dust to the board. "Three tones. Not four."

"Not the hour," Eden said.

He let **[Inead]** go. The gold light went out of the room, and the lamp was only a lamp again. He knocked once on the ring, and **[Exa]** unfolded back into a lance. Then he put it away in the black under his hand.

The Frost came then, the way it always did after the armor: a cold that started in his fingers and settled behind his eyes. He breathed out slowly. The helm opened, the plates went back into the floor one by one, and the coal-smelling air of the exchange came in.

Vesk was already at the board with a cloth. "No trace," he said. "I know. You say it every time."

"There is a list."

It was pinned beside the board under a brass clip: one sheet of paper, ruled in columns. Eden took it down and read it under the lamp.

Calls, dates, addresses. The last column held names. Not the callers. The people the calls were about.

He read down the page. Most of the names were ordinary: a solicitor, a dressmaker, a retired sergeant. Then, halfway down, one name had a circle around it, drawn twice, so hard that the pencil had torn the paper.

Tallis.

Eden read the address beside it. Then he read the next line, and the next. All of them were Hallowcrest families. All of them had a child in Year 5.

Vesk leaned over his arm. "The school."

"Yes."

"One of them is circled."

"Yes."

Vesk was quiet for a moment. Then he took a folded paper from inside his plate and held it out. "The grey office sent this up the stair while you were inside. A boy on a bicycle. He did not wait."

The paper was folded in three, the way Ysolde folded everything. One line, in her small flat hand.

Form 13-B. Before the first bell.

Eden folded it again, put it in his coat, and looked once more at the circle around the name.
