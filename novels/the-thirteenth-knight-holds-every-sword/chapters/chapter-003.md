# Chapter 3: Short Shadows

The tax clerk bought the same thing every night. It was one candied pear on a stick, from the same stall, from the same old woman. He paid with a silver quarter, and then he waited for change he did not need.

For three nights I had watched him do it. For three nights the old woman's cat got up and left when he came close, a fat striped cat that slept in her coin box.

On the fourth night, Ysolde put the file in front of me. "Well?"

"The cat knows," I said.

"I can't file a cat."

We sat in the back room of a bookbinder's shop in Solmarch, the big market town below our school. It was after sundown and the shop was closed. The room smelled of glue and cold tea, and a dark cloth hung over the window.

Ysolde kept her coat on, as always. She is a thin woman with sharp eyes and short gray hair, and she always looks about to leave. She is the King's secretary, and she wants paperwork for everything.

The file said: *Emeric Fauconier. Thirty-four. Tax clerk. Unmarried. No debts. Clean.*

"He changed four weeks ago," she said. "His landlady says he stopped humming. The man at the next desk says he got better at sums."

"People do that."

"Not at thirty-four. Men that age don't change, they only get tired." She turned her teacup a quarter turn on the saucer, which is what she does instead of sighing. "I want this quiet. The Golden Seal has six investigators in town this week. They are the King's police, and they hate any secret they did not find first. If they see your mask, they will want it."

"They can have the mask."

"Eden."

"I'm only saying it's not a good mask."

"It is the only one you have."

"Then it is a very good mask."

She looked at me. Her face, like mine, does not do much, but on her it looks planned.

"And your curfew," she said. "I hear you have a supervisor now."

I did not ask how she knew, and I answered. "She goes to bed at half past the tenth bell. She checks my door at the tenth."

"Then be back by the sixth. And the Bell Assessment is in nine days. Three tones, like always. Not three and a half."

Last year I put my hand on the big bronze bell in the school hall and pushed for three clear tones. In the quiet after, so faint I thought it was my pulse, the bell rang a fourth time. Nobody turned their head, so I decided I had imagined it.

"Three," I said.

"Say it like you mean it."

"Three. Clear tones. No fourth."

She stood and buttoned the top button of a coat she never unbuttoned. "Form 13-B on my desk by noon. All sections."

* * *

The lantern market runs every autumn night until the guards clear it out. Paper lanterns hang over its two streets, so thick you can hardly see the sky. Some are a cheap green that the dye-sellers say is lucky. The air smells of hot wine and fried dough.

It is a terrible place for me to work.

My power is doors, and a door needs an edge, a hard line where light stops and dark starts. Under a thousand lanterns the shadows come out short and soft, and they run into each other. There are almost no edges.

I had spent three nights finding the few I could use. The best one was under the dye-seller's canvas roof, one strip of honest black right over an iron hatch. The hatch was rusted shut, and nine feet under it was a cellar full of dye barrels.

At the ninth bell I was on the roof tiles above the chestnut stall. Smoke from the roasting pans stung my nose. I let Umra come.

Umra is black armor, and it rises out of my shadow like water filling a footprint. First come the boots, cold on my feet, then the legs and the chest, plate by plate, closing from the ground up. Each piece is cold for one heartbeat and then warm like my own skin. The last piece is the helm, smooth and without a face, with only a thin eye slit sewn shut by one white thread. The armor is light as felt and almost silent, but the cold stays deep behind my ribs.

"Cold again?" said a small voice from the dark under the chimney. "Your teeth say so."

"My teeth are quiet."

"Not to me."

It was Vesk. I could not see him, because you almost never can. He is the smallest of my three shadow friends, quick, with hands like knives, and he lives in other people's shadows and comes back with opinions.

"He's late," Vesk said. "He's never late."

"Maybe he's busy."

"With what? He has no friends. I checked."

"Maybe. Also, the pear woman's grandson is chasing the girl from the fried dough stall. Badly. He brought her a pear. Her *competitor's* pear." He paused. "Thought you'd want to know."

"I didn't."

"You'll want it next week, when the stalls fight."

"Is he coming?"

"Yes. Here he—oh. He bought *two* pears."

I looked down. The clerk came up the street through the crowd in a neat brown coat with ink on his right cuff. He had a nice, forgettable face, and he held a pear in each hand.

"Who's the second one for?" Vesk whispered.

"Me, maybe."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke."

A change in routine, and I did not like it.

"Eden, he's smiling at the cat," Vesk said. "The cat's leaving. Again."

"I see it."

He still stopped at the stall, and he still paid and waited for his change. The cat climbed out of the coin box and walked away. Under the red lanterns he stood smiling at the old woman, and I watched his shadow on the stones.

"Eden," Vesk whispered. "Look at his shadow."

"I am."

"It isn't breathing."

"I know."

Shadows breathe, though you do not notice because you do not look. They grow a hair bigger and smaller with the chest. His lay on the stones like paint.

"He's looking up," Vesk breathed. "Eden. He's looking *up*."

"I see."

He turned his head and looked straight at my roof, though he should not have been able to see me, because Umra sits in the dark like a stone in a pond. But he looked, and his friendly face did not change at all. He dropped one pear and ran.

* * *

He was fast for a man with a clerk's knees. He pushed through the crowd the wrong way, against the people, which was smart, because nobody in a lantern market looks up at the roofs.

I ran along the roofs and jumped the alley to a balcony. Below me he shoved through a family of five and cut left toward the river stairs.

A big fire basket hangs at the top of those stairs. Its light would kill every edge, and past it were the dock lanes, where the Golden Seal had its men.

"He's going left," said Vesk, from somewhere. "No, *right*, he's—"

"I see him."

"Do you? Because I'm not sure *he* does."

"He knows where he's going. He's running straight down the middle."

"Then why's he—"

"Vesk."

"Quiet. Got it."

"Where are the Seal men?"

"Two streets east. Eating. They always eat."

The shortest way to the stairs went right past the dye-seller's stall. That was why I had spent three nights on it, because I wanted him to run over the black strip.

"Plan?" said Vesk.

"The dye-seller's shadow. I open a door, he falls, I follow."

"Alone?"

"So no Seal man sees a thing."

I knocked once on the air, the way you knock on a door you know is already open. In the black strip under the awning, a door opened in the shadow. You would have had to stand on it to see it, a rectangle of deeper dark, frame and all, set into the stones. The clerk's left foot came down on it.

The iron hatch stayed rusted shut. He went through the shadow instead and fell nine feet into the cellar, fast and quiet, and the door closed over him. A man selling green dye looked at his own feet, frowned, and went back to shouting about luck.

"Lucky green! Get your lucky green!"

I climbed off the balcony and crossed the street at a walk, and then I stepped into the shade after him.

* * *

The dark in the cellar was total. The air was cold and smelled of wet earth and old paint, and there was no crack of light from the street.

I landed beside a dye barrel and stood still.

"Eden?" Vesk whispered, from far above. "I can't follow you in there. It's too dark. Even for me."

"I know."

"Is that bad?"

"Yes. Stay on the roof. If I'm not out by the next bell, tell Ysolde about the cat."

"That's a terrible last message."

"Then I'll come out."

I saw my mistake at the same moment the Veil did: no light, no edges, and no edges, no doors.

A Veil is a monster that wears a dead person's face and life. This one had stopped pretending to breathe, because Veils do not need air. They breathe only for the neighbors. Then I heard a tin clink, and I knew he had taken the lamp off its hook by the stairs and smashed it on the floor, so I would have nothing. He knew what I was, or he knew enough.

He came at me in the dark and I only heard it. His feet hit the packed earth with a stride too long for a clerk, and then came the sound a coat makes when the arms inside grow longer than the sleeves.

I pulled out my lance and closed it into the shield, a door-shield the size of a small door with an iron ring-knocker in the middle. His first blow hit it with a weight no clerk ever had. I let it push me back one step, to learn how strong he was.

He was strong, but not strong enough.

He circled, and I turned with him, by ear. He struck low and I caught it on the bottom edge of the shield. Then a gray finger slid over the rim and touched my wrist. It was cold, and the joints bent the wrong way.

That was when my sword hummed.

My sword is called Candor. I had not called it yet, and it waited inside, behind the Gate, the place where I keep it. From in there it made one low, clean note, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass. It had never done that before, because swords do not hum. The Veil jerked back as if the note had burned him.

I had a tinderbox in my left glove, next to the peppermint, and I struck it. One spark, then a flame the size of a fingernail. That was all I needed. Light on one side and dark on the other made an edge, and an edge makes a door. I reached into the Gate, and Candor came into my right hand as if it had been waiting there all along.

Candor does not shine, though people expect it to. What it does is make any light tell the truth. In the light of one match, the clerk's brown coat hung off something gray and thin, as tall as a door. It had long legs and too many elbows, like a heron made of ash, and the clerk's face sat on the front of it like a mask that had slipped.

I always confirm before I end one, because that is my rule. I made it at thirteen, and I have never broken it.

"Your middle name," I said. "Say it."

The face opened its mouth and nothing came out, and when it tried again there was still nothing.

That was my answer, so I used the last light of the match, which shook in my fingers. I caught the next swing on the shield, opened its guard with the lance at the shoulder, and put Candor through the place where a heart should be.

It sank slowly between the dye barrels, and the gray turned to dust from the edges inward, like burning paper. Only the face was left, lying in the dirt, with its eyes on me.

"You," it said, in the clerk's voice, clear as a bell, "smell like the bottom of the world."

Then the face was dust too.

The match burned my fingers, so I shook it out and stood in the dark. Through the stone above me, very faint, the market went on, and a man shouted about lucky green.

* * *

Ysolde was waiting in the lane with her coat on. She looked at the soot on my glove, then at the brown coat under my arm.

"Well?" she said.

"It's done."

"Quiet?"

"Quiet enough. A man sold a lot of green dye."

"Form 13-B," she said. "Write about the cat."

"You want the cat in the report?"

"I want everything in the report. Was it a Veil?"

"Yes."

"Did it say anything?"

"It said I smell like the bottom of the world."

"Put that in too. Quote it."

"It will make the Seal nervous."

"Good. Nervous men make mistakes."

Umra had already sunk back into my shadow, though I could still feel its cold behind my ribs. She held out her hand for the coat. Before I gave it over, I took the clerk's book out of the inside pocket, because she would have done the same.

"Anything else?" Ysolde asked.

"Give me a second. Hold the lamp lower."

"It is a very good lamp."

"It's a very bad angle."

It was a tax book full of names and sums, but the last six pages were not in the clerk's neat columns.

"A list," I said. "Forty names, in different handwriting. Rounder."

"Whose names?"

"Mine too."

They were all students from my year at school. Mine was on the second page, between two girls from the riding club, with nothing written beside it. Rowan Tallis was on the third page. Someone had circled his name twice, so hard that the paper was dented, and next to it, in the same round hand, was a note.

"Ysolde," I said. "Whose handwriting is this?"

She took the page and read it. If she had held a teacup, it would have turned a quarter turn.

"Not his," she said.

"I know it's not his."

"Then I will find out. Go to bed, Eden, before your supervisor checks the door."

*Lantern Ball. Before the last dance.*
