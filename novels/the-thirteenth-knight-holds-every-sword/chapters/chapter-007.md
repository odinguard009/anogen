# Chapter 7: Medical Exemption

The warrant was pinned to my coat when I woke up. It was not in my pocket, and it was not on the grass by my head. Someone had pushed a brass Committee pin through the cloth, and the paper went up and down on my chest when I breathed. I would have had to be dead to miss it.

*WARRANT OF ATTENDANCE (FIRST). Eden Wren, Year Five. Absent from Strategy, ninth bell. The student must go to the issuing officer at once.*

Under the words was a wax seal, or almost a seal. The Committee's little bell inside a circle was there, but it was pale and pink. Someone had pressed the stamp on a dry pad, and then pressed it again, harder, because she was angry.

The sun had moved across the apple tree, so I had slept about two hours. For a roof sleeper, that was a short morning. The morning before that had been long.

* * *

Rowan found me at breakfast, or really he found me from the far end of the hall. Two hundred people sat between us, and he shouted my name anyway.

"EDEN!"

Every head turned to see who Eden was. Most of them had never cared before.

"I saved you a seat," Rowan said when he got to me. He had. He had put his bowl on it.

Felix sat across from me, buttering his roll, and did not look up.

"So where did you grow up?" Rowan asked, sitting down. "Do you ride? Can you throw a spear? I can show you, it's all in the hips. Oh, and someone said you sleep on the old tower roof. I said no, that's crazy, nobody sleeps on a roof in autumn."

"It's a good roof," I said.

"Have you eaten?"

Felix looked up fast. That was his question, and he had never said so, but it was.

"Technically," I said.

"Technically isn't eating." Rowan put his egg on my plate.

He found me again at the seventh bell, outside the spell class. "This way," he said. "Your seat is here. I checked." He walked me to it. He found me again in the cloister at the eighth bell, and by then a small crowd followed him like birds behind a fishing boat. I stood in the middle and heard about a mule named Corporal.

"Why Corporal?" I asked. I had not meant to ask.

"Because he never got promoted," Rowan said. "He bit a general once. Only once. After that they respected each other."

Strategy was at the ninth bell. The room was round, at the top of the west stair, quiet and warm, with a window seat I liked. I stood at the bottom of the stair and looked up.

Then I thought about Rowan. He took Strategy too, and he had told me twice that he sat in the front row. The seat next to him had been empty all week. And the old tower was on the other side of the cloister, where no one followed me up two hundred and six steps to a door that stuck.

* * *

The door stuck now. Someone pushed on the other side, said a bad word in a whisper, and pushed again.

Lysette came out onto the roof with papers under one arm and a Committee armband on the other. Her calm had a small dent in it. She looked at me, and then at the warrant on my chest.

"You found it," she said.

"It found me."

"Good. Then you'll come down."

I sat up, and the paper crackled. "I have an exemption."

"You have sixty-one absences. Now sixty-two."

"It's medical." I took a folded paper from my inside pocket and held it out.

She did not take it at first. I watched her think that taking it might be a loss. Then she crossed the grass, walked past the herb beds, took the paper, and read it.

It was one page in the headmistress's writing. The letters were huge, because the headmistress was half blind and wrote for people who were more blind. The page said that the student Eden Wren, on medical advice, did not have to attend class while he rested on the roofs of the school. No officer of the student body could disturb him there. Under the words was the name *H. Ostwald* and a real seal, deep red, with an owl on it.

Lysette read it twice. The second time, her lips moved on one word.

"Roofs," she said.

"Plural."

"I can see it's plural." She held the page closer. "What medical advice?"

"It doesn't say."

"I can see it doesn't say. That's why I'm asking."

"Then you'll have to ask the headmistress."

She looked at me over the paper, the way you look at a cart that lost a wheel in a narrow street. She was not angry yet, because no one had decided whose fault it was. I could see her thinking about the handbook. Was a Committee vice-chair an officer of the student body? Yes. Could a headmistress's paper beat a Committee warrant? No one knew, because no one had ever been dumb enough to try.

I had thought I would enjoy this more. A person who loves rules had just found a rule she did not know, and it was less fun to watch than I expected.

"This isn't in the handbook," she said.

"No."

"There's a whole part about exemptions. I wrote half of it."

"The handbook is yours," I said. "The roof is hers."

She opened her mouth and shut it. Then she looked at something else, the herb beds.

Someone had pulled weeds there that morning. The soil was dark where it was new, and a small pile of chickweed lay by the stone edge, starting to dry. I had stepped over it on my way to the apple tree. Whoever did the work had come before dawn, worked with care, and left her trowel in the sage, handle up. A strip of dark wool was wrapped around the handle, the same color as a Committee coat.

I did not look at it. I looked at the warrant instead, which was easier.

"Your seal didn't work," I said.

"It worked."

"It's pink."

"The pad was dry. I asked for a new one." She took the warrant off my coat, pin and all, and stuck her thumb on the pin. She said nothing about it, very loudly. "You are impossible."

"I'm exempt."

"On *roofs*." She folded the paper along its lines in sharp little moves and gave it back. "Fine. You can lie on every roof in school. But lunch is not on a roof."

"No," I said.

"And our Lance eats at the long table by the east windows. The instructor ordered it, all five of us, starting today. So you will come to lunch, you will eat, and I will mark your name."

"That's attendance."

"It is not. It is lunch. So your *exemption*"—she leaned on the word like it might break—"has nothing to do with it."

I thought about it. Her answer had a hole in it big enough to sleep in, and she knew that. She stood in front of the hole and looked at me.

"Fine," I said.

She blinked. She had climbed two hundred and six steps for a fight. "Fine?"

"I'm hungry."

That was true, and I had not planned to say it. She looked at me a little too long. Then she turned and pushed the door with her shoulder. It opened easily from that side, and she went down the stair without looking at the sage.

* * *

The hall at noon was the closest thing the school had to a battlefield, and it was worse, because nobody could win. Four hundred Year Fives, two food windows, one bell. They stood in line the way water goes down a drain.

The long table by the east windows had five places and a slate at the end that said LANCE NINE in chalk. That was the instructor's idea of a joke, or a warning.

Rowan was already there. Clara sat next to him and peeled an orange in one long curl. Felix had come with me and said nothing, which was different from Felix being quiet. It meant he was saving something.

"Eden!" Rowan half stood. "Lysette got you! She said she would. She said, I'll have him at that table by noon or I'll eat my armband."

"I did not say that," said Lysette, from the far end.

"You said something like it."

"I said I would *see to it*."

Rowan leaned toward me to say something private, but Rowan cannot do private. It came out as loud as a man ordering beer. "She's scary on duty, isn't she?"

"Yes," said Lysette. She sat four seats away and did not look up from her soup.

Clara laughed and put a piece of orange on Rowan's plate. He ate it and did not notice, and Lysette watched him not notice.

That was when Seraphine came. She did not sit with us, because she was in another Lance with its own table. She stopped behind Felix with her tray on her hip, looked at him, looked at me, and looked back at him.

"My savior is at a table," she said. "In public. With people. Felix, what did you do?"

"Nothing."

"You must have done something. He's eating."

"Lysette did it."

"Ah." She looked up the table at Lysette and smiled the smile she used on her brother when he was about to lose an argument. Then she turned to Felix. "Do you ever laugh? I have never seen you laugh. Not once in five years. Is it a rule in your house?"

Felix thought about it for real. "No."

"Do you know any jokes?"

"One."

"Tell me."

"It's not good."

"That's not a reason. That's a review." She waited, but he did not tell it. She narrowed her eyes, happy, the way she was when she saw a lock she wanted to open. "Fine. Before the Ball, I will make you laugh."

"Why?"

"Because you asked why," said Seraphine, and she went back to her table.

Felix watched her go like a man who had just heard his house would get new paint. He did not know whose house it was.

* * *

I went to the food window for bread, because our basket was empty and Rowan kept looking at it.

The crowd was worst right after the bell. The second wave came before the first one left, so you stood pressed between coats and trays with an elbow in your back. A kitchen girl gave out rolls from a basket as big as a baby's bed. She had flour up to her wrists and a faded green scarf tied over her hair. It was the green people wore in the south hills, and no one wore it here on purpose.

She gave me two rolls. Under the second one, flat against my hand, was a card.

It was black and stiff, as big as a playing card. A rose was pressed on it, flat and dry, the color of old ash. Every petal was whole. Someone had taken great care with a dead flower. Under it, in white ink, were two words.

*Remember Thornmere.*

I looked up. She was already giving rolls to a boy from the north towers, and her face showed nothing. It was a tired kitchen face. Then the boy moved, and she was behind the basket, then behind the window, and then I could not see her.

Thornmere was the name on my school papers. My mother had picked it, and the house that owned the name was gone, every one of them. A dead house did not send relatives to ask questions. But someone had come to remember it anyway.

I went back to the table and put a roll in front of Rowan.

"Oh, you *legend*," he said.

I sat down, took my Strategy book out of my coat, and opened it to the chapter on supply lines. I had not read it. I slid the card inside, where it made a good bookmark, and the rose lay on the page about horse food. Then I put the book back in my coat, next to the exemption.

Felix had seen the card. He looked at it, looked at me, buttered another roll, and said nothing. That was fine.

The doors at the far end of the hall opened. They opened all through lunch, because people came and went, but these opened slowly. The man who came in did not go to the food windows. He was about forty and wore a plain gray staff coat, so new that the fold lines still showed in the sleeves. He stopped just inside and looked down the long tables like a man matching faces to a picture.

He found me. I am sure of it. He found me as I put the book away, and he watched my hand come out of my coat with nothing in it.

Then he took a small notebook from his pocket and wrote one line. His thumb held the page open, and the nail was black from brazier work or a coal bucket.

*The Golden Seal,* I thought. Ysolde had warned me about them. They were investigators who came up the hill to look at the school, and they looked for one reason. Someone had been blamed, and now someone else had to be found.

He closed the notebook and left. The doors swung shut behind him, slowly.

"Who was that?" Rowan asked with his mouth full.

"Nobody," I said. "Eat your roll."
