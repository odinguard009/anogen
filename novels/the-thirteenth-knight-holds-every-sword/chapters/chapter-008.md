# Chapter 8: Four Notes on the Stair

The workshop doors under the Undercroft had no lamps above them, only numbers cut into the stone, and the numbers had been rubbed smooth by hands. Vesk stopped at the top of the stair and set his back to the wall.

"I can go as far as the turn," he said. "After that it is dark in a way I do not like."

"Stay there," Eden said. "Whistle if anyone comes down."

"Whistle. Like a bird." Vesk's mouth went thin. "You forgot the last time."

Two people waited below him. Gorrath was a giant in a grey coat with a tower shield as tall as a door strapped to his left arm, and he held it careful of the walls, the way a man carries a cup full to the brim. Mirelle stood beside him in a plain black mask with no mouth in it, and a long thin blade low at her hip. She wore her coat buttoned to the throat and said nothing at all.

"Sorry," Gorrath said to the stone as his shield touched it. "Sorry."

"Where do you want us?" he asked.

"The passage," Eden said. "Keep the door. Do not let anyone follow me out."

"Yes." Gorrath shifted his shield. "I am not afraid of the dark."

The shield knocked once against the wall. Mirelle looked at it, then at Eden. "Ready," she said. One word, and she had used it up.

"What if he runs?" Gorrath asked.

"Let him."

"But he has the dust."

"I want to know who sent him more." Eden looked at Mirelle. "Can you pin a sleeve without cutting it?"

"Yes."

"Then we do not need to stop a man. We need a pocket."

Gorrath thought about that. "A pocket does not run."

"No," Eden said. "It does not."

Moonlight came through a high window at the end of the passage and lay on the floor in a pale strip. Eden needed an edge of light, so he walked to the strip and looked at the shadow it threw from a workbench. The shadow had a clean line along the stone. That was enough.

"Stay here," he said. "When I knock, come."

* * *

The workshop held three long tables and a shelf of stone jars. The jars were labelled in chalk, and most of the labels had been wiped. Dust lay on the tables in soft grey ridges, and the moonlight made each ridge shine at the edge.

Eden did not touch anything at first. He counted the doors. One behind him, one at the far end, a high window that no man could reach without a ladder. He read the chalk on the nearest jar: CORE PLATE, USED, one line crossed out. The jar under it was empty, and a thin ring of grey marked where something had sat.

A clipboard hung on a nail by the far door. Eden took it down. The top sheet was an order for one core plate, dated early in November, with a wide careful signature at the bottom.

Master Sorn. The man who had been dead for a year.

He tore the top sheet from the clipboard, put the clipboard back on its nail, and moved to the shelf. Under the third jar he found a little pile of dust on the stone, no bigger than a coin, with a scuff beside it where a hand had swept more into a bag. The dust was the same grey as the dust in the construct's core, fine and heavy at once. It did not shine. It sat in the moonlight and made a dark mark on it.

Boots sounded at the far door.

Eden slipped the sheet into his coat and stepped into the shadow of the workbench. He knocked twice on the stone beside him. A black rectangle opened where the shadow met the wall, with a thin edge of pale light along one side, and he went through it into the dark space behind the bench and was still.

The door at the far end opened. A man came in with a workman's coat pulled up around his ears and a small bag in one hand. Eden could not see his face, but the coat was the grey one from the dining hall and the man carried the same small book he had written in. The man went straight to the shelf, swept the rest of the dust into his bag with two fingers, and turned to go.

Eden knocked once more.

The passage door opened at the same time. Mirelle came through it without a sound, and Gorrath set his shield across the passage behind her with his boots braced.

"No farther," Gorrath said. He turned his head. "I mean it."

The man in the grey coat stopped. His hand went into his sleeve, and Mirelle's blade was already there.

Mirelle's **[Sile]** was a thin blade with a grey line running down the flat of it, so faint it looked like a scratch. She did not strike at the man. She put the blade through the door Eden had opened, and the man's sleeve stayed where the blade had passed through it, held by a thread no thicker than a hair. It pinned cloth to wood without cutting skin and without a sound. Without the door, there was nothing for the thread to hold, so she could only use it where Eden made an opening.

The man pulled once. The thread held. He looked at the shield, at the mask, at the empty space where the door had been, and decided to stand still.

Gorrath cleared his throat. "We do not know what you did," he said. "That is the trouble."

Mirelle reached into the man's coat with her free hand and took a folded paper from the inside pocket. She gave it to Eden. Then she let the blade go, and the sleeve came loose from the wood with a small tear.

"Go," Eden said.

The man went past Gorrath's shield without touching it and was gone up the passage in three steps. Gorrath let him pass. His hands were shaking a little, and he hid them behind the shield.

"I did that right," he said.

"You did," Eden said.

"I lied about not being afraid."

"Yes."

Gorrath shifted the shield and looked at the floor. "I am a terrible liar."

"You were frightened," Eden said.

"Yes."

"Then why say you were not?"

"I wanted you to think I was brave." Gorrath turned the shield over. "Did it work?"

"No."

"I will try again tomorrow."

Eden showed him the sheet from the clipboard. "A dead man's order."

Gorrath read the name and gave it back. "That is not good."

Mirelle looked at the line under the signature. "Names," she said.

"I know," Eden said. "That is why I came down here."

Mirelle put the blade away and said nothing. Eden unfolded the paper. It was a list of names, written small: the master of the forge, two store clerks, a groundskeeper, and a girl from the exchange. One line had been crossed out. The ink on the crossed line was older than the rest.

* * *

The stairs up from the Undercroft came out beside a classroom that still had light in it. Eden stopped at the foot because the light on the floor was not a lamp. It was moonlight, square and pale, running from the window to a desk.

Halvard Strent sat at the desk with a stack of papers in front of him and a pencil in his hand. He was a thin man in a brown coat with his hair cut short, and he had taken off his glasses to read the paper in front of him. He marked a line, made a small face at it, and marked it again. He had not noticed the door.

"I am sorry," he said, to the paper or to the room. "It is late."

Eden put one foot on the top step.

Strent looked up and smiled, and the smile arrived before the apology. "Mr. Wren. I did not hear you. I am sorry. I grade here because the lamps give me headaches. It is very stupid of me, I know. The other masters laugh."

"They do not," Eden said.

"They are kind about it, which is worse." Strent put the pencil down. "Are you looking for someone? The workshops are locked at this hour."

"I was downstairs."

"Of course." Strent looked at Eden's coat, then at his boots, and said nothing about either. "You have dust on your sleeve. Here, let me." He brushed at it with a handkerchief and missed, then tried again. "There. I am afraid it is ground in."

He went back to his papers. As he read the next line, he hummed.

It was four notes. They went up, then down, then the same four again, quiet enough to be a breath. Eden's other foot stayed on the floor. He had one hand flat on the stair rail, and the rail was cold under his glove. He counted the notes. Four, four. The cold went up his wrist. He did not take the next step. He did not let go of the rail.

Strent hummed the four notes a third time and stopped in the middle of them, as if he had forgotten the rest. He looked at the paper again. "Ah. That is the trouble with songs. You remember the first part all your life and lose the end."

His pencil moved across the page. "You look frozen, Mr. Wren. Would you like my coat?"

"No."

"Quite right. I am always cold myself." He put the paper aside and glanced at the window. "It is a fine night for the moon. I used to think it was a poor light to work by, but it is honest."

Eden took the next step. The rail came out of his hand, and his glove left a warm mark on the stone for a moment and then it was gone. He walked into the classroom and set the folded paper on the corner of Strent's desk.

"That was found downstairs," he said. "It is not mine."

Strent looked at it without touching it. "Then I shall put it with the lost things."

"Yes."

"Is there anything else?"

"No."

"Are you in trouble, Mr. Wren?"

"No."

"Good. The office sends me the ones who are, and I never know what to say to them." Strent picked up his pencil. "I say I am sorry, and then they feel worse."

Eden said no again, and Strent went back to his papers. The hum did not come again. At the door Eden stopped, and Strent's pencil stopped too, and the moonlight lay across both their boots.

"Wren," Strent said. "A Thornmere name. Your mother must have sung that to you too."
