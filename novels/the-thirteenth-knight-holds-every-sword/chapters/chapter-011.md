# Chapter 11: Nine Minutes of Somebody Else's Maze

"Who's carrying the slate?"

Nobody answered. Okoru waited with a piece of dried licorice root in his cheek, and he never shared it with anyone. He held the slate out at the end of his one arm and asked again, with his eyebrows this time.

"Hollen has the slate," said Felix.

"I have the Committee's clipboard," said Lysette. "That's different."

"Is it?"

"It has a clip."

Okoru dropped the slate into Clara's hands, because she stood closest and looked like the kind of person who would take it. She looked at it the way she looked at most things, with no opinion at all, and put it under her arm.

We stood in the first gallery of the cellars under the school, cold and made of stone. It was morning, but no sun came down here. A green-white glowstone hung over the big door, and the air smelled of wet rock. Four teams had gone in already that day. The best one was on the board at six minutes forty, and nobody would beat that. The star-readers had finished at nineteen minutes with a burnt sleeve, and they were still arguing about whose fault it was.

"Graded field run," said Okoru. "Nine minutes on the glass. Every Lance gets a different maze, so don't ask the ones who finished. They don't know yours, and they would lie anyway."

"What's in it?" said Rowan. He had been bouncing on his heels since we came down.

"Walls that move. Some walls that aren't there. At the fourth minute the Kennel opens. The Kennel is whatever the bursar could pay for."

"What's the Kennel?"

"A lesson in saving money." Okoru turned the glass over. "Tenets are allowed. Relics are not. The exit is where the exit is. Go."

The door swung open on a passage as wide as two men, the color of wet slate. Rowan ran through before the first sand had finished falling.

* * *

I knew one thing after forty steps, and I knew it from the door frame before I even looked. The lamps were wrong.

Every turn down here has a glowstone on a chain, a lump the size of a thumb. Each one makes one shadow under a bench or a boot, and that is all. Here every shadow had a second one lying faintly under it, like a ghost. All the faint ones leaned the same way, down the passage and off to the left.

That meant one very strong light far ahead, and I knew what it was. Whoever built this maze had hung a big lamp over the exit. They had forgotten that light goes around corners.

I did not say so. I was the boy who slept, the one with sixty-one absences and a lance for blocking.

"Dead end!" Rowan called from the front.

It looked like one, with a rough wall, a gutter and a rusty ring in the stone. Lysette stepped up beside him and touched the wall with two fingers. Her eyes went pale, and the edge of each one lit up like the rim of a coin.

The wall remembered for her. A ghost-light rose in the stone, a small gray man in a leather apron. He pulled a painted canvas across the passage and dropped a bar to hold it, all in the time of a blink. Then he faded, and Lysette lifted her hand.

"It's canvas," she said. "Two bars, top and bottom. Someone put it there this morning."

"You *saw* that?" said Rowan.

"I'm a Witness. That's what the word means, you know."

He kicked out the bottom bar, and the canvas fell flat. More passage lay behind it. Clara stepped over it and said to nobody, "She's good."

"Don't say it like that," said Lysette. "It's just my job."

"It's a good job," said Clara.

Lysette's neck went red above her collar. She wrote something on her clipboard, and I am sure it was not a mark.

I stayed at the back and watched the faint shadows. At the second turn the passage split three ways. Felix went down on one knee, because his Tenet, Trail, lets him hear where people have walked. It is quiet and does not glow. He just goes still, like a man trying to hear one voice at a loud table, and then he pointed right.

"Boots," he said. "Heavy ones, going in and out. The floor is worn smooth."

"Worn means people walk it," said Lysette.

"People walk every maze to set it up. But this one was walked a hundred times." He frowned. "That's more than you need."

"So it's the right way," said Rowan. "Come on."

"Or it's bait," I said.

They all looked at me, and I hate that.

"Left," I said. "I think. I'm guessing."

"You're guessing," said Lysette.

"My Tenet makes shadows do things. It's only a feeling." I gave the shrug I had used for years, one shoulder, and it looked sorry. "Left feels deeper."

Felix looked at the left branch. Then he looked at me, half a second longer than he needed to, and stood up.

"Left," he said, as a man does who has decided to be wrong with friends.

We walked a long way down that passage before I stopped watching the back of his neck.

* * *

At the fourth minute the Kennel opened, and the bursar had bought what he could pay for.

Five of them came out of the dark in a line. The board called them dust-hounds, and each was a frame of cane with canvas stretched over the ribs. They had sand in the belly for weight and a tin bell for a tongue. They were cheap and fast and not funny at all.

One tin tongue rang, and then all of them rang. The noise came down the passage, sharp as a sack of knives.

"Back!" Felix had his bow out before the second ring. "Everyone back!"

The rule was no relics, but plain weapons were fine. He shot an arrow through the front leg of the first hound, and it tripped over its own foot and crashed into the second. They fell in a pile against the gutter, rattling, and sand poured out of one like stuffing from a torn pillow.

"Three left," said Clara. Her voice was flat as a bench.

The three split around the pile. Two ran at Felix and one ran at Rowan.

"Get behind me!" Rowan told everyone.

"Don't!" said Lysette. "You're in the open! You don't have a line! You're—"

He lit his hand. It was Spark, a small gold flare the size of a lantern. It flew up into the cane faces, and whatever the hounds saw with, it was not made for gold. Two froze and swung their heads from side to side, as if the passage had changed shape. The third ran into the wall, chasing its own shadow.

Rowan was grinning. "Did you see that?" he said. "Did you see my hands?" Then he saw Felix's arm.

"Huh," said Felix.

A broken cane rib had cut him, not deep, but the blood ran out in a thin line like ink from a pen. He looked at it with polite interest. Clara was already beside him. She had not run. She had been standing there, and then she was next to him, holding his wrist in both of her small hands. Her lips moved on words I could not hear. You do not watch that part, so I watched his sleeve instead, and the edges of the cut came together and turned pink and closed.

"Oh," said Felix. "That's strange." He sounded as if she had pulled a coin from his ear.

For about four seconds we were a real Lance. Rowan's light held the hounds, Felix loaded his bow, and Lysette took two steps left and called the weak points.

"The bottom rib! It's tied there! All four are tied there!"

Felix shot the knot on the nearest hound, and it came apart in the middle of a step. Then Rowan's hand began to shake and his light went out. The next hound turned around.

"Run," I said, in the voice I keep for bad news in a warehouse. Nobody argued.

* * *

I used to think the worst part of being chased by a sandbag was the noise. It is not. The worst part is that it never stops, because it has no mind to lose. A hound that has your footsteps keeps them until its sand runs out, around corners and down stairs and over a floor someone just washed.

We ran with the glass ticking in our heads. Rowan carried Clara over a gap she could never have jumped alone. Felix hit a wall twice and swore in a language I did not know he had.

"Was that real swearing?" Rowan panted.

"Run," said Felix. "Ask later."

At a square junction under a nearly dead lamp, Lysette stopped so fast that I almost hit her. Two passages lay ahead, both lit and both empty, and behind us the tin tongues were close.

"Which?" she said.

Both shadows leaned left. The faint ones, the borrowed ones, all pointed the same way, but I could not tell her why I was sure. I would have had to tell her what I was.

"That one," I said. "Please."

The *please* worked, because I did not say it often. She looked at me once, fast, and went.

The left passage grew smaller until it became a crawl under a low arch. A rusty gate hung halfway down it, and you had to go under on your stomach. Lysette went first, then Clara, then Rowan with his shoulders turned sideways. Felix reached back through the gap.

"Hand!" he said.

I took it, and he pulled. The first hound reached the arch just as my boots cleared it. It pushed its head under the bar and one paw into the passage, and its tin tongue rang at my ankle.

I kicked it, badly, the way a lazy boy would. My shoe came off in its canvas mouth.

Lysette had waited on the other side to see if I had made it. "You lost a shoe," she said.

"Yes."

"And your face." She held her clipboard up as a shield and frowned at the spot above my eyebrow. "That's still open."

"I walked into a door. It will close."

"It's been nine days." She said it fast, and then she looked at the clipboard as if the number might be written there. "It's Committee records. If it's infected, I'm the one who fills in the form."

"It's not infected."

"Fine." She dug in her sleeve and held out a square of waxed cloth with tape across it. It had a stamp. "It's for the floor, Wren. You're bleeding on the floor."

I was not, but I took it anyway and pressed it over the cut. It was warm from her sleeve, and I had not expected that.

Ahead the passage ended at a plain gray door with a brass ring. Above it hung the biggest glowstone I had seen down there. The exit.

* * *

We came out at eight minutes fifty-one. Okoru was waiting with the glass. He turned it over, looked at the sand and then at me.

"Third," he said. "Behind the best team and the one from the north. You lose a minute for the hounds you didn't tag." He chewed. "At the second turn you went left. Your friend's Trail said right. And then left was right. Nobody does that by feel."

"It was a guess," I said.

"Was it."

That was all he said. Okoru leaves a sentence hanging for you to pick up, and I did not pick it up.

"Are you hurt?" Okoru asked the room. No one answered, so he nodded, as if that were an answer.

Everyone was bent over or sitting. Rowan lay flat on the stone with his arms out. "I can never move again," he said. "Tell my mother I was brave."

"Your mother lives in the next town," said Felix. "Tell her yourself."

"You can," said Clara. "You'll just complain."

She sat beside him and looked at the ceiling. Lysette wrote with a very straight back and the face of someone who had found two things she was not ready to write down.

I lifted my head and looked up. The finish hall had a narrow iron balcony along the north wall, for the judges, and today it was empty except for one man. I knew his gray staff coat, because the cloth was still stiff and creased at the sleeves. He leaned on the rail with his arms crossed. He was not watching the board or the other Lance, and he was not watching me.

He was watching Rowan.

His thumb lay along his sleeve, and the nail was black. I had thought it was coal a few weeks ago, but coal smears. This was a deep brown-black with a yellow edge, the stain you get from oak-bark liquor. That was what Ysolde had written on the paper she gave me. *Tannery streets.*

I blinked. I did not mean to. Rowan laughed at something Felix said. It was so loud that it bounced off the roof, and the man on the balcony stood up straight as if the sound had told him what he wanted to know.

By the time I turned all the way around, the balcony was empty. The door at the end of it was swinging shut on its own weight, slowly, like a door someone had closed with great care so it would not slam.
