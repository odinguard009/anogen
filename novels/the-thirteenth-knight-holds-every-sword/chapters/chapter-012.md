# Chapter 12: Perrin

The bench behind the stables faced a wall that kept the sun until the middle of the day, and on Tuesday the wall was warm enough to sit against without a coat. Hay stuck out of the stable door, and a horse somewhere inside kicked once at nothing. A line of laundry hung above the yard with two grey shirts on it, and a cat slept under the bench with its tail over its nose. The wall was warm through Eden's coat, and he could feel the heat coming into his back one inch at a time. Eden sat at one end of the bench with a strategy book open on his knee and had not turned a page in ten minutes.

Felix came round the corner with a paper bag in one hand and his book in the other. He sat down at the far end, which meant he wanted to talk, and set the bag between them.

"Have you eaten today?" he said.

"A little."

Felix looked into the bag. "I have two buns here. Do you want one?"

"Yes."

Felix took out two buns and gave the larger one to Eden. "I bought these from the woman at the gate. She puts too much sugar on them."

Eden bit into the bun and found that she had. "It is good."

"It is too sweet. That is why I bought two." Felix opened his book and then shut it again. "Why did you come here?"

"The wall is warm."

"Everyone knows about this wall, but you never come here. You sit in the hall or on the roof." Felix pulled a thread off the bench. "I want to know one thing. Did you come here to be alone, or did you come because I was not on the roof?"

The cat under the bench opened one eye and shut it again. Eden waited three seconds before he answered. "Both."

"That is honest, at least." Felix leaned back against the wall. "I used to ask where you went at night. I stopped because you never lied to me. You only told me true things that helped nothing."

"I tried."

"I know." Felix looked at the horse door. "So I will ask a different question. First, a story. My mother told me to sit with the quiet boy in the first week," Felix said. "She said he would have no one if I did not. I sat down and you looked at me for a long time, and then you moved your bag to make room."

"I remember."

"The next day you sat in the same place. That was when I decided to stay." Felix picked at the paper bag. "In five years I have never asked you this. Why does nobody get close to you?"

The horse kicked again, and a stable boy somewhere out of sight told it to be quiet. Eden read the same line in his book twice. He could have said that he slept in the day and was busy at night, or that people who got close asked questions he could not answer. Both were true. Neither was the reason.

"I do not know how," he said.

"How to let someone close?"

"Yes."

Felix turned his bun in his hand without eating it. "That is sad, but it is true." Felix said it flat, with no anger. "I am not testing you. I am your friend, and I still do not know what to ask you. You never ask me anything either."

"I ask if you are eating."

"That is your one question." Felix smiled at the wall. "It is a good one. I just wish you had another."

Eden finished the bun. There was sugar at the corner of his mouth, and he wiped it on his glove and put the glove back on before Felix could say anything about it.

A bell rang somewhere over the yard, and the stable boy led a brown horse out through the door and past the bench. The horse stopped to sniff at Felix's paper bag, and Felix moved it out of reach without looking up from his book. The boy led the horse away, and its hoofprints filled with water from the last of the rain.

"My name is Felix Perrin Ashgrove," Felix said. "Perrin is my middle name. Only my mother uses it. She writes it on my letters, even though I have told her six times that the postman does not need it."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you have told me nothing, and I do not mind. But I wanted to give you something first. It is not a trade. Now you know one thing about me that nobody else in this year knows."

Eden looked at the paper bag, which had a crease where Felix had folded the top over twice. "Eden Wren," he said.

"I know your name."

"That is all I have."

"There must be more."

"No."

Felix was quiet for a while. A cart went past on the far side of the stable and its wheels threw water up against the wall. "Is Wren your real name?"

"It is the name I use." Eden closed the book. He had not turned a page, and he did not pretend he had. "I cannot give you a middle name. I have another name, but I cannot say it out loud."

That other name belonged to a house, a father and a room he had not seen for years. He said none of that. The silence lasted until the cat stood up, stretched, and lay down in a different place.

Felix nodded once, as if that were enough. It was not enough, and he knew it. He was being kind, and he did not ask the next question.

"Then you keep Perrin," Felix said. "Do not write it down."

"I will not."

"If you ever have a name you can say, tell me then. I am not going anywhere."

Eden watched a drop of water run down the stable wall and break on the bench. "Thank you," he said.

"For the bun?"

"For not asking again."

Felix picked up his book and opened it at the page he had been reading, and the two of them sat against the warm wall without talking. That was how Felix did it. He stayed until the silence was ordinary and then let it be.

* * *

The sound of a pencil tapped against a clipboard came round the corner before Lysette did. She stopped three steps from the bench with two paper bags in her hands and her black book under one arm. The band on her sleeve was pinned straight, and she was not looking at Eden.

"Committee work," she said. "You skip meals and you skip class. I will not lose track of you."

Felix looked up. "We are only behind the stables."

"At lunch time. I wrote that down." She held out a bag to Felix. "Yours. I wrote it down as two rolls and an apple."

"Thank you." Felix took it with a straight face.

She held the second bag out toward Eden, still looking at Felix. "Yours. There is tea as well. It is in the cup and it will go cold while you decide whether to take it."

Eden took the bag. "I ate a bun."

"A bun is not lunch." Her pencil touched the page. "I have written 'bun, partial' in the space. Do not make me write a worse word."

"I will eat it."

"Good." She turned a page. "You have had one bun today. I know because the prefect at the gate asked the woman who sold it. It took four minutes. Do not be impressed. It is procedure." Her gloves lay folded on her knee, and one had a faint green stain on the thumb from the roof garden.

Felix stood up with his bag and his book. "I will leave you to your questions." He took two steps, turned back, and said, "Perrin," to Lysette with a small nod. Then he went away around the corner.

Lysette's pencil stopped. "Why did he say that?"

"It is his middle name."

"I did not know he had one."

She looked at the bench, at the second bag in Eden's hand, and then at the stable wall, anywhere rather than at him. "Sit down and eat," she said. "I will write that you did."

She sat at the other end of the bench, four feet from him, and opened her book. The pencil moved for a while and then stopped. "Your handkerchief," she said, to the page.

"It is yours to keep."

"It is not. I said I would wash it." She turned a leaf. "I have washed it twice in my head. The first time the water was too hot. The second time I forgot the soap." Her ears went pink. "That is not a thing to write down."

"I will not tell."

"You will not need to. I have it in the book already, under the heading of things I must not do." She did not look up. "Eat your roll."

He ate it. The roll was still soft, and someone had cut a small cross in the top, the way the kitchen did for people it liked. Eden did not know if Lysette had asked for that.

From behind the corner, where Felix had clearly not left, a flat voice said, "Did you eat?" Lysette wrote down the answer without being told it. The cat walked across all three laps in turn and settled on Eden's knee.

Eden took the cup out of the bag. It was a white cup with a chip on the rim, the kind the kitchen used for staff, and the tea was still warm. The wind had turned, and the smell of hay came across the yard instead of wet stone. He drank half of it before he remembered to check whether she was watching.

She was writing. She had not looked at him once since she came round the corner. The line she wrote was short, and from four feet away he could not read it.

"I will read it to you," she said, because he had been looking. "Wren. Behind the stables. Ate a bun, a roll and half a cup of tea. Two witnesses."

"Felix is the second witness?"

"Felix is the second witness. The cat does not count." She turned the page. "Now I have a real question, and it is not for the book. Okoru gave us the Southern Wall in January. Four weeks. We leave on the fourth of January and come back on the first of February."

"I know. It is on the card."

"The card does not say who carries the food." Her pencil tapped once. "On the Wall, nobody will bring you a bag. If you skip a meal there, you will fall asleep standing up, and Rowan will walk into a Hushborn trying to wake you."

"He would."

"So you must learn to eat on a plan. I am not asking you to like it. Three meals, same hour, every day, until January."

Eden looked at the cup. The tea had a thin skin of cold on top now. "Where?"

"Here. This bench. It is warm, and nobody asks you anything." She did not look up. "I will bring the bags. Felix will bring the buns, because he cannot stop himself."

"Yes," Eden said.

She wrote one word and underlined it once. A bell rang over the yard, and the stable boy came out to take the cat away from Eden's knee. It went without a fight.

Lysette closed her book and stood. "Tomorrow, the sixth bell, here," she said. "If you are late, I will write down the reason, and the reason had better be good."

Eden stood too. His legs had gone stiff from sitting so long, and the sun had moved off the wall while they talked. For the first time in a month he walked to class from a meal, not from the roof, and he noticed that it did not feel very different.
