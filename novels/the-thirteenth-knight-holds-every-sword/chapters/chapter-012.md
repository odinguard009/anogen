# Chapter 12: Perrin

The bench behind the stables faced a wall that kept the sun until the middle of the day, and on Tuesday the wall was warm enough to sit against without a coat. Hay stuck out of the stable door, and a horse somewhere inside kicked once at nothing. A line of laundry hung above the yard with two grey shirts on it, and a cat slept under the bench with its tail over its nose. The wall was warm through Eden's coat, and he could feel the heat coming into his back one inch at a time. Eden sat at one end of the bench with a strategy book open on his knee and had not turned a page in ten minutes.

Felix came round the corner with a paper bag in one hand and his book in the other. He sat down at the far end, which meant he wanted to talk, and set the bag between them.

"Eaten today?" he said.

"Technically."

Felix looked into the bag. "That is a bun, not a technically."

"It is in the food family."

"There is a whole family of things you have invented." Felix took out two buns and gave the larger one to Eden. "I bought these from the woman at the gate. She puts too much sugar on them."

Eden bit into the bun and found that she had. "It is good."

"It is too sweet. That is why I bought two." Felix opened his book and then shut it again. "Why did you come here?"

"The wall is warm."

"Everyone knows about this wall. You never come. You sit in the hall or on the roof." Felix pulled a thread off the bench. "I asked because I wanted to know if you came here to be alone or if you came here because I was not there."

The cat under the bench opened one eye and shut it again. Eden counted three seconds before he answered, which was long enough for Felix to notice and short enough that he did not write it down.

Eden thought about that for a moment. "Both."

"That is not an answer, but it is honest." Felix leaned back against the wall. "I used to ask where you went at night. I stopped because you never lied. You just said things that were true and useless."

"I tried."

"I know." Felix looked at the horse door. "That is why I am asking a different question now."

"My mother told me to sit with the quiet boy in the first week," Felix said. "She said he would have no one if I did not. I sat down and you looked at me for a long time, and then you moved your bag."

"I moved it."

"Then you came back the next day and sat in the same place." Felix picked at the paper bag. "That was when I decided to stay. I have one question I never asked."

"Which is?"

"Why does nobody get close to you?"

The horse kicked again, and a stable boy somewhere out of sight told it to be quiet. Eden read the same line in his book twice. He could have said that he slept in the day and was busy at night, or that people who got close asked questions he could not answer. Both were true. Neither was the reason.

"I do not know how," he said.

"How to let someone close?"

"Yes."

Felix turned his bun in his hand without eating it. "That is worse than a lie."

"I know."

"I did not mean that as a test. I asked because I am your friend and I have been for five years, and I still do not know what to ask you." Felix said it flat, without any heat in it. "You never ask me anything either."

"I asked if you were eating."

"That is your one question." Felix smiled at the wall. "It is a good question. I just wish you had another."

Eden finished the bun. There was sugar at the corner of his mouth, and he wiped it on his glove and put the glove back on before Felix could say anything about it.

A bell rang somewhere over the yard, and the stable boy led a brown horse out through the door and past the bench. The horse stopped to sniff at Felix's paper bag, and Felix moved it out of reach without looking up from his book. The boy led the horse away, and its hoofprints filled with water from the last of the rain.

"My name is Felix Perrin Ashgrove," Felix said. "Perrin is my middle name. Only my mother uses it, and she writes it on the letters even though I have told her six times that the postman does not need it."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you told me nothing." Felix took a breath and went on. "If you cannot give me something, I wanted to give you something first. Then it is not a trade. It is just a thing I know about you that nobody else in this year knows."

Eden looked at the paper bag, which had a crease where Felix had folded the top over twice. "Eden Wren," he said.

"I know your name."

"That is all I have."

"There must be more."

"No."

Felix was quiet for a while. A cart went past on the far side of the stable and its wheels threw water up against the wall. "Is Wren not your real name?"

"Yes."

"Then why say it like that?"

"Because it is the one I use." Eden closed the book. He had not turned a page, and he did not pretend he had. "I cannot give you a middle name. I cannot give you the other one either. I do not have one I can say out loud."

There was a name that was not Wren, and it belonged to a house and a father and a room he had not seen for years. It would not fit in this yard. He said none of that, and the silence lasted long enough for the cat to stand up, stretch, and lie down again in a different place.

Felix nodded once, as if that were a complete answer, which it was not. He was being kind about it, and Eden could tell because he did not ask the next question.

"Then I will keep Perrin," Felix said. "You can have it. Do not write it down."

"I will not."

"And if you ever have a name you can say, you can tell me then. I am not going anywhere."

Eden watched a drop of water run down the stable wall and break on the bench. "Thank you," he said.

"For the bun?"

"For not asking again."

Felix picked up his book and opened it at the page he had been reading, and the two of them sat against the warm wall without talking. That was how Felix did it. He stayed until the silence was ordinary and then let it be.

* * *

The sound of a pencil tapped against a clipboard came round the corner before Lysette did. She stopped three steps from the bench with two paper bags in her hands and her black book under one arm. The band on her sleeve was pinned straight, and she was not looking at Eden.

"Committee efficiency," she said. "You skip meals and you skip class. I am not losing track of you."

Felix looked up. "We are behind the stables."

"You are behind the stables at lunch. That is a place, not a reason." She held out a bag to Felix. "Yours. I wrote it down as two rolls and an apple."

"Thank you, ma'am." Felix took it with the straight face he used for jokes he intended to keep.

She held the second bag out toward Eden, still looking at Felix. "Yours. There is tea as well. It is in the cup and it will go cold while you decide whether to take it."

Eden took the bag. "I ate a bun."

"A bun is not lunch." Her pencil touched the page. "I have written 'bun, partial' in the space. Do not make me write a third word."

"I will eat it."

"Good." She turned a page and read something from it to herself. "Rowan says you were in the maze on Saturday. He says you found the door."

"I read a shadow."

"Felix found the tracks. I wrote that down as well." She read from the page. "Two rolls, an apple, tea. That is three things in one bag, which is more than I expected from the kitchen." She looked at the wall above his head. "You have had one bun today. I know because I asked the woman at the gate."

"You asked the woman at the gate?"

"I asked the prefect on the gate. The prefect asked her. It is a short chain and it takes four minutes." She wrote something and covered it with her hand. "Do not be impressed. It is procedure." She smoothed the page with the side of her hand, then checked the corner of the bag to see that it was closed. Her gloves were folded on her knee, and one of them had a faint green stain on the thumb from the roof garden.

"Rowan says a lot of things. I write down the useful ones."

Felix stood up with his bag and his book. "I will go and let you finish the interview." He walked two steps, then came back for the thread he had dropped on the bench. "Perrin," he said to Lysette, with a small nod, and went away around the corner.

Lysette's pencil stopped. "Why did he say that?"

"It is his middle name."

"He has a middle name."

"Yes."

She looked at the bench, at the second bag in Eden's hand, and then at the stable wall, anywhere rather than at him. "Sit down and eat," she said. "I will write that you did."

She sat at the other end of the bench, four feet from him, and opened her book. The pencil moved for a while and then stopped. "Your handkerchief," she said, to the page.

"It is yours to keep."

"It is not. I said I would wash it." She turned a leaf. "I have washed it twice in my head. The first time the water was too hot. The second time I forgot the soap." Her ears went pink. "That is not a thing to write down."

"I will not tell."

"You will not need to. I have it in the book already, under the heading of things I must not do." She did not look up. "Eat your roll."

He ate it. The roll was still soft, and someone had cut a small cross in the top of it, the way the kitchen did for the people it liked. Eden did not think Lysette had asked the kitchen for that. He did not think she had not. When he finished, Felix, who had stopped behind the corner and had clearly not left, said "Did you eat?" through the wall in a flat voice, and Lysette wrote down the answer without being told it. The cat on the bench stretched, walked across all three laps in turn, and settled on Eden's knee as if the matter had been decided in committee. Eden took the cup out of the bag. It was a white cup with a chip on the rim, the kind the kitchen used for the staff, and the tea was still warm. The wind had gone round the stable so that the smell of hay came across the yard instead of the smell of wet stone. He drank half of it before he remembered to check whether she was watching. She was writing. She had not looked at him once since she came round the corner, and the line she wrote was short enough that he could not read it from four feet away.
