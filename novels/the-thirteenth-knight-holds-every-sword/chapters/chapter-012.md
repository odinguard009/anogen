# Chapter 12: Two Lunches

The wall behind the stables was low and made of warm stone, and the hay in the yard had been stacked up to the eaves. Eden sat on the wall with his back against the post and his boots on the lower rail, and the smell of horses and wet straw came through his coat. Felix came around the corner at noon with a paper bag in one hand and his bow case on his shoulder.

"Eaten today?" Felix asked.

"Yes," Eden said.

Felix sat down beside him and held out the bag. "That is a lie, but it is a polite one."

"It is a small lie."

"The small ones are the ones you tell every day." Felix opened the bag and took out two buns, and he gave one to Eden. "I brought extra, because the kitchen ran out of the dark bread."

Eden took the bun and looked at it. It had a crack across the top and a little salt in the crust, and it was still warm. "Why are we eating here?"

"The dining hall is loud, and the stables are quiet." Felix bit into his bun and chewed for a while. "Also, I wanted to ask you something, and I did not want an audience."

Eden ate half the bun in four bites and held the rest in his hand. "Ask."

Felix looked at the haystack and then at the yard gate. "Why do you never let anyone close?"

Eden said nothing.

"Not the other people," Felix said. "Me. You answer me when I ask, and you sit here with me, and you still keep one step back. I can feel it."

"A step is a small distance."

"It is a distance you put there."

"Put where?" Eden asked.

"You know where." Felix did not look at him. "You go still, and your answers get shorter, and then they are true but small."

"They are small because the questions are small."

"Ask me one back, then." Felix turned to him. "If they are so small, ask me one."

Eden took a breath and held it, and the breath did not turn into a question. He let it go and looked at the horses instead. "What did your father want from this?"

"My father sent me here to be friends with you," Felix said. "He gave me a list of three names, and yours was on it, and he told me to sit with you at lunch."

"Why?"

"He said it was a debt." Felix took a straw from the stack and bent it in two. "I thought it would be boring. I brought you peppermints because they were in my pocket, and you did not say thank you."

"You did not say you were sorry."

"Sorry," Felix said.

Eden looked at him, and the corner of his mouth moved, and then it stopped. "You stayed after the three names were done."

"I stayed because you ate the peppermints."

"That is a thin reason."

"That was the reason I had then." Felix broke the straw again. "Now I have more, and I am not telling you all of them."

Eden looked across the yard. A groom led a grey horse out of the stables and tied it to the rail, and the horse stamped once and stood still. The wall was warm under him, and the sun had reached the top of the haystack, and he could have stayed there all afternoon.

"I am not good at being close," he said.

"You can want it and still be bad at it."

"No," Eden said. "It is not."

Felix finished his bun and brushed the crumbs off his coat. He had a quiet way of waiting, and he let the silence sit for as long as it needed, and then he filled it with something small. "Seraphine asked me if you were a wall."

"What did you tell her?"

"That you were a door, and the door was locked."

"Why?"

"Because it was funny." Felix reached into the bag and took out a third bun, and he broke it in half and gave Eden the larger piece. "She laughed, and then she wrote something in her book. I think I am at six now."

"At six what?"

"Jokes." Felix said it flatly, and then he looked at Eden, and they both said nothing for a moment.

A bell rang over the academy, and somewhere in the yard a horse kicked a door. The horse kicked again, and the groom went in to check on it, and the yard settled. A cart rolled past on the far path with its wheels wrapped in cloth, and the driver held a whip but did not use it. Eden ate the second piece of bun and licked the salt from his thumb. "You asked me why I keep a step back," he said. "I do not have a good answer."

"Give me the bad one."

"People who get close ask questions, and I do not like answering them."

"Which questions?"

"Who I am. Where I sleep. Why I know things I should not know." Eden kept his eyes on the horse. "There is no answer I can give that is both true and safe."

"Then give me the true one."

Eden turned to him. Felix was looking at the yard gate, and his face was calm, and he had asked the question and it were about the weather.

"I cannot," Eden said.

"Because it is not safe?"

"Because I do not know it yet."

Felix nodded once, and he put the empty bag on the wall between them. "That is an answer."

"It is not much of one."

"It is more than you gave me last month." Felix leaned back on his hands. "I will wait, and I will ask again when you know."

Eden did not answer. The horses in the stables moved in their stalls, and a stable boy whistled a tune with three notes and got it wrong twice. Felix reached into his coat pocket and took out a small piece of chalk, and he wrote a word on the stone wall between them. The letters were neat and small, and the chalk was white against the grey.

"Perrin," Eden read. "Is that your name?"

"My middle name." Felix put the chalk away. "My mother gave it to me, and my father's people never used it, and I like it better than Felix. It is not a thing I tell many people."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you gave me a true answer, and I wanted to give you one back." Felix looked at the word on the wall. "You cannot tell me yours, and that is all right. You said it plainly, and I believe you."

Eden looked at the chalk word for a while. The letters had a small curl at the end of the last letter, and the grey dust had already begun to fill the lines.

"I have a middle name," he said.

"I know you do."

"I cannot tell you."

"I know." Felix took the bun bag and folded it into a square. "You are hard work, Wren, but you are honest hard work."

Eden put his hand on the wall beside the word and left it there. The stone was warm, and the chalk was cool under his fingers, and the two temperatures met in one small place. He did not say anything, and Felix did not ask him to.

The chalk word stayed on the wall through the afternoon, and then rain came and ran down the stone in a thin line, and the letters went soft and ran together. Eden saw them go when he came back for evening drill. He did not wipe the wall, and he did not write anything beside the word.

Rowan came past with Clara on the path to the practice yard, and he saw them on the wall and raised one hand. "Lunch!" he called. "We saved you a table!"

"We are eating," Eden said.

"With Felix!" Rowan said, as if that made it better, and Clara put a hand on his sleeve and pulled him on. She looked back once at the wall, and then she walked on with her head down.

Eden watched them go. The path was bright with sun, and Rowan's voice came back around the stable corner, and Eden did not wish it would stop. That was odd enough to notice, so he ate his bun and did not think about it.

* * *

They sat until the second bell, and then Lysette came around the corner of the stables with two paper parcels in her hands. She walked fast and stopped in front of the wall, and her coat was buttoned to the top and her book was under one arm.

"Committee efficiency," she said. "You skip meals and you skip class. I am not losing track of you."

She held out the parcels, and she looked at the horses, and then at the gate, and then at the haystack. She did not look at Eden.

Felix took one parcel and opened it. "You brought two lunches."

"I brought two, because one of you is thin and one of you never eats enough. This is not kindness. It is paperwork."

"It tastes like kindness," Felix said.

"It is soup and bread." Lysette set the second parcel on the wall beside Eden's hand and kept her eyes on the yard gate. "The soup is still warm, and I walked fast, so eat it while it is."

Eden took the parcel and opened it. There was a small bowl of soup inside with a lid of thick paper, and a piece of dark bread, and an apple. The soup smelled of onion and pepper, and the steam had warmed the paper through.

"Why are you not looking at me?" Eden asked.

"You are looking at me," Lysette said. "That is enough for both of us."

"I am asking a question."

"You are asking a question you do not want an answer to."

Felix stood up and took his bow case from the wall. "I will go and let you two finish this."

"No," Eden said.

"Yes," Felix said, and he walked toward the gate with his hands in his pockets. "Same time tomorrow?" he called back, and he did not wait for the answer.

Lysette stayed standing with her book against her chest. She watched Felix go, and then she looked at the wall behind them, and at the horses, and at the ground.

"I am not hungry," she said.

"You brought two lunches."

"I brought two because I knew you would not come in." She pushed the hair back from her face with one hand and kept her chin turned away. "Eat the soup, Wren. It is cold out here, and it is the only warm thing you will get before evening drill." She said it in the same voice she used for the round sheet, and the parcel sat on the wall between them with a small patch of warmth under the paper.

Eden lifted the paper lid and took a spoon from the parcel. The soup was thick and hot, and he ate three spoonfuls while she stood beside him. She did not sit down, and she did not look at him, and she wrote one line in her black book without turning the page toward him.

"Is the soup good?" she asked.

"It is soup."

"That is not a yes."

"It is a yes for soup."

Lysette shut the book and held it with both hands. "You and your bread," she said, and she started walking toward the gate. "Finish the apple as well."

She walked fast, and her book was tight under her arm, and she did not turn at the gate. Eden watched the white collar go out of sight, and then he looked at the two parcels on the wall. Her soup had gone cold at the top and warm in the middle, and he had eaten all of it.

"She always brings two," he said.

"She brings two every day," Felix said from the gate. "One for you and one for me, and she tells us it is paperwork."

"Is it paperwork?"

"No," Felix said. "It is the only way she knows how to do it."

Eden ate the apple after she was gone. He put the core on the wall beside the chalk word, and the birds took it before the bell rang again.
