# Chapter 13: The Empty Frame

The lamp-house on the Long Stair had twelve oil lamps hanging from a rack, and each one threw a small hard shadow on the white wall. The lamplighter stood under them with a pole in his hand and his back to the door, and he had been dead for some time before Eden came in.

Vesk had smelled him from the stair. "Veil," he said, outside. "It is wearing a man who carried lamps. I cannot come in, you know that."

"How many calls?" Eden asked.

"Four this week. All from houses on the Long Stair." Vesk's yellow eyes moved over the dark behind him. "It likes stairs. People look up when they walk them, and a lamp looks like a friend."

Eden went in alone. The floor was wet with rain brought in from the stair, and the lamp oil smelled of nuts and smoke. The lamplighter turned with a face that was too calm for a man who worked nights, and his smile arrived one beat after his eyes.

"Late tonight," the man said, and his voice came out in three voices at once, an old man, a boy and a woman, all saying the same words together.

Eden stopped by the door and looked at the room before he spoke. It was a small square room. The rack of lamps hung in the middle, twelve lamps, twelve shadows on the white wall. One door behind him, one pole with a hook in front of him. If the Veil reached him, it would reach him with that hook. His plan was simple: let the shadows work for him.

He kept his hands where the man could see them. "You are the one calling the houses."

"I carry the light." The lamplighter lifted the pole, and the end of it had a hook for pulling lamps down. "People are afraid of the dark. I give them a small piece of it to carry home."

"Your mother kept a lamp by the door," the boy's voice said, and the old man's voice added, "She waited up for you."

Eden did not answer. He watched the hook on the pole and counted the lamps again, because a thing with twelve shadows could be trusted to keep its own shape.

The lamplighter came forward, and the twelve shadows on the wall leaned toward Eden together. Eden needed an edge, and there were twelve of them, so he put his back to the rack and let the nearest shadow fall across his own boot. He knocked once on the wall, inside the nearest shadow. A Door opened there, black, with a thin edge of light. The lamplighter swung his pole at Eden, and Eden stepped aside, so the pole went into the Door up to the hook. The man pulled and could not get it back.

He did not look surprised, and that was the worst part. He let go of the pole and reached for Eden's face with fingers that were too long. The hook came round on the swing and caught Eden's shoulder, and the ache went into his elbow. One lamp was still moving above them, and Eden waited for it to stop before he moved again.

Eden drew **[Exa]** from his shadow. He knocked once on its iron ring, and the lance folded into a small black shield on his forearm. The fingers closed on its edge and slid. They were colder than the stair and wetter than the rain, and where they touched the iron a thin frost grew and cracked. Eden stepped back with the shield between them. The Veil leaned after him, and Eden waited, because a thing that leans has already chosen where its weight goes. When the weight was over its front foot, he pushed the nearest lamp so that it swung on its chain. Twelve shadows slid across the wall at once, and the Veil, which had fixed its eyes on the nearest one, lost him for one breath.

"Say his middle name," Eden said.

The three voices stopped. Then the boy's voice said a name, and the old man's voice said a different one, and the woman's voice said nothing at all.

"Again," Eden said.

The grey showed at the edge of the stolen face, under the skin, where the lamp light could not lie about it. Eden drew **[Inead]** in one motion, and the gold made the room honest: the lamplighter's coat fell on a grey body with too many joints, folded the way a dead insect folds. One cut from the shoulder ended it. The voices stopped one by one, and the last voice said something about the bottom of the world before it was gone.

There was no blood on the floor, only dust, and the stolen face lay flat with its eyes open. The coat under it had gone ordinary grey, with nothing left of the man who had worn it. Eden wiped the rack where it had touched, brushed the dust into a cloth, and set the pole back against the rack where it had been. Then he put the dust cloth in his coat. At the top of the stair the tower bell rang three slow tones. Third bell. The lamps in the house went out on their own, one after another, and the stair went grey.

"Clean?" Vesk asked from the dark.

"Clean."

"You smell of oil," Vesk said.

"I will wash."

"Do not go to breakfast like that." Vesk stepped back into the shadow. "You are faster when it is nearly morning. I do not know why."

"Neither do I."

Vesk stayed a moment longer. His yellow eyes went up the stair to where the lamps had gone out one by one, and he said, quietly, "It spoke about your mother."

"They all do."

"Not all of them know the lamp by the door." Vesk did not say anything else. He had a way of putting a thing on the ground between them and leaving it there, and Eden stepped round it as he always did, carefully, as if it might wake.

* * *

At breakfast a student found the body and screamed. That was how the Discipline Committee heard about it before the kitchen had finished the porridge. By the time Eden came down from the roof, Lysette was already at the lamp-house door with her red band pinned and her book open, and Okoru stood behind her with his stick under his arm. The morning light came down the stair in a pale strip and stopped at the door, so the lamp-house was still half dark.

She did not see Eden at first, or chose not to. He stood against the wall by the stair with his hands in his pockets and watched her work.

"Four steps from the rack to the door," Lysette said. "Wet floor, so the boot marks are clear. Nobody came in after the lamps went out."

She asked the prefect to write his own name and the hour he had come on duty. He wrote slowly, and she read it back to him, and he corrected the hour. "Better," she said, and put the paper in her book.

"What did he say to you last night?" she asked the student.

"He said to come up for the light."

"Those were his words?"

"Yes. I did not go."

"Good. You did the right thing." Lysette wrote it down. The student looked at his shoes, and she said nothing more to him, which was kind in her own way.

A prefect held the door. The dead man on the floor was the real lamplighter, an ordinary old man in a lamplighter's coat. The students outside did not know a Veil had worn him. They only knew he was dead.

Lysette knelt beside the body and put one hand flat on the stone. This was her ability, **[Witness]**: it showed her a faint copy of what had happened in a place. Ghost-light formed in the air over her, thin in the morning sun. It showed a figure in black, standing where the rack was now, with no face. Behind the figure was a door-shaped patch of darkness, in a place where no door stood. The figure raised an arm. A blade of light came down. Then the picture was gone.

Eden watched her face while she worked. The figure in the ghost-light was himself, in his black armour, as the lamp-house had seen him a few hours earlier. She could not see his face in it, because the helm had none. He had known that, and he still felt the back of his neck go cold. Her pencil did not stop. She asked the prefect for the exact time of the third bell and crossed it out when he said "around" instead of "at". Okoru stood behind her and said nothing about the time.

"Five minutes," he said at last. "Then let them go."

"I need one more," Lysette said. She stood and brushed the dust off her knees. "Sir, I want to show you something. My second ability is **[Testimony]**. It holds the picture in the air so other people can see what I saw. It only works in a still room, and I cannot hold it for long."

She set her feet and raised her hand. The ghost-light came again, brighter, and everyone in the lamp-house could see the faceless figure in black and the door-shaped dark behind it. The students went quiet. The figure turned once, as if it had heard someone speak, and the dark door turned with it. Lysette's wrist began to shake. After six breaths she let the light go. Then she wrote one word in her book, "door", and drew a line under it.

Okoru wrote one line of his own. "That is enough. Do not use it again today."

"Is it the Thirteenth Vigil?" the prefect asked.

"It is a person in black who makes doors where there are none," Lysette said. "That is not the same as a name." She shut her book and looked at the wall behind her.

On the stone above the place where she had been kneeling was a pale shape, clean, as tall as a man. The soot of the lamp-house covered the rest of the wall. Only this shape had been wiped clean. Its edges were straight, like the mark left when a big picture frame is taken down.

Eden looked at it too. "What is that?"

"Nothing," Lysette said, and she turned her back to it too quickly. She did not look at it again.

She walked past him to the door. Okoru gave a small grunt that might have been approval. "You write faster than the sergeant. He has been here an hour and has written four words."

"Which four?"

"Dead man. Lamp-house. Sad."

"He is not wrong," Lysette said, and for the first time that morning her mouth nearly moved.

Okoru stopped beside Eden on the way out. "You came down from the roof early this morning," he said, so quietly that Lysette could not hear. "The roof garden is a long way from the Long Stair."

"I heard the scream."

"From the roof?"

"Yes, sir."

Okoru looked at him for the length of one slow breath. He did not say that he believed it, and he did not say that he did not. He only tapped his stick once on the floor, the way he did when he was filing something away, and walked on.

Okoru led the students out into the stair, one hand on the shoulder of the one who was still crying. Eden stayed at the door and counted them as they went: eleven students and two prefects. Every one of them looked at their shoes. Not one of them looked up at the lamps.

Lysette came out last and stood beside him without looking at him, the way she had at the stables. She put her pencil in her book and shut it. She did not ask why he was standing at a death scene. Below them the students were being sent back to breakfast, and one of them was still crying.

"Did you see anyone on the stair?" she asked.

"No."

"Then you have nothing to tell me."

"No."

She put one hand on the stair rail and held it there for a breath, then took her book under her arm. "The Thirteenth Vigil was here," she said. "I will find out who opens the doors."
