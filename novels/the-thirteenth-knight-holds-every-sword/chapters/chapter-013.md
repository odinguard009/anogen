# Chapter 13: The Empty Frame

The lamp-house on the Long Stair had twelve oil lamps hanging from a rack, and each one threw a small hard shadow on the white wall. The lamplighter stood under them with a pole in his hand and his back to the door, and he had been dead for some time before Eden came in.

Vesk had smelled him from the stair. "Veil," he said, outside. "It is wearing a man who carried lamps. I cannot come in, you know that."

"How many calls?" Eden asked.

"Four this week. All from houses on the Long Stair." Vesk's yellow eyes moved over the dark behind him. "It likes stairs. People look up when they walk them, and a lamp looks like a friend."

Eden went in alone. The floor was wet with rain brought in from the stair, and the lamp oil smelled of nuts and smoke. The lamplighter turned with a face that was too calm for a man who worked nights, and his smile arrived one beat after his eyes.

"Late tonight," the man said, and his voice came out in three voices at once, an old man, a boy and a woman, all saying the same words together.

Eden kept his hands where the man could see them. "You are the one calling the houses."

"I carry the light." The lamplighter lifted the pole, and the end of it had a hook for pulling lamps down. "People are afraid of the dark. I give them a small piece of it to carry home."

"Your mother kept a lamp by the door," the boy's voice said, and the old man's voice added, "She waited up for you."

Eden did not answer. He watched the hook on the pole and counted the lamps again, because a thing with twelve shadows could be trusted to keep its own shape.

The lamplighter came forward, and the twelve shadows on the wall leaned toward Eden together. Eden needed an edge, and there were twelve of them, so he put his back to the rack and let the nearest shadow fall across his own boot. One knock, and the Door opened black behind the lamplighter. The pole went into it up to the hook, and the man pulled and could not get it back.

He did not look surprised. He let go of the pole and reached for Eden's face with fingers that were too long. The hook came round on the swing and caught Eden's shoulder, and the ache went into his elbow. One lamp was still moving above them, and Eden waited for it to stop before he moved again.

Eden drew **[Exa]** from his shadow and caught the fingers on the iron ring, and the cold went up his arm when they closed on nothing but air.

The lance shortened under his hand as the ring knocked, and the black shield opened on his forearm, small and round and quiet. The fingers closed on its edge and slid. They were colder than the stair and wetter than the rain, and where they touched the iron a thin frost grew and cracked. Eden stepped back with the shield between them and let the Veil lean after him, because a thing that leans has already decided where its weight goes.

"Say his middle name," Eden said.

The three voices stopped. Then the boy's voice said a name, and the old man's voice said a different one, and the woman's voice said nothing at all.

"Again," Eden said.

The grey showed at the edge of the stolen face, under the skin, where the lamp light could not lie about it. Eden drew **[Inead]** in one motion, and the gold made the room honest: the lamplighter's coat fell on a grey body with too many joints, folded the way a dead insect folds. One cut from the shoulder ended it. The voices stopped one by one, and the last voice said something about the bottom of the world before it was gone.

There was no blood on the floor, only dust, and the stolen face lay flat with its eyes open. The coat under it had gone ordinary grey, with nothing left of the man who had worn it. Eden wiped the board where it had touched, brushed the dust into a cloth, and set the pole back against the rack where it had been. Then he took a different pole from the wall and carried that one with him, because a lamplighter who walked away without his pole would be noticed. At the top of the stair the tower bell rang three slow tones. Third bell. The lamps in the house went out on their own, one after another, and the stair went grey.

"Clean?" Vesk asked from the dark.

"Clean."

"You smell of oil," Vesk said.

"I will wash."

"Do not go to breakfast like that." Vesk stepped back into the shadow. "You are faster when it is nearly morning. I do not know why."

"Neither do I."

Vesk stayed a moment longer. His yellow eyes went up the stair to where the lamps had gone out one by one, and he said, quietly, "It spoke about your mother."

"They all do."

"Not all of them know the lamp by the door." Vesk did not say anything else. He had a way of putting a thing on the ground between them and leaving it there, and Eden stepped round it as he always did, carefully, as if it might wake.

* * *

A student found the body at breakfast and screamed in the Long Stair, which was how the Committee heard about it before the kitchen had finished the porridge. By the time Eden came down from the roof, Lysette was already at the lamp-house door with her red band pinned and her book open, and Okoru stood behind her with his stick under his arm. The morning light came down the stair in a pale strip and stopped at the door, so the lamp-house was still half dark.

She did not see Eden at first, or chose not to. He stood against the wall by the stair with his hands in his pockets and watched her work.

"Four steps from the rack to the door," Lysette said. "Wet floor, so the boot marks are clear. Nobody came in after the lamps went out."

She asked the prefect to write his own name and the hour he had come on duty. He wrote slowly, and she read it back to him, and he corrected the hour. "Better," she said, and put the paper in her book.

"What did he say to you last night?" she asked the student.

"He said to come up for the light."

"Those were his words?"

"Yes, ma'am. I did not go."

"Good." Lysette wrote it down. "You did the right thing." The student looked at his shoes, and she said nothing more to him, which was the kindest thing she had done all morning.

A prefect held the door. Two students stood in the stair with their faces turned away, because the body on the floor was the stolen man and not a grey thing, and they had not been told what that meant.

Lysette knelt beside the body and put one hand flat on the stone. **[Witness]** came as ghost-light in the air over her, faint in the morning, and this time it showed more than a shape: a figure in black standing where the rack had been, and a door-shaped patch of darkness behind it where no door stood now. The figure had no face. Its arm went up, and the ghost-light of a blade came down, and then the shape was gone.

Eden watched her face while she worked. Her pencil did not stop. She asked the prefect for the exact time of the third bell, wrote it down, and crossed it out when the prefect said "around" instead of "at". She asked one of the students what the lamplighter had said the night before, and wrote that down too, and did not comment when the student began to cry. Okoru stood behind her with his stick under his arm and said nothing about the time.

"Three minutes," he said at last. "Then let them go."

"Four," Lysette said. "I have one more question."

"Are you finished?" Okoru asked.

"Almost." She stood and brushed the dust off her knees. "I want to show you something. **[Testimony]** holds the replay in the air so other people can see what I saw. It only works where the place is still and nobody is moving through it. I cannot hold it for long."

She set her feet and held up her hand. The ghost-light came again, brighter this time, and the figure in black stood over the body for everyone in the lamp-house to see, and the door-shaped shadow stood behind it. The students went quiet. One of them made a small sound and was told to be still by the prefect. The figure in the light turned once, as if it had heard someone speak, and the door-shaped shadow turned with it. Lysette's hand began to shake at the wrist, and she let the light go after six breaths. She wrote the exact words the student had said, then drew a line under the word "door".

Okoru wrote one line. "That is enough."

"Is it the Thirteenth Vigil?" the prefect asked.

"It is a person in black who opens doors that are not there," Lysette said. "That is not the same as a name." She shut her book and looked at the wall above the place where she had been kneeling. There was a pale shape on the stone the size of a tall person, empty and clean, where the soot of the lamp-house had been rubbed away. It was taller than the rack and narrower than a man, with edges as straight as a frame that had been taken down.

Eden looked at it too. "What is that?"

"A mark." Lysette turned her back to it. "None of your business."

"Was it there before?"

"No." She touched the wall beside it and looked at her fingertips, then wiped them on her coat. "The stone is clean. The soot around it is old. Somebody took something off this wall and took it recently." She walked past him toward the door, and stopped beside Okoru with her book closed. "But it is on my desk at school as well, and I have been trying to work out what took it off the wall."

"Did anyone else see that mark?" Okoru asked.

"No," Lysette said.

"Write it down."

"I already did." Okoru gave a small grunt that might have been approval. "You write faster than the sergeant," he said. "He has been here an hour and written four words."

"Which four?"

"Dead man. Lamp-house. Sad."

"He is not wrong," Lysette said, and for the first time that morning her mouth nearly moved.

He looked at the pale shape for a moment longer than the prefect had, then led the students out into the stair.

Eden stayed at the door with his hand on the pole he had brought back. He counted the students going down the stair: eleven, and two prefects. Every one of them looked at their shoes. Not one of them looked up at the lamps.

Lysette came out last and stood beside him without looking at him, the way she had at the stables. She put the pencil in her book and shut it, and she did not ask why he was standing at the scene of a Vigil kill. Below them the students were being sent back to breakfast, and one of them was still crying.

"Did you see anyone on the stair?" she asked.

"No."

"Then you saw nothing."

"Yes."

She put one hand on the stair rail and held it there for a breath, then took her book under her arm. "The Thirteenth Vigil was here," she said. "I will find out who opens the doors."
