# Chapter 13: The Lamp-House

Vesk came out of the shadow under the stair and stood in the lamplight with his hands in his coat pockets. "Long Stair," he said. "The lamp man is late, and the third bell is in twenty minutes."

Eden closed his book and put it in his coat. "Is it a Veil?"

"Nobody knows. The man has worked the stair for eleven years." Vesk's eyes were lamp-yellow in the dark, and his coat had holes at both elbows. "You go, and you do not take the funicular. It stops at the middle, and you cannot open a door in moving air."

"How many?" Eden asked.

"One. It has worn the lamp man's coat for a week."

"Did it kill him?"

"No one knows, and no one is asking." Vesk stepped back into the shadow. "The bell is yours."

Umra came out of Eden's shadow without a sound. The black plate closed over his chest and arms, and the white-thread slit in the helm made a thin bright line across his face. The cellar air went dark around him, and every edge in the stairwell grew sharp: the iron rail, the step, the line where the lamp light stopped on the wall. He put one glove on the rail and felt the cold through the leather.

The Long Stair climbed the outside of the plateau in four turns, and the lamps stood on iron poles at every landing. The wind came up the rock in cold pushes, and the city lay below in a grid of yellow windows. Eden kept to the wall side where the shadow was deepest, and he counted the poles as he went: eight, nine, ten, and the eleventh lamp was out.

The lamp-house stood at the top of the last turn, a small stone room with a glass roof and a door that had no lock. Inside, rows of spare lamps hung on brass hooks, and the smell of oil was so strong that it made the back of his throat burn. A thick glass chimney lay broken on the floor, and oil had soaked into the stone in a dark patch.

A man stood beside the rack with his back to the door. He wore the grey coat of the stair workers, and his cap was pulled low, and his hands were red with oil. He turned when Eden stepped in.

"Late," the man said, and his smile came one beat after the word, and stayed after he stopped smiling.

Eden stopped at the door. The man's shadow on the wall was flat and still, and it did not breathe when his chest moved. Veils always kept one old habit when they took a person, and this one had kept the smile.

"Your lamp is out," Eden said.

"Wind."

"Wind does not break glass from the inside."

"You are early for a check," the man said, and his voice came from two places at once, one high and one low, as if two people had spoken together.

"I check the lamps," Eden said.

"Not this one." The smile held. "This one is mine."

The man's smile came again, late and smaller. He took a long brass pole from the rack, and his hand went around it with the slow care of someone who had done it for eleven years. Eden drew **[Inead]**. The gold sword made one clear note in the room, and the light from it laid the truth over the man's face: the skin went thin as paper, the eyes became two dark seams, and the grey body showed under the coat like grey dust under a sheet.

The bronze pole came down. Eden put Umra's shoulder into it and turned the blow on the flat of the blade, and the pole bent at the middle and struck the glass rack. Lamps swung and rang, and one chimney fell and broke. The Veil came on with its hands open, and its smile had vanished, and its shadow lagged half a step behind it on the wall.

Eden tapped twice on the shadow edge where the wall met the floor. A black opening opened in the dark under the rack, and the Veil's next step took it into the dark. The body was held there, and the grey did not come out. Eden put Inead through the opening in one cut, and the light flashed on the brass hooks, and the thing inside came apart into grey dust that fell in a soft line onto the stone.

The opening closed. The lamp-house was quiet except for the wind at the glass roof, and the oil on the floor kept the shape of a boot. Eden put Inead away, and the dark air went back to normal around him, and Umra folded into his shadow without a sound. He took the broken chimney from the floor and set it upright on the rack, and then he walked down the stair before the third bell could finish ringing.

* * *

The report came at breakfast the next morning. A first-year from the lower school stood in the Committee room with his cap in his hands and his ears red, and he told Lysette Hollen that the lamp man had not come down all night, and that the spare lamp on the landing was still dark.

"The lamp-house door was open," the boy said. "There was oil on the floor, and a broken glass, and no man."

"Did you touch anything?" Lysette asked.

"No, miss. I came straight here."

"Good." She wrote the answer in her book and gave the boy a slip. "Go to class, and tell the porter what you told me. Do not tell anyone else."

"Am I in trouble, miss?"

"No. You did well." Lysette wrote another line. "What time did you come up?"

"After first light, miss. The lamp was dark from the turn."

"That is all." She tore the slip from her book. "Go."

Eden stood against the Committee wall with his hands behind his back, and Okoru stood beside the door with his one arm folded. Lysette put on her coat and took her batons from the flat case on her belt. She moved quickly and did not ask anyone to help, and Eden went with her because Okoru told him to.

The lamp-house looked smaller in daylight. The glass roof let in a flat white sky, and the broken chimney had been set upright on the rack, and the oil patch on the stone had dried to a dark ring. Lysette stood in the middle of the room and turned in a slow circle, and she did not speak for a while.

"You were here last night," she said.

"I was on the stair," Eden said. "I saw the lamp was out."

"Did you see a man?"

"No."

She wrote that down. "The boy said there was no man. You say you saw no man, and the lamp was out." She looked at the rack, and at the broken chimney, and at the floor. "Nobody has said what happened here."

"It is a puzzle," Eden said.

"It is a room with an oil patch and a broken lamp and a man who worked eleven years. That is not a puzzle. "That is a death, and somebody stood here when it happened."

"Do you always talk to a room?" Eden asked.

"I talk to the person in it." She looked at him. "You were here last night, and you know what I am looking for."

"I told you I saw a lamp out."

"You told me one thing twice." She wrote it down. "That is a habit worth writing."

Lysette put her book under her arm and stepped to the middle of the stone floor. "**[Witness]**."

The air behind her went pale, and a replay opened in the room like thin rain lit by moonlight. It showed the lamp-house as it had been the night before: a man at the rack with a brass pole in his hand, and the pole bending, and then a figure in black that came from the wall. The figure stood very still, and the replay showed where its hands had been. Behind it, the shadow on the wall opened into a black upright shape, like a door cut out of the dark.

Lysette did not move while it ran. Her face went still, and one hand held her book tight against her coat, and the ghost-light shook at the edges when the wind moved under the roof. Then the picture thinned and was gone, and she put her hand on the wall to steady herself.

"A black figure," she said. "And a door that was not a door." She turned to Okoru. "I can show you, if the room is still."

Okoru came into the middle of the floor and put his stick across his shoulders. "Show me once."

Lysette lifted both hands, and the replay came back brighter than before. This time the whole room could see it: the bent pole, the grey shape under the coat, the black figure with its arm up, and the door-shaped opening behind it. The light held for six breaths, and her wrist shook, and then she let it go. The room was only a room again, with oil on the floor and a hard white sky above.

Okoru looked at the place where the door had been. "That is a door shape," he said. "It is not a shadow shape."

"No," Lysette said. "A shadow does not open and shut."

"It does if somebody uses it." Okoru looked at Eden, and then away. "Good work, Hollen. Write it up, and keep the shape in your report."

"Is it a Thirteenth door, sir?" Lysette asked.

"Write what you saw." Okoru turned his stick over. "Names are for people who have proof."

"I have a shape."

"Then keep the shape."

"Yes, sir."

They left the lamp-house together, and Eden walked behind her down the Long Stair, and he kept his hands out of his pockets because the wind had picked up. At the fourth landing Lysette stopped and looked at the city below, and she did not seem to be looking at anything in particular.

"You said you saw no man," she said.

"I saw a lamp out," Eden said.

"You saw more than that."

"I saw enough to know you would find the rest."

"You are very sure of me."

"I know what you do when a thing does not fit," Eden said. "You look at it again."

"That is a habit, not a gift."

"It is a good habit."

She turned on the step above him and looked at him for a while. Her eyes were deep blue-violet, and the shadowed lower lids made her look tired even when she was not. "One day you will tell me what you saw," she said. "I do not know when, but you will."

"Yes," Eden said, and he went down the last flight ahead of her.

* * *

The Committee room was empty when Lysette came back from the stair, and Eden went in behind her with a message from Okoru. Her desk sat under the narrow window, and a pile of round sheets lay on one corner with her black book on top of them.

Above the desk, the wall was a different colour.

Eden stopped in the doorway. The paint around the space was pale grey from years of smoke, and inside the space it was a lighter cream, clean and square, with a thin line of grey dust at the edge where a frame had once hung against the wall. Nothing hung there now. The pale shape had no nails in it and no marks, and it was the size of a tall picture, and its edges were clean.

Lysette followed his eyes and put her book down. "The room came that way," she said. "There was a frame when I was little, and my mother had it taken down."

"What was in it?"

"I do not know." She sat at the desk and straightened the round sheets. "Nobody told me, and I did not ask."

"Does it bother you?" Eden asked.

"The empty space?" She put the pen down. "It was my grandmother's, and the frame went away the year my brother died. Nobody said why, and I stopped looking at it."

"Do you want it back?"

"No," Lysette said. "I want to know what was in it."

Eden looked at the pale square again. It was the exact shape of the opening in the lamp-house replay, tall and narrow with a light edge at the top, and the wall around it kept the faint outline of a frame that no longer existed.

"The Thirteenth Vigil was here," Lysette said, and her pen moved across the page. "I will find out who opens the doors."
