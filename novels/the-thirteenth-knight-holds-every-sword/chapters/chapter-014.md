# Chapter 14: The Nap Record

Eden kept the nap record in the back of his notebook, and on Saturday morning it had six clean lines. Each line held a day, a time, and a place, and the last column was for interruptions. Six lines had nothing in the last column. He wrote the seventh date at the top and put the notebook under his pillow, and then he lay down on the roof bench with his coat over his face.

Footsteps came up the stair, and he knew them before they reached the top. "Wren!" Rowan's voice arrived a step ahead of Rowan. "Lysette is looking for you, and she has a runner!"

Eden did not move the coat. "I am sleeping."

"You are writing."

"I am writing in my sleep."

Rowan stood at the end of the bench and looked down at the city. "She has three runners now. Felix is one of them, which is strange, because he usually warns you."

"That is the double agent."

"What?"

"It means he works for both sides," Eden said. "He tells her where I am, and he tells me when she is coming."

Rowan thought about that for a while. "Then why take the lunch?"

"Because she brings two."

"Ah." Rowan sat on the wall and put his hands between his knees. "I would take the lunch as well."

The stair door opened, and Lysette came out with a clipboard and her book under one arm. She wore her dark coat with the white collar, and her braid sat flat across the top of her head. Behind her, a first-year boy peered around the door and then went back down the stair.

"Wren," Lysette said. "You are not in class."

"It is Saturday."

"Saturday morning class exists, and I have a sheet for it." She held out a white slip. "This is a warrant, and it is your second this term."

Eden lifted one corner of the coat. "I know you are the vice-chair, and I know you have a sheet. You also have a garden."

"Do not change the subject."

"It is the same subject."

Lysette looked at the bench, and at the low wall, and at the gravel with one stone out of place. "Where did you sleep last night?"

"Here."

"Behind the tank as well?"

"Yes."

She wrote a line and drew a box around it. Then she looked at Rowan, who was trying to look like a wall. "Did you see him here at midnight?"

Rowan opened his mouth, and Eden did not look at him. "I saw him at midnight," Rowan said. "He was asleep, and he had a book on his face."

"A book is not a pillow."

"It is if you are him."

Lysette shut the book and walked to the stair. At the door she turned. "The door of the roof garden is locked at ten. If you are on the roof after ten, you are breaking a rule to sleep."

"I will sleep somewhere else," Eden said.

"You do not have somewhere else."

She went down the stair, and Rowan waited until her steps were gone. "She knows," he said.

"She has known for weeks."

"Then why ask?"

"Because she wants me to say it first."

Rowan leaned back and looked at the grey sky. "That is what my sister does," he said. "She asks a question when she already knows the answer, and then she is angry at the answer you give."

"Does it work?"

"On me, yes."

* * *

The raid grew during the week, and by Thursday it had a name. The runners called it the Eden File, and they carried white slips in their coat pockets like badges. Lysette put a notice on the board outside the hall. It had a list of seven dates, a column of class names, and a blank column headed "explanation," and she wrote the word "ROOF" in the margin in her own neat hand. Okoru stopped in front of it with his stick under his arm and read it twice.

"You have written roof six times," he said.

"Seven," Lysette said. "He was in the library on Thursday."

"Then where was he?"

"Behind the infirmary, from the sound of the door."

Okoru took the notice down and folded it. "Put it in the staff room instead. The first-years think it is a punishment list and are taking photographs of it."

"Why?"

"Because you are the vice-chair and you look serious." He walked off, and Lysette followed with the folded notice in her hand. A second-year stopped Eden in the corridor and asked him to sign a sheet that said he had been present at a class he had not attended. Eden signed it in the wrong place, and the boy ran off with the paper, and Lysette tore it up an hour later.

On Tuesday Lysette caught Felix coming out of the library with two trays baspeard on one arm. "Felix Ashgrove," she said. "Why are you carrying lunch for two?"

"Committee business," Felix said.

"You are not on the Committee."

"I am helping a member of the Committee."

"Who?"

"The thin one," Felix said, and he looked at the ceiling while he said it.

Lysette took the top tray from his arm and set it on the wall. "This one is bread and cheese, and the thin one does not eat cheese."

"That is why I am taking it away."

She looked at him for a while, and then she gave the tray back. "Do not tell him I said this, but you are a better liar than he is."

"He knows," Felix said. "I tell him everything you say."

Felix brought the news at lunch. He sat with Eden behind the stables and took a bun from the paper bag. "I have good news and bad news," he said.

"Give me the bad one."

"Lysette has a roof stakeout on Friday. Two older students and a lantern."

"And the good?"

Felix took a bite and chewed. "I told her you would be in the library."

"Where am I?"

"Behind the stables, eating your lunch." He offered the bag. "Bun?"

Eden took one. "You are a poor double agent."

"I am a very good one." Felix looked at the wall. "She knows I lie to her, and she knows I tell you when she is coming. She still brings the lunch, so everybody is honest about the part that matters."

On Friday the two older students sat on the roof with a lantern and a round sheet, and they wrote nothing for two hours because Eden was in the library. He was not in the library. He was in the old store room next to the infirmary with a chair tilted back and his coat over his face, and he slept for forty minutes without an interruption.

Seraphine found him there just before the bell. "Wren!" she said, and she stood in the doorway with her hands on her hips. "You are not where Felix said you were."

"I know."

"Felix is terrible at lying."

"He is lying to both of us, and he is good at it." Eden sat up and put the chair legs down. "What do you want?"

"Nothing. I came to tell Felix he is at nineteen."

"Nineteen what?"

"Jokes." She pulled a folded paper from her pocket and held it out. It had a column of small marks, and some marks had a date beside them. "I keep a record as well."

"Why?"

"Because he never laughs at his own jokes, and I want to know when he does." She folded the paper and put it away. "He is at nineteen, and I am running out of paper."

"Give me an example," Eden said.

"You are not helping." Seraphine held up one finger. "On Monday he said my tea was a weapon, and he said it with a straight face. That was seventeen."

"Why was that a joke?"

"Because she heard him," Felix said from the doorway. He had been standing there with a bun in his hand. "And she laughed before she told me it was not funny."

"Get a bigger sheet."

"That is a joke." Seraphine pointed at him. "Write it down."

"I am not writing it down."

"Then it does not count." She walked down the corridor, and her red coat went around the corner before the bell.

At lunch Lysette brought two parcels and set one beside Eden's hand, and she did not look at him while she did it. Felix took the other parcel and opened it before she had finished speaking.

"Two lunches," she said. "I walked twice, and I am not doing it again."

"It is the seventh day," Eden said.

"Do not make me write a number."

Felix ate his soup with his head down. "It is a good number."

"It is a bad number," Lysette said, and she walked away with her book under her arm.

Eden opened his parcel. Soup, dark bread, and an apple, the same as every day for a week. He ate the bread first, because the soup was still too hot, and Felix did not say anything about it.

* * *

On Saturday evening, the roof garden was quiet except for the water in the tank. Eden came up through the door at dusk and walked to the far end where the herb bed sat under a low frame of wood. The thyme had new green tips, and the mint had been cut back, and someone had tied a string between two posts to keep the birds off. A watering can stood under the wall with a dent in its spout, and a pair of grey gloves hung from a nail on the post. The gloves were small and had mud on the fingers, and Eden had moved them aside with his own boot so he would not crush them when he lay down. The soil under the thyme was damp, and the mint had been watered the day before.

He lay down in the herb bed because the soil was warm from the day and the smell of thyme was strong. He put his coat over his face and folded his hands on his chest, and the record in his notebook would have to wait until Monday.

Lysette came up the stair a little before dark. She stopped at the far end and stood very still, and she did not wake him.

Eden watched her through the thin cloth over his face. She looked at the herb bed, and at the string between the posts, and at the green tips on the thyme. Then she looked at him lying in the middle of it, with his boots on the wooden frame and his hands folded neatly on his coat.

She stood there without moving. Then she reached down and pulled two dead stems from the mint and put them in her pocket, and she turned and went back down the stair without saying a word.

Eden lay still until her steps were gone. Then he sat up and brushed the soil from his sleeves, and he looked at the place where she had pulled the stems. The herb bed was hers. He had known that since the first week, and he had never told anyone, and she had just found out that he knew.

Above the wall, the roof door was shut, and the gravel had been swept in a straight line by the wind. The city below was a grid of lamps, and the tram bell rang once at the corner and went away.

He took the notebook from his coat and opened it to the nap record. Under Saturday he wrote a time and a place and one word in the last column: "Caught." Then he looked at the word for a while and crossed it out. He had kept six clean lines, and the seventh line had a word in the last column that he had taken back. He did not know what to write instead, so he left the space empty.

"Not caught," he said, and he put the notebook back in his coat. "She walked away."
