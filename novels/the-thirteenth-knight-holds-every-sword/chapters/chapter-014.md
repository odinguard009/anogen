# Chapter 14: The Herb Bed

Eden kept the nap record on the back of an old timetable, with one line for each day and a mark for each hour. The paper had a brown spot in one corner from Felix's tea, and the marks had been made with a pencil that kept breaking, so the hours were not all the same size. The week had started badly because he had fallen asleep in Spell Grammar on Monday and woken with a page of notes under his cheek. By Saturday he had slept in the roof garden four times, behind the stables twice, and once in a chair in the library that he did not count because the chair was not a bed.

Felix found the timetable on the roof bench and read it upside down. "Seven lines," he said. "One of them says 'chair, do not count'."

"Monday counted twice," Eden said. "I fell asleep in class and again here."

"That is not a record. That is a confession." Felix sat on the bench and put the timetable back where it had been. "If she finds this, she will write you up for seven things instead of one."

"She will find it."

"Then why keep it?"

"To know if the week is perfect." Eden looked at the bird nest in the telescope. The bird had added a piece of red string, which did not belong to any nest Eden had seen before. "The chair was not a nap."

"It is a sleep with a chair." Felix sat down and put a paper bag on the bench. "Two lunches today. One from the kitchen, one from Lysette. I am a double agent and I am being paid in rolls."

"Who pays you?"

"Both of you. That is the problem with being an agent." He took a peppermint out of his pocket. "She asked me where you were at the second bell."

"What did you tell her?"

"That you were in the library." Felix put the peppermint back. "You were not. You were behind the telescope. I did not say which telescope."

A runner came up the stair before the prefect could, a first-year with a note folded into three and a face full of importance. "Committee," he said. "North door. Now."

Eden read the note. It said only WREN in Lysette's hand, with a bell number underneath and a line drawn under the number.

"She has hired runners," Felix said. "That first-year has been paid in rolls as well. I am losing my monopoly."

A prefect's voice came up the stair well, three floors below. "Wren! The Committee wants you at the north door."

Felix stood up without hurrying. "That is the second runner. She is building an army." He picked up the paper bag. "I will tell her you went to the infirmary. Eat something before she gets here."

"Is that true?"

"No. But it is a better place to be than the north door."

* * *

The north door of the dining hall had a bench beside it and a notice board above it, and Lysette stood under the notice board with a clipboard and three prefects. Two of them carried written warrants. The third carried a ladder, because the roof garden had a locked door and someone had told her about the key.

"Wren," she said, without looking up. "Hold out your hands."

Eden held them out. She looked at them, then at his sleeve, and wrote something on the clipboard.

"Hands are clean," she said. "That means you have not been in the herb bed today."

"I was in the library."

"You were in the library at the second bell." She turned the clipboard so he could see the lines. "I have a runner who saw a boy behind the telescope. The runner cannot say which boy, because the runner was looking at his boots. But the boots were grey."

"Many boots are grey."

"Yes." She wrote again. "That is why I have written 'grey boots, probably Wren' and not 'Wren'."

A prefect shifted the ladder against the wall and looked at it, embarrassed to be holding it in front of a Year 5 student. Lysette did not look at him at all.

"The warrant for Friday says 'absence from strategy, roof'," she said. "The office returned it because the word roof is not a reason. I have written a new one. It says 'absence from class, medical, place: various'."

"Various is not a place."

"It is three places written as one." She took a fresh sheet from under the clipboard. "Sign here."

"Where does it go?"

"The office. The runner takes it. I do not trust the runners with the folded ones, so I keep those." She put the top copy in her book. "Sign, then eat something. Those are two different boxes and neither of them says sleep."

Eden signed. Felix, standing behind him with a roll in his hand, made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

Seraphine came down the north stairs with a card in her hand and stopped beside them. "What number are we on?" she asked Felix.

"Eighteen," he said.

"You are wrong. It is seventeen." She showed him the card. "I have crossed out the two you did not laugh at."

"That is not how a count works."

"It is my count." She wrote on the card and turned to Eden. "Eighteen, then. I will save nineteen for something good."

"Please do," Eden said.

Halfway into the hall Felix dropped his spoon. It landed on a plate with a sound that made three people turn, and Seraphine wrote on her card without slowing down.

"Nineteen," she said.

"I did not do that on purpose."

"That is the best kind." She put the card in her glove. "I will tell you the joke at the end of term."

Felix went after her with his roll, and Lysette watched them go with her pencil held still. Then she looked at the ladder again and sent the prefect with it back to the store.

"You cannot reach the roof that way," she said to Eden, when the prefect was gone. "The door is locked and I have the only key."

"The garden door has a loose hinge."

"I know."

She said it so flatly that he looked at her. She had put her book under her arm and her gloves in her pocket, and the red band on her sleeve was the same one she had worn every day that week.

"I have known for a month," she said. "The hinge is on the east side. It drops a finger's width." She walked toward the stairs. "Do not tell anyone I told you."

"I will not."

"You already knew." She stopped on the first step. "That is what I mean."

* * *

By the afternoon the raid had become a campaign. Warrants lay in three piles on the Committee bench, with runners coming and going between the hall and the east stairs. One of them reported a boy in grey boots on the roof. Another reported a boy in grey boots in the library. A third reported no boy at all, which Lysette wrote down as 'no boy, useful'.

One warrant was written for the roof, one for the library, one for the stables and one for "various, see attached". The attached sheet had three places on it and a line through two of them. Lysette made four copies of the whole set, because the office liked copies and returned anything with fewer than three. By the last bell the Committee bench looked like a small wall of paper, and the runners had started charging each other for the news.

Eden went up to the roof garden just before the last bell and found the herb bed damp from the morning's rain. The mint had grown over the edge of the stone and the sage had gone woody at the stem, but the thyme was thick and green. A trowel lay where she had left it, point down along the stone.

He lay in the herb bed because the grass outside it was wet and the bed was warm from the day. The smell of thyme came up into his coat, and a small bee worked the mint flowers a hand's width from his ear. The stone under him had been dug and turned and dug again, and the soil was fine where her hands had been. He meant to close his eyes for one minute, and he wrote the minute on the back of the timetable before he put it away.

When he woke, the light had gone gold and Lysette was sitting on the stone edge of the bed two feet from his head. She had her book open on her knee and her gloves in her hand, and she had not woken him.

Eden did not move at first. Her pencil was still, and she was looking at the dwarf apple tree, not at him. The white flower on the highest twig had gone. In its place there was a small green apple, hard and out of season.

"How long?" he asked.

"Eleven minutes." She did not look down. "I have written it as rest, medical."

"You did not wake me."

"You were in my herb bed." She put the gloves on one at a time. "I have been looking for you in this garden for a month and you have been sleeping in it for a month, and I did not once think to look down."

"You knew I was here."

"I knew the hinge was loose." She shut the book. "I did not know whose hand had pushed it." She stood up and brushed the leaves off her skirt, and there was a small green smear on one knee from where she had knelt beside him. "You knew I gardened here the first day."

"Yes."

"For a whole month you never told anyone."

"No."

She stood over him with the book under her arm and the green smear on her knee, and the trowel lay point down between them. Then she took off her coat and laid it across his boots, because the evening had gone cold, and walked to the door without waking him properly.

Eden took the timetable out and wrote eleven minutes beside Saturday, in the small careful hand that the broken pencil allowed. The bee had gone. The apple tree held its one green fruit above the stone pots, and the mint had started to close its flowers for the night. Two prefects went past on the stair below with a ladder and did not look up. Eden kept the coat on his boots until the light went off the wall.

On the stair she stopped. "You can tell Felix I found you," she said. "Tell him eleven minutes. Not a word more."
