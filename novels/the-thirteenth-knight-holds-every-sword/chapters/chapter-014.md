# Chapter 14: The Herb Bed

Eden kept a nap record on the back of an old timetable. It had one line for each day and one mark for each hour he slept. The paper had a brown spot in one corner from Felix's tea. The pencil kept breaking, so the marks were not all the same size.

The goal was simple. For one week, he would sleep only in places where nobody could write him up. It was Saturday now, and the week was not perfect. He had fallen asleep in Spell Grammar on Monday and woken with a page of notes stuck to his cheek.

Felix found him on the roof bench and read the timetable upside down. "Seven lines," he said. "One says 'chair, do not count'."

"The chair was in the library. A chair is not a bed."

"It is if you sleep in it." Felix sat down and put a paper bag between them. "I have two lunches today. One is from the kitchen. One is from Lysette. She asks me every day where you are, and I tell her the truth, slowly. Then she gives me a roll."

"Why slowly?"

"So you have time to move." Felix took out a peppermint, looked at it, and put it back. "Today she asked where you were at the second bell. I said you were in the library. You were not. You were behind the telescope. I did not say which telescope."

"That is a lie."

"It is a small one, and she paid me for it." Felix grinned. "I am a double agent. I am paid in rolls by both sides."

Eden looked at the bird's nest in the telescope. The bird had added a piece of red string. He did not see why, and he decided it was not his business. The roof was quiet in the mornings now, and he had started to think of it as a place that kept his secrets. He had one rule for it. He never took anyone up there who had not already found the way.

A first-year boy ran up the stairs with a folded note. He was out of breath, and his face was full of importance. "From the Committee," he said. "North door. Now."

Eden read the note. It had one word in Lysette's hand, WREN, and a bell number underneath with a line drawn under it. That was how she wrote a summons.

"She has hired runners," Felix said. "That boy is paid in rolls as well. I am losing my business."

Eden stood up. Felix held out the bigger half of his roll to the boy, who took it with both hands. "Thank you, sir," the boy said, as if he had been promoted.

* * *

The north door of the dining hall had a bench beside it and a notice board above it. Lysette stood under the board with a clipboard and three prefects. Two of them held papers. The third held a ladder, and he looked as if he wanted to put it down.

"Wren," she said, without looking up. "Here is what I have done this week. Every class you missed needs a warrant, a paper that goes to the headmistress. The office sent three warrants back. They said 'roof' is a place, not a reason."

"What reason do they want?"

"A reason that fits in a box. So I wrote a new one." She turned the clipboard to show him a line. "It says 'absence from class, medical rest, place: various'."

"Various is not a place."

"It is three places written as one word. The office will accept it." She held out a pen. "Sign here. Then eat something. Those are two different boxes, and neither is called sleep."

Eden signed. Behind him, Felix made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

Seraphine came down the north stairs with a small card in her hand. She stopped beside Felix. "How many?" she asked him.

"How many what?"

"My jokes. I have told you eighteen, and you have laughed at none." She showed him the card. It had eighteen tally marks on it. "Number nineteen is coming."

Eden understood the joke only after Felix explained it, later. Seraphine told Felix jokes and counted each one he did not laugh at. She was not angry. She wanted him to laugh, and she did not know how to ask.

"Eighteen is enough," Felix said, and went red to the ears.

"Nineteen," Seraphine said, and wrote it on the card. "You went red, but you did not laugh. That counts." She went into the hall with her chin up.

Lysette watched them go. Then she looked at the ladder and told the prefect to take it back to the store. "I waited on the roof at noon," she said to Eden, "and you did not come. The garden door was locked, and I hold the only key. So how did you get in last week?"

"The hinge on the east door is loose."

"I know." She said it flatly. "It drops by a finger's width. I have known for a month."

"You never fixed it."

"I fixed the west door last week. I left this one alone." She picked up her book. "A person needs one door that does not argue. Do not tell anyone."

She walked up the stairs. Eden stood at the notice board with the pen still in his hand, like a boy who had been handed a plate and then told it was not for him.

* * *

At noon Lysette tried a stakeout. She sat on the roof with two prefects, a flask of tea and a blanket, and she waited for Eden to arrive. She had a plan. If he came to the roof, she would be there, and she could write the time.

Eden knew about the plan because Felix told him. Felix told him in the arms hall, where Eden was sitting behind a rack of practice spears with a roll in each hand. The two lunches had become a routine by then. The kitchen's came with the soup bell, and Lysette's came with a note that said how many bites she expected.

"Three hours," Felix said, and sat down on the floor beside him. "They have been up there for three hours. One prefect has fallen asleep. Lysette has written four pages and drawn a map of the roof."

"Is the bird still in the telescope?"

"They are leaving it alone. She says it has a right to the roof. It has a better reason than you." Felix handed him the second roll. "Eat. If she comes down and finds you hungry, she will say the stakeout was a failure of her own making."

Eden ate. It was the first time all week that he had eaten two lunches before the first afternoon bell, and he decided not to write it down.

By the afternoon the raid had grown. Warrants lay in three piles on the Committee bench, and runners ran between the hall and the east stairs. One runner reported a boy in grey boots on the roof. Another reported a boy in grey boots in the library. A third reported no boy at all, and Lysette wrote that down as 'no boy, useful'.

She made four copies of every warrant, because the office liked copies and sent back anything with fewer than three. By the last bell the bench looked like a small wall of paper. The runners had started charging each other for news.

Eden went up to the roof garden just before the last bell. The east door was loose on its hinge, as she had said, and he lifted it a finger's width and slipped through. The herb bed was damp from the morning rain. The mint had grown over the stone edge, and the sage had gone woody at the stem. The thyme was thick and green. A small trowel lay where someone had left it, point down on the stone.

He had never wondered whose trowel it was. Now he did. The soil in the bed was fine and dark, and it had been dug many times by someone who kept it neat.

He lay down in the bed, because the grass outside was wet and the soil was still warm from the day. The smell of thyme rose into his coat. A bee worked the mint flowers a hand's width from his ear. He meant to close his eyes for one minute, and he wrote the minute on the timetable before he put it away.

When he woke, the light had gone gold. Lysette was sitting on the stone edge of the bed, two feet from his head. She had her book on her knee and her gloves in her hand. She was looking at the dwarf apple tree, not at him.

The white flower on the highest twig was gone. In its place hung one small green apple, hard and out of season.

"How long?" he asked.

"Eleven minutes." She still did not look down. "I have written it as rest, medical."

"You did not wake me."

"You were in my herb bed." She put on her gloves, one at a time. "I plant it. I dig it. The trowel is mine."

Eden sat up. "I know."

"How long have you known?"

"Since the first week. The soil is dug in rows, and the rows are straight. Nobody else here digs straight."

She turned her head at last. For a moment her face did not hold the Committee's calm. "You never told anyone."

"No."

"Not Felix. Not Rowan. Not the headmistress, who would have been pleased to have a reason to take this garden away from me."

"It was yours," Eden said.

She was quiet for a while. A bee crossed the garden and landed on the sage. "I grow thyme because it asks for nothing," she said. "It does not need a form. It does not need a warrant. Water it, leave it alone, and it comes back every year." She turned the trowel over with one finger. "Everything else I look after has a list."

"Is the list a bad thing?"

"No. It is only a lot." She said it quietly, and then she seemed to hear herself, and her face went back to being the Committee's.

"That is not a reason." She looked at the trowel, then at the green apple, then at him. "That is what I wanted to find out. I did not think anyone could keep a thing for a month and not say it once."

"I kept the roof too."

"Yes. I noticed." She stood and brushed the leaves off her skirt. There was a green smear on one knee where she had knelt beside him. "I wanted to know if you were the kind of person who would use it. You are not. So I will stop writing 'roof' on the warrants, and you will stop saying you were in the library."

"That is two things."

"It is one rule with two ends." She took off her coat, laid it across his boots because the evening had turned cold, and walked to the door. "I will collect it tomorrow. Do not lose it."

At the door she stopped. "You can tell Felix I found you. Tell him eleven minutes. Not a word more."

Eden took out the timetable and counted the week. Seven lines, one of them a chair, one a confession. It was not a perfect week. But for the first time, one of the lines had been written by someone else's hand in someone else's book, and the line said rest, medical, and nobody had been angry. He thought that was a better record than a perfect one.

He wrote eleven minutes beside Saturday. The bee had gone, and the thyme smelled stronger in the cold. The apple tree held its one green fruit above the stone pots, and the mint had started to close for the night. Two prefects walked past on the stair below with a ladder and did not look up. They looked tired, and one of them was still wearing the blanket from the stakeout.

He kept the coat on his boots until the light went off the wall.
