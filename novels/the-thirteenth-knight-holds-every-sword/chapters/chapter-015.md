# Chapter 15: The Bakery

The lower city began where the tram lines ended, and the road sloped down toward the dye works with a canal on the left. Eden got off at the last stop with his collar up and a paper file in his coat, and he walked slowly because he knew someone was following him.

Lysette had come out of the academy gate ten minutes after him, wearing her dark coat and carrying a clipboard. She had told the porter she was checking a student's address in the dye district, and she had written the address in her book before she left. Eden had seen her do it from the library window.

He took the long way. He passed the market where women sold soap in yellow blocks, turned left at a phone booth with a cord that had been cut, and walked under a row of washing lines that crossed the street in grey sheets. A soap seller called out a price, and Eden held up two fingers and kept walking. A man stood at the phone booth with the receiver against his ear and his mouth moving, and the cord lay on the ground in a loop at his feet. No sound came from the receiver, and the man kept nodding. The dye works stood at the end of the road, a low brick building with two chimneys and a yard of blue stains. The Ember cell met in a house behind it, and Ysolde's file had a drawing of the door. The drawing showed a blue door with one window and a crack in the step. Eden had memorised it in the tram, and now he walked past the house with his eyes on the canal. A man in a staff coat stood at the corner of the yard with a cigarette in one hand and a newspaper under his arm, and Eden counted him as the fourth name on the list.

Eden did not look at the house. He walked past it with his hands in his pockets and kept going toward the canal, and after three streets he turned into a narrow lane where the walls were close enough to touch from both sides.

Footsteps followed him at the same distance. They stopped when he stopped, and started when he started, and they were light steps made by someone who did not want to be heard. Eden walked another two streets and then sat on a step outside a closed shop and took out a piece of bread from his pocket.

A girl came around the corner with a basket on her hip. "Wren!" she said, and then she saw the woman behind him and went quiet.

"Hello, Mira," Eden said.

"You did not come yesterday."

"I was busy."

"Rosa saved you the end of the loaf." She held out a small paper parcel. "She said you would come when the trams stopped."

Eden took the parcel and put it in his coat. "Thank her for me."

"I will tell her you said it." The girl looked at Lysette, and then at the clipboard, and she ran back the way she had come.

Lysette stopped a few steps away. "Do you know her?"

"I know several people here," Eden said.

"You said you were going to the library."

"I went to the library yesterday." He stood up from the step and brushed the grey dust from his coat. "You said you were checking an address."

"I am."

"Whose?"

"Yours," Lysette said, and then she shut her mouth, and the colour went up in her face.

Eden looked at her for a moment. "You followed me for six streets to check my address, and I am standing on it."

"You told me you did not come off campus at night."

"I did not say that."

"You let me think it." She held the clipboard against her coat with both hands. "That is worse."

"Yes," Eden said.

They walked on in the direction of the canal, and neither of them spoke for a while. At the corner a woman sold hot chestnuts from a tin drum, and the smoke rose in a thin grey line. Lysette bought a paper cone and held it without eating. "You did not tell me about the children," she said. "People tell that when they know a street."

"I did not know you would follow me."

"You hoped I would not."

"Yes."

She ate one chestnut then, and the paper cone was empty in four bites, and she threw it in a bin. A barge went past with a man at the tiller, and the water slapped the stone edge, and the smell of dye came down the canal in a thin sour line.

"You are looking for the Embers," Lysette said.

"I am looking at a street," Eden said.

"You have a file in your coat, and you walked past the dye works without turning your head. Nobody walks past a building like that without looking at it unless they already know what is inside."

"You are very good at this."

"I know." She did not smile when she said it. "I do not know what you are doing, and I do not like it."

"Who gave you the file?" she asked, and she stopped walking to ask it.

"A woman at the palace."

"Ysolde Lisle."

"Yes," Eden said, and he walked on.

"Then this is crown work." Her voice went flat. "You are a student, and you are doing crown work on a Sunday, and you told no one."

"I am telling you now."

"You are telling me because I followed you."

"Yes."

She wrote a word in her book and crossed it out. "Do you know what an Ember cell does?"

"Buys grey dust, and sells it to people who turn grey."

"Then why watch them and not stop them?"

"Those are different jobs," Eden said.

"Not for a knight with a rank."

"I know," he said, and he looked at the canal. A barge moved under the bridge with a low scrape of rope, and the water rose against the edge and fell.

"Good."

"Good?"

"If you liked it, I would have to stop."

Lysette stopped walking, and Eden stopped with her, and a tram bell rang somewhere up the hill. She looked at him and she looked at a page with a number that did not add up, and then she looked away.

"Where are we going?" she asked.

"To buy bread."

"You already have bread."

"It is for the bakery," Eden said. "The end of the loaf is for me, and I pay for the rest."

* * *

The bakery stood at the bottom of the hill with a green door and a window that had been cracked and mended with tape. Inside, an old woman called Rosa stood at a board with a long peel in her hand, and three children sat on a sack of flour in the corner with their legs swinging.

"Wren," the old woman said, and she did not look surprised. "You are late."

"I brought a friend."

Rosa looked at Lysette and at the clipboard, and then she went back to the oven. "She is not a friend. She writes things down."

"I am Committee," Lysette said. "I am checking an address, not eating."

"Everybody eats." Rosa pulled a flat loaf from the oven and set it on the counter. "The address is here, and it is not a thing you need to write."

The children slid off the flour sack and came to stand beside Eden. One of them had flour on her sleeves up to the elbow, and she kept wiping her hands on her apron and making them dirty again. Rosa told her to stop, and the girl stopped for two seconds and then began again. The oven gave a small crack, and the children all turned to look at it and it might speak. One of them, a boy with a missing front tooth, held out a small wooden horse with one wheel missing. "You fixed the cart last week," he said.

"It was a wheel," Eden said. "Not a cart."

"Same thing." The boy put the wooden horse in Eden's hand and ran off.

A girl with a red scarf took Eden's other hand and showed him a row of counting marks on her palm. "I ate three days," she said. "Two days were bread, and one was soup."

"That is three days," Eden said.

"Rosa said you count."

He put a coin on the counter, and then a second coin, and then a third. The old woman took two and pushed the third back.

"One loaf," she said. "You always pay for two."

"The bread is for the children."

"The children eat because people pay, and you are one of the people." Rosa folded her arms. "Eat something yourself, or take nothing."

Eden looked at the loaf, and then at the coin on the counter, and he took the loaf in his hand. "I will eat it later."

"You will eat it now," Rosa said, and the children clapped because the old woman had won.

Lysette stood beside the door with her clipboard down at her side. She watched him break the loaf and give the first piece to the boy with the missing tooth, and the second to the girl with the red scarf, and the third he put in his own mouth and chewed. The children counted the pieces out loud, and got the count wrong, and started again.

"You said you did not eat," Lysette said.

"I said I ate sometimes."

"You pay for bread you do not take, and here you take it." She looked at Rosa, and then at the children, and then at Eden. "Why?"

"Because they know me here," Eden said.

"That is not an answer."

"I have no better one." He broke the last piece of the loaf in two and gave half to the boy who had run off. "Come back at six, and I will bring the cart wheel."

Lysette held the door for him, and they went out into the cold street. A poster nailed to a post had half of it torn away, and a public telephone box stood on the corner with its glass cracked in a star. A man used the phone with his back to the wind, and he spoke in a low voice and kept looking at the bakery door. At the top of the hill she stopped and shut her book, and she did not write anything in it.

"I am not writing the address," she said.

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me. I do not know what you are doing, and I do not approve of it." She walked toward the tram stop, and then she turned back. "But I know where you go on Sunday, and I will not follow you next time."

"Yes," Eden said.

"You owe me one true answer."

"Ask on Monday."

"Monday," Lysette said, and she got on the tram without looking back.

* * *

That night the dream came with a board at the edge of nothing and a small man writing on it in chalk. The Tutor's board held one line: "What was promised." Eden stood in front of it with his hands at his sides, and the air around the board had no wind and no smell.

The Dragon came in from the right, wearing the shape of a large man in a grey coat, and it was complaining as usual. "The lower gates have no respect for a ledger. In my day a gate stayed where it was put, and a tenant paid in advance, and a landlord could count his rooms without finding a new one every century. I said to Liora, and she said to me..."

It stopped. The board's chalk line stayed where it was, and the small man at the board stopped writing, and the Dragon stood with its mouth still open. Eden waited, and the small man waited too, and the nothing around them kept its silence. In the dream the board usually held three lines, and two of them were always wrong. Tonight it had one line, and there was no wrong part in it.

"What did you say to her?" Eden asked.

The Dragon's face changed, and the grey coat hung on it like a coat on a peg. "I promised someone," it said.

"Who?"

The Dragon did not answer. The board at the edge of nothing went blank, and the chalk fell to the floor in one piece, and the dream ended with the sound of a tram bell far away.
