# Chapter 16: The Line

Eden came out of the hall with his drill lance in its cloth case and his shield on his shoulder, and the cold hit his face at the door. Rowan was waiting on the step with Clara beside him.

"Valerius has never lost a Ladder duel," Rowan said.

"He has lost," Clara said. "Not this year."

"That is what I said." Rowan looked at the lance case. "Are you going to win?"

"No," Eden said.

Rowan opened his mouth and then shut it, and Clara looked at Eden for a while. "Then do not lose badly," she said.

"That is the plan."

"Is he better than the Veil we fought?" Rowan asked.

Clara hit his arm with her book. "Do not say that here."

Eden looked at the hall door. "He is better at standing still."

Rowan held the door open, and Clara went through first, and Felix followed with his hands in his pockets. Felix took a bun from his bag and put it in Eden's hand. "Eaten today?"

"Not yet."

Lysette Hollen came around the corner with her black book under her arm, and she stopped when she saw the five of them, and she did not ask why they stood there. "The yard is west," she said. "The judge is waiting."

"You are early," Eden said.

"I am never early." Lysette walked ahead of them without looking back.

The duel yard was a square of pale stone with a chalk line across the middle, and the judges' table stood on the north side under a grey flag. Five steps north of the line, Valerius tightened his glove with his teeth and did not look at the crowd. Eden stood five steps south with the **[Drill Lance]** in one hand and a drill shield on his arm.

Students lined the north wall in two rows, and the older students kept them behind a rope. The judges wore white collars and carried clipboards, and each had two pens, one for the touch and one for the bell. The grey flag above them had no crest, and it moved when nobody touched it.

Okoru stood at the west wall with a strip of dried meat in his hand and chewed without hurry. "Ladder duel," he told the older student beside him. "One touch to the body, or the bell. Nobody here will rescue anybody."

"Is the boy nervous?" the older student asked.

"He is never nervous," Okoru said. "That is the problem."

Okoru put his stick on the ground between the two positions and pointed at each end of it. "Say it back, Wren."

"Place: the duel yard, chalk line in the middle. Foe: Valerius and his walls. Stakes: my mark, and my cover. Clock: the judges' bell."

"And?"

"One new thing today, and I do not know what it is."

"Good." Okoru picked up his stick. "He will show you." He looked at the grey flag. "And no doors. The judges have a counter for shadows."

The judge read from his paper. "Valerius Aurenmark, senior edge. Wren, eight. Bow.""

They bowed, and Valerius's boots did not shift on the stone.

"You placed third in the maze," Valerius said.

"Yes," Eden said.

"I watched your footwork. You step back before you need to."

"I step back when something is in front of me."

"That is what I mean." Valerius pulled his glove straight. "Today I will put three things in front of you, and you can step back from all of them, or you can answer one."

"Which one?"

"You will know when the bell rings."

The **[Crest Harness]** had hung on the rack until the judge called their names, and Eden lifted it down. The coat was dark grey with steel plates sewn into the chest and shoulders, and the plates took the cold out of the air faster than the cloth. He put the coat on and pulled the buckles tight one after another. Each buckle gave a small dry click. The shoulder plates settled with a heavier sound, and the bracers locked at his wrists. The belt pulled the weight down onto his hips. The boots had iron in their soles, and the flagstones rang when he stepped.

Eden knocked once on the iron ring of the shield, and the iron rang, and the judges looked up. He held the shield out and let the weight settle, and the pull went into his wrist and then into his elbow.

The helm came last. It was a plain steel cap with a dark bar over the eyes, and the inside smelled of oil. Eden pulled it down and looked out through a narrow strip of the yard. One breath went in, and one breath came out, and the plates stayed where they were when he moved. His knees felt the weight of every step.

Valerius wore a grey practice harness with a white band on his chest, and his longsword had a blunted point and a bright new grip. He flexed his left hand twice and then stopped, and he kept his eyes on Eden's front foot.

"Are you ready?" the judge asked.

"Yes," they said together, and the judge raised the bell.

Valerius drew his sword and set the point on the chalk line. He dragged it one foot to the left, and the steel made a clean pale scratch on the stone.

"**[Injunction]**," he said. "This line is not crossed."

A wall came up along the scratch. It was waist-high and thin as glass, so pale that Eden could see the judge's boots through it, and the air near it went cold. Valerius's breath came out in a short white puff. He stepped around the wall without looking at it.

Eden moved back one step.

Valerius set his point on the line again. "A line is a promise," he said. "Break it, and the wall moves."

"Who moves it?"

"The one who made it." He dragged the second scratch, and the second wall rose. "You are still behind the first one."

"I am still standing."

"For now."

"Are you afraid of a line?" Valerius asked.

"I am measuring it," Eden said.

"Measure this one."

The second wall rose on Eden's left. Valerius's breath went out again, shorter than the first, and a thin white showed at the corner of his mouth. He dragged a third line with the sword point, and the third wall came up on Eden's right. The three walls made a funnel, and the funnel pointed at the south wall of the yard. The judge called, "Wren, no pausing. Continue."

Eden stepped sideways and put a hand on the nearest wall. It was cold, and his glove stuck to it for a moment, and he pulled it away. "How long do they last?"

"As long as I pay for them," Valerius said. "Ask with your feet, not your mouth."

The third wall held its place, and the space between the walls was narrow enough that Eden had to turn his shield edge-on to move through it. The flag moved again, and the cold from the walls went down into the chalk line, and the line held.

"Say the rule," Valerius said.

"This line is not crossed," Eden said, and he said it in the flat voice he used for sums.

"Then stay behind it."

Eden gave ground. He let his shoulder drop an inch and let the lance point fall, and Valerius's eyes went to the drop and a duellist's eyes always went to a weakness. The crowd along the north wall went quiet, and the grey flag moved once although the air was still.

* * *

The judge rang the half-bell, and the sound went across the yard and came back off the stone.

Eden looked at the gap under the wall and then at Valerius's front boot. The boot stood an inch too far forward, and the sword hand was one beat behind it. He had seen that in the maze, in the constructs that moved on a clock. A thing that moved on a clock always showed its next step first.

Valerius came on between his own walls, and the sword stayed low and close to his hip. He did not hurry and he did not shout, and Eden watched the front of his boot instead of his face.

"You are not using the door trick," Valerius said.

"No," Eden said.

"Everybody uses something."

"I use the lance."

"Then use it before the bell."

Eden looked at the stub of the lance, and at the gap he had found, and he did not answer.

"Your walls are thin," Eden said.

"They are lines," Valerius said. "A line should be thin."

"This one has a gap."

"Then walk through it."

"Here," Valerius said, and his sword dragged a fourth line.

The wall rose too fast. Valerius had placed it in a hurry, and its edge did not meet the ground. A gap stood under it, wide enough for a boot. Eden put his shield into the gap and pushed. The wall slid sideways with a sound like thin ice breaking, and it passed his shoulder and left cold air behind.

Valerius's sword came in at his ribs. Eden turned on his front foot and a door turns on its iron pin, and the blade went past the place where his body had been. His heel stayed where it was, and his hips went around, and the lance butt came up under Valerius's knee and stopped one finger short of the joint.

"**[Hinge]**," Eden said, and his voice stayed flat and quiet.

The front foot went numb as he set it down. The bell rang on the touch, and the two men stood very close, with the lance butt an inch from the knee and Valerius's sword an inch from the ribs, and neither of them moved. Valerius took half a step back and put out his free hand, and Eden kept the lance where it was. The judge's pen scratched on paper. Along the north wall, students began to stand.

Through the narrow strip of the helm, Eden saw Rowan stand up on the wall with both fists in the air. Lysette sat on the bench beside the older students with her book open on her knee, and she was writing, and she did not look up at all.

Okoru had stopped chewing. The strip of meat hung from his hand, and he looked at Eden's front foot, and then at the pale wall pushed aside on the stone, and then at the chalk line again.

The judge stood up. He took the sheet from his clipboard and read it twice, and he looked at both fighters and wrote one line on his sheet. "Touch to neither. The bell rules. A narrow loss to Valerius on points."

Rowan's voice came from the wall. "Wren! He touched your knee!"

"He did not," Lysette said, and she said it across the yard, and Rowan sat down.

Valerius turned to look at her. "You keep his notes, Hollen."

"I keep the notes of everyone in my year," Lysette said. "You have twelve absences, and three were illness."

Valerius's mouth moved, and then he shut it, and his jaw worked once. "Twelve," he said. "That is a good number for a duellist."

"The bell saved him," Valerius said.

"The bell is the rule," the judge said.

"It is not the answer I came for," Valerius said.

"The answer is on the stone," the judge said, and he wrote it down.

"I know the rule." Valerius looked at the lance butt for a while. "I wanted an answer."

He lowered his sword, and the blade slipped from his hand and fell on the stone, and the sound went across the yard. Eden kept his shield up until the blade lay still, and then he let it down, and his front foot told him it would not take another step.

Eden looked at the pale marks on the stone and said nothing.

"Again," he said. "And this time try."
