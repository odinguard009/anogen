# Chapter 17: A Finger's Width

*Code of the Ladder, Rule Nine. A bout in which a blade is lost without a touch is void. Either duelist may ask the court to resume. Two judges must agree.*

That was the whole rule. It was four lines in a clerk's neat hand, on a slip of paper, and Valerius held it up between two fingers as if he were showing a coin.

"I ask the court to resume," he said.

He did not say it loudly because he did not need to. The whole court had gone so quiet that I could hear the sand shift under the benches.

"Seconded," said Brandmeier at once. She loves a rule the way other people love dogs,, and she was almost singing. "It's in the rules. It's clearly in the rules."

Okoru said nothing. He put the licorice root back in his cheek and chewed it slowly while he thought, and that was worse.

That left Dame Ostwald. She sat with her flask between her knees and tried to open the lid with one hand, because the other hand held her cane. The lid had been winning for a while. She gave it a last twist, frowned at it and set it down on the table.

"Resume," she said. "I want to see how it ends."

She did not look at me. That was kind of her, but it was also a decision, and I noticed.

I could not refuse. Refusing meant the Inquiry, and the Inquiry meant a clerk with a long list of questions. Where was Wren born? The answer sat in a locked drawer in the palace. So I stood in my corner, and my left side burned every time I breathed. I thought about the one thing I still had to decide. How to lose.

Everyone thinks losing is easy. It isn't. If you lose too soon, you are a coward. If you lose too well, you are an actor. If you lose by a lot, people ask what a man on the eighth rung does all day. Only one loss is never questioned, and that is the close one, where the better man wins by a hair and everyone feels the hair. It needs a finger's width. No more.

I had one chance to find it. After that, Valerius would stop being polite.

"Wren," said Valerius.

"Sir."

"Valerius."

"Still Wren."

He almost smiled. Then he lowered his blade, and his face went flat and cold and honest. I had seen that face once, years ago, on the stairs, when someone said something bad about his sister. Now he was done holding back.

* * *

The second bout lasted ninety seconds, and I remember every one of them. The sand was cold under my boots, and the evening sun made long orange stripes between the benches.

"This court is closed," said Valerius. He brought his sword down across the sand.

A wall rose across the middle of the sand, ten paces long. He stepped left and drew another, then stepped right and drew a third. In three breaths the oval was gone. It was a set of lanes with cloudy walls, and I stood in one lane while he stood at the end of it.

"I won't stand here and be hit," I said.

"No. I know you won't."

He ran down the lane.

So I ran too, the other way, because a dead end with a prince in it is a bad place to be. I reached the end of my lane and found a gap. I went through it sideways and found another lane, and then a turn. The walls were head-high panes of cloudy force, and I could hear his boots through them. He ran one lane over, level with me, the way a man walks a horse along a fence.

"You're slower on the left," he called through the wall.

"I'm saving it."

"For what?"

"Lunch."

"You've never once been funny in a fight, Wren."

"You've never once listened."

"I'm listening now. Your boots are loud."

"They're new."

"They're terrible."

He drew another line, and a wall rose in front of my face. My nose almost touched it. I spun and went back, and my cracked rib made a noise inside me like a spoon in a jar.

I went through four lanes, then five. He was herding me. Each time I found a way out, a line was already there, drawn half a second before I arrived. He was not chasing me. He was reading me. He had studied how Wren runs, and that meant he had watched me for years.

I took a corner too fast. My right glove scraped along the edge of a wall, and the cold force took the skin off my knuckles through the leather. I did not stop, because a runner who stops is a dead runner, and I had decided not to be dead.

Then I saw the end.

On the north side of the court, below the judges' table, there was a pocket with three walls and a gap. In the gap, six feet away, stood the bell stand with its rope hanging from the bell's tongue. Okoru's hand rested close to it.

I had been counting rope lengths since the first lane. That was my plan, and it was a small one. The bell rings when a judge sees a clean touch, and Okoru pulls the rope when he is sure. He has a habit. His hand lifts one finger's width before he pulls, and a good student of Okoru learns to watch that hand like a clock.

I ran into the pocket, turned and put my back to the wall.

Valerius came around the last corner. He had read me perfectly and knew I had no road left.

"Done," he said, and he lunged.

The lance came up in my left hand on a good, true line. It would reach his throat before his blade reached my chest. I knew it the way you know a step is there in the dark. I could use the Hinge, or one more small step, and either of them would end it my way. The finish was right there, a finger's width from the tip of my lance, and it was beautiful.

I held it.

Okoru's hand lifted one finger's width.

I let his blade come in. It touched the front of my coat, just over my chest, as softly as a knock on a door. And the bell rang.

It rang while his blade was still on me and my lance was still in the air, a finger's width from his throat. The whole court could see how close it was. The year gasped, and someone began to cheer and stopped, as if he had been caught.

Valerius did not move, and his blade still touched my coat. He looked down my lance, along its whole length, to where the tip hung in the air.

"The first rung wins," said Brandmeier. She sounded sad. I think she wanted a longer bout.

After the bell, Rowan caught my sleeve with both hands.

"You were so close! Did you see his face? I thought you had him!"

"Ribs," I said.

"What?"

"You're pulling on the side with the ribs."

"Oh! Sorry. But you did have him!"

"I didn't."

"The lance was right there! I saw it!"

"Rowan," said Clara quietly. "Let go of his arm."

He let go. Felix stood a little behind them with his hands in his pockets and said nothing at all. He looked at my right glove, which was red at the knuckles. Then he looked at my face, and then he looked away. That is the other kind thing he does.

* * *

"You held it," said Valerius.

He had followed me into the waiting room. The door was shut and no one else was there. He stood with his back against it, breathing hard, with his sword still bare in his hand.

"I lost."

"You lost by one finger. I counted. You could have had me. You had me, Wren, and you gave it away." His voice shook. It was the first time I had ever heard it shake. "That was not a loss. That was a favor."

"It was a bell."

"Don't." He pointed the sword at the floor. "Don't be funny at me. I have been your rival for five years, and I have never beaten you. Do you understand? Not once. I only beat the part you let me see."

"What do you want me to say? Sorry?"

"I want you to say why."

"Wren."

"I heard you."

I had no answer, so I sat down on the bench because my ribs asked me to.

Then the door pushed open behind him, and he stepped aside like a man stepping out of a river. Lysette stood there with a roll of gauze and a tin of salve in her hands. The duel book was still under her arm.

"Out," she said to Valerius.

He went. She did not even look at him.

She shut the door and put the book down on the bench. "Give me your hand."

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. You're bleeding on the Committee's floor. Hand."

I gave her the right one. She took off the glove and looked at my knuckles, and her mouth went small and tight. She took some salve on two fingers and started to work as if she were angry at the hand.

"Ow."

"It's supposed to hurt."

"That's not true."

"It's a little true." She wound the gauze around my knuckles, three times, then four, and pulled each turn tight to make a point. "You could have yielded. I told you to yield."

"You said to say it out loud."

"And you didn't!"

"I didn't need to."

Her hands stopped. She tied the knot, and it came undone. She tied it again, and that one came undone too. She said something under her breath that the Committee handbook does not allow.

"It's the gauze," she said. "It's cheap gauze. The bursar buys it."

"I know."

"Don't say you know."

The third knot held. She pressed her thumb on it, hard, and left it there a second longer than the knot needed.

"I watched the whole thing," said Lysette. "I watched you in the lanes. I have kept notes on you for weeks, Wren, and today you ran as if you had drawn that map yourself." She did not look at me. "And then you let him win."

I said nothing.

"I don't know what that is yet," she said. "But I will."

She picked up her book and left without slamming the door. I sat there with my hand wrapped like a parcel and a crack in my ribs, and I did the thing she had not asked me to do. I did nothing.

Losing hurt, and I will admit that. It hurt in a small, stupid place. I had held the finish in my hand and put it down. My whole life is putting things down, and I am good at it. Today it felt like a skill I never asked for.

* * *

The corridor behind the armory was empty when I came out. Evening light lay on the stones in orange bars.

Valerius waited at the far end, alone, with his cloak on again and his sword back in its sheath. He did not come closer because the corridor was narrow and his voice carried.

"My father knows who you are," he said.

My stomach did not move, because I am good at that too.

"So do I," said Valerius. "Not the name. I don't need the name. I know what you are, and I know what you held back. I have known since the stairs." He turned to go. Over his shoulder he said quietly, "Next time I won't ask politely. I will make you show them."
