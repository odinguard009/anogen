# Chapter 17: The Bell

Eden came down the stair with the bandage on his right hand and the lance case on his shoulder, and Felix walked with him because Felix walked with him on duel mornings.

"Does it hurt?" Felix asked.

"No."

"That means yes."

"It means no." Eden flexed his fingers, and the bandage pulled at the knuckles. "Lysette tied it."

"Ah." Felix put a bun in his hand. "Then it hurts."

Rowan waited at the bottom of the stair with Clara, and he carried a wooden sword from the practice rack. "I am your second," he said.

"You are not my second."

"I wrote it in my book." Rowan swung the wooden sword once and hit the wall, and Clara took it away from him. "I will stand on the wall and shout," he said.

"That is what you did yesterday."

"Then I will shout better."

Lysette came along the corridor with a fresh bandage roll in her coat pocket, and she looked at Eden's hand and then away. "The yard is clear," she said. "Valerius has been there since first bell."

"He is early."

"He is always early." She walked ahead and did not turn.

Felix leaned close to Eden. "She packed three bandages."

"Why three?"

"Because you lost by one touch."

The duel yard was scuffed where the pale walls had stood, and the chalk line had been redrawn in a thicker hand. It kept the marks of two days of boots. A bellwire ran from the judges' table into the hall, and the clerk at the other end kept a sheet with two columns. The grey flag hung above the judges with no crest on it.

Eden stood five steps south of the line with the **[Drill Lance]** low and the drill shield on his arm, and the **[Crest Harness]** sat square on his shoulders, cold at the chest and warm at the back from yesterday's sun.

Okoru leaned on the west wall with another strip of dried meat, and he was chewing when the judge raised the bell. "Good," he said around the meat. "Yesterday he stopped chewing, and I did not like it."

"Is that a rule?" the older student beside him asked.

"No," Okoru said. "It is a habit, and habits tell you when a day has gone wrong."

Okoru pointed his stick at the chalk line. "Say it."

"One touch, or the bell. No doors, no tricks on the stone," Eden said.

"And?"

"Win if you can."

Okoru chewed for a while. "That last part is mine, not the rule book."

Valerius bowed from the north side and came forward without a word. He did not drag his sword on the line this time. He set his boots apart, and he came in from the left with the blade held high near his shoulder.

Eden gave ground one step and watched the sword hand. Valerius's walls had been lines on stone, and lines had been honest; a thing placed in a hurry showed its gap. This was different. Valerius came at him with his whole body, and there was no line to read and no clock to count.

Valerius came in from the left again, and the sword stayed high. Eden met it with the shield rim, and the impact ran up his arm. He stepped back two inches, and the flagstones were cold through his boots.

The shield took the first cut on the rim. The sound went up Eden's arm and into his shoulder. He slid his back foot and turned the second cut aside with the lance shaft. The blunted point scraped the stone. Valerius was stronger than the constructs. He was faster than a senior should have been. He pressed without anger, and he did not waste a step.

"Your left foot is slow," Valerius said.

"It was hurt yesterday," Eden said.

"Then I will use the left."

"You already are."

Valerius's next cut went at the bandaged hand. Eden took it on the shield and let the force turn him half a step. The shield rim rang, and the sound went across the yard. He set his feet again and kept the lance point low, and Valerius came on, and the gap between them closed to one stride.

"Why do you never strike first?" Valerius asked.

"Because you are better."

"That is not a reason to lose."

"It is a reason to wait."

"You are waiting for me to make a mistake," Valerius said.

"No."

"Then what?"

"For the bell." Eden moved his shield an inch. "It is the only thing in this yard that does not lie."

Valerius came again, and the sword went high and then low. Eden read the low cut on the third step. He turned it on the shield rim, and the wood of his lance scraped the stone. Valerius came back with a cut at the helm bar. Eden dropped his shoulder and let it pass. The flag fluttered once.

Valerius's blade came at his shoulder. Eden turned his hip and let it pass. He put the lance point at the space under Valerius's arm, and he held it there for one beat, and then he took it away.

He gave another step and let his elbow drop. The sword went over the drop and under his guard, and it touched two ribs through the harness.

Eden did not move. He let the blade sit where it was. The plates held, and the bandage pulled at his knuckles. His heel stayed where it was. He counted one beat, and the judges' pens moved on their clipboards. He counted two, and the crowd stopped shouting. He counted three, and the judge's hand came down. The bell rang.

The yard went quiet. The blade was still touching the two ribs, and Eden's lance was still an inch short of Valerius's knee, and the bell had rung while both men stood there.

"Touch to Valerius," the judge called. "A narrow loss."

Valerius pulled his sword back and looked at it, and then at Eden, and then at the judge's bell. His face did not change, but the vein at his temple stood out, and his jaw worked twice before he spoke.

"You waited for the bell," he said.

"I lost on touch," Eden said.

"You waited for the bell to ring on my touch." Valerius put the sword in its scabbard with one hard motion. "You could have turned that blade at your ribs. You did not turn it because you wanted the loss to be exactly one touch."

"It is a Ladder duel," Eden said. "One touch is a loss."

"A loss is a loss." The judge wrote on his sheet and looked at the bell in his hand. "The touch is recorded," he said. "Both men may speak."

Valerius did not speak to the judge. He walked three steps toward the north gate and came back, and his gloves were tight in his hand.

Valerius stepped closer. "You made it a message."

"You held back at the first cut," he said. "You turned the second with the shaft, and you could have used the point."

"The point was not there."

"The point was there." Valerius held up two fingers. "Twice. You took both away."

"You are very close," Eden said.

"I am close enough." Valerius looked at the bandage again. "My father used to say that a man who hides his hand is either a coward or a king. You are not a coward."

He stopped, and he looked at the pale marks on the stone, and then at Eden's face, and he made it a message.

The crowd along the north wall was on its feet, and Rowan was shouting something about the bell, and Clara was pulling his sleeve. Lysette came through the rope with a bandage roll in her hand and her book under her arm, and she walked past Valerius without looking at him, and she stopped in front of Eden. She took his hand and turned it over, and her fingers went over the old bandage.

"Give me your hand," she said.

He gave her the hand. She unwound the old bandage in one pull. She wrapped a fresh strip around his knuckles and pulled it tight.

"Hold still," she said.

"I am holding still."

"You are holding still like a man who expects to be hit." She pulled the ends down with her teeth and tied them. The knot pressed into the bone of his wrist. "There is a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes." She looked at the knot and then at his face, and her brow was drawn. "You let him touch you twice yesterday and once today, and you wrote nothing in your own book about it. I wrote all three."

"I do not keep a book."

"You keep one in your head." She pressed the knot once more, and he let her, and she did not soften her hand. "It is a bad habit."

"So I have been told."

"You pressed that knot too hard," Eden said.

"Good." She wrote another line. "If it is loose, you will pull it off and fight with an open hand, and then the next person who touches you will leave a mark."

"It is a small cut."

"It is your hand." She put the bandage roll back in her pocket and shut the pocket with her thumb. "I have bandaged six people this term, and four of them did not take care of a small cut."

"And the other two?"

"They are in this yard."

Felix came over while she was writing, and he looked at the knot on Eden's hand. "Three bandages," he said. "She brought three."

"She did."

"Did you need three?"

"No."

Felix nodded and put the empty bun bag in his coat. "I will tell her you said so."

She wrote a line in her black book and walked back through the rope without asking why he had waited for the bell. The bandage held his knuckles tight enough that he could feel his pulse in them.

* * *

The yard emptied slowly, and the judges carried their table inside, and the grey flag came down from its pole. Valerius stayed by the north wall with his gloves in one hand, and he waited until Lysette was gone and the older students had taken the rope away.

Eden took off the helm and held it under his arm, and the cold air came into his face. The plates of the harness held their weight through his shoulders, and the strip of meat in Okoru's hand had stopped moving.

Valerius came close enough that his voice would not carry. "My father knows who you are. So do I." He looked at Eden's hands, and at the tight bandage, and then at the chalk line. "Next time I'll make you show them."

Eden did not answer, and Valerius walked out through the north gate with his sword at his side, and the grey flag was gone from above him.

Felix fell in beside him as they left the yard, and Rowan came behind with the wooden sword under his arm. "You lost on purpose," Rowan said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the bell was the only honest part."

Rowan thought about it and then gave up, and Felix put a bun in Eden's free hand. "Eaten today?"

"Yes," Eden said, and this time it was true.

The clerk at the end of the bellwire typed the result twice, because the first sheet had a smudge.

Rowan carried the wooden sword back to the rack, and he put it in the wrong slot, and Clara moved it to the right one without saying anything.

Okoru stood at the west wall until they were gone, and he put the rest of the dried meat in his pocket without eating it. He wrote one word on his slate and turned it face down. The word was "wait," and the angles of it were sharp in the chalk.
