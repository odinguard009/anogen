# Chapter 18: The Night of Lights

Ysolde gave the order in the palace corridor on Friday morning, and she did not stop walking while she gave it. "Watch Rowan without Rowan noticing," she said. "That is the whole order, and it is harder than it sounds."

"Why him?" Eden asked.

"Because the call list has his name twice, and because the Embers are asking about him in the lower city." She stopped at a window and turned a card over in her hand. "You are the only person he does not look at twice."

"I am easy to forget."

"You are difficult to see." Ysolde put the card away. "Those are different things. Report at the end of the week."

Eden walked back to the academy with the order in his coat and a small notebook in his pocket. He had watched Rowan for two weeks without meaning to, and he knew things about him that Rowan had never told anyone. Rowan put his books down in a stack of three. He ate bread before soup. He said good morning to the gate porter by name, and he never remembered the names of the prefects who sat beside him at assembly.

Rowan Tallis noticed everything except danger. The next day he saw that Clara had changed the band on her wrist, and he asked her about it in the middle of class. The day after that he saw a new stitch in Felix's coat and asked who had sewn it. When Seraphine sat down beside him, he told her that her tea was cold before she had taken a sip.

"That is a strange thing to say," Seraphine said.

"It is cold," Rowan said.

"It was hot when I poured it."

"You poured it four minutes ago." He looked at the cup. "It is cold now."

Seraphine looked at the cup and then at Eden. "Does he do that often?"

"Yes," Eden said.

"Is it a gift?"

"It is a habit." Eden turned a page in his book. "He looks at everything." Rowan's shirts were always buttoned wrong at the top, and he never noticed, and Clara had stopped telling him.

At practice Rowan stood in the middle of the yard and called out positions for the whole group, and he got two of them wrong, and he corrected himself without stopping. Clara put a mark on the ground with her staff where she wanted the shield, and Rowan stepped on the mark and thanked her.

"You are standing in my line," Lysette said.

"Then move the line."

"The line is the drill."

"Then I am the drill." Rowan moved one foot and stood in the mark again.

Lysette shut her book and wrote a line in it. "One day you will fall over that foot."

"I have fallen over worse."

Eden watched from the wall with his book open on his knee, and he wrote nothing. Rowan turned once and looked straight at the wall, and Eden turned a page, and Rowan went back to the drill.

Felix stood beside Eden for a while with his bow case on his shoulder. "He saw you," he said.

"I know," Eden said. "He looks at everything except the thing that matters."

Felix thought about that. "Then he will never see the man in the grey coat," he said.

"No," Eden said.

A man in a grey staff coat stood across the court for ten minutes, and Rowan did not see him at all. Eden watched the man from the library window. The man wrote in a notebook, and he kept his left hand in his pocket, and his coat had a tear under the arm where a badge had been pulled off. When Rowan came out of class with Clara, the man folded the notebook and walked toward the gate ahead of them.

Eden followed at a distance into the lower city, and he kept one hand in his coat pocket with the notebook ready. The man took the tram to the dye stop and walked down toward the canal. Rowan and Clara went another way with a prefect, so Eden followed the man. That was the easier choice. Ysolde's order had said to watch Rowan, and the man who watched Rowan was part of the same problem.

The man walked past the bakery where the children knew Eden's name, and he did not look at it. He turned into a lane with no lamps. The lane smelled of wet lime and old dye, and a drain ran along one side with a thin line of water. A cat sat on a windowsill and watched Eden pass, and then it went back to sleep. Halfway down, the man took a small mirror from his pocket and held it up at an angle, and the glass caught the light from the street behind them. Eden stepped into a doorway and waited until the mirror went away. Eden stopped at the mouth of the lane and waited, and the man stopped too, and then he went on into the dark.

Eden followed him with one hand on the wall and his feet placed carefully, because the lane had a step down in the middle that he remembered from the bakery walk. The wall on the left held the shadow of a high cellar grate, and a hard edge of light ended there. He put two knuckles on the edge and knocked twice. A **[Door]** opened in the dark under the grate, black and upright, with a thin line of light at one side.

The man turned at the sound. Eden stepped into the opening, and the man came after him, because the lane was narrow and the only way past was through the dark. Eden shut the black opening on him and waited ten breaths with his hand on the wall. The lane was a lane again, with the step down in the middle and no light at all, and the man was held in the dark. Eden did not know how long the dark held a person, and he did not want to find out with his hand on the grate. The stone under his boots was damp, and the wind came down the lane in short pushes, and a tram bell rang twice at the stop he had passed.

Eden lifted the iron grate and climbed into the empty cellar. The cellar held a stack of empty crates and a broken lamp, and the floor had been swept recently. The man sat on the floor with his back against a barrel and his eyes closed, and he was asleep, and there was no grey on his hands or his face. Eden picked up the notebook from the stones and read it by the light coming through the grate.

The grate made a small sound when he shut it, and he listened at the top of the lane until his breath was steady.

The notes were short. "Tallis, talks to the healer girl after class." "Tallis, no evening class." "Alive on the night of lights." Under that last line the man had drawn a small square and written a number in the corner: 12.

Eden turned the page. The next sheet carried a list of names typed in three columns, and he knew the list because he had read it in Ysolde's office: forty-one names, with circles and crosses beside some of them. This copy had no circles. At the top someone had written "please confirm" in a wide, careful hand, and the hand had a small hook at the end of the last letter.

Eden shut the notebook and put it back on the stones. He took the man's cloth badge from under the barrel and put that in his pocket instead. Then he climbed out of the cellar, pulled the grate shut, and walked back up the lane with his collar against the wind.

* * *

The palace office smelled of paper and cold tea, and Ysolde kept her cards face down in a fan. Eden put the badge on her desk and the notebook on top of it, and he did not tell her where he had found them.

"The call list," he said. "The Embers have a copy, and they are checking the names."

Ysolde looked at the notebook without touching it. "Where did you get this?"

"From a man who was watching Rowan."

"Is he dead?"

"No."

"Good." She opened the notebook and turned to the list. "The exchange call list was the guest list for the old banquet two years running. I wondered why the Crowns wanted it. They are not calling people. They are checking who will be in one room on one night."

"The night of lights," Eden said.

"Twelve December." Ysolde shut the notebook and put it in her drawer. "The Ball is on the twelfth, and the king will be there, and so will every minister with a seat at the war council." She laid a card on the drawer. "They want the boy alive on that night, which means they want him there."

"Why?"

"That is your question again." Ysolde looked at him over the cards. "You may answer it before the Ball, or you may answer it after. I would prefer before."

"What does 'alive' mean in that sentence?" Eden asked.

"It means they do not want him dead before the Ball." Ysolde wrote the word on a slip of paper and pushed it across the desk. "It may also mean they do not care what happens after."

"Then someone will be waiting for him there."

"Yes." She looked at the slip. "You are thinking of a rescue."

"No," Eden said. "I am thinking of a room with one door."

Ysolde put her hand over the slip. "Then think of the windows as well."

Eden took the badge from the desk. It was a square of grey cloth with a small burnt mark where a flame had been sewn, and the thread had been pulled out in a hurry. "Are the other names on the list coming?"

"Some of them." Ysolde turned a card over. "The duke will be there, and the headmistress, and the border captains. Your name is on the working list as a senior student, though I cannot imagine who put it there."

"I can," Eden said.

"Then tell me who."

"Later," Eden said, and he did not look at the card in her hand.

"Later is a word you use when you have no answer."

"It is a word I use when the answer is not mine to give."

Ysolde put the cards in a pile and squared the edges. "Then keep your answer, and bring me a better report next week." She looked at the badge in his hand. "Do not wear that."

"I was not planning to."

"I know what it is," Eden said.

"That is not what I said."

"You said not to wear it."

"Yes." Ysolde put the notebook back in the drawer and locked it. "A badge is a thing a person can see from across a room, and you are the one boy in this city who is easy to miss. Do not give them a mark to look for."

Outside the office the corridor was empty, and the palace clock said four. Eden put the badge in the lining of his coat instead of the pocket. He went past the kitchens and the stable yard and up the long stair to the plateau, and the city was a grid of yellow windows below him. A tram turned at the corner with a scrape of metal, and the palace clock rang four behind him. He counted the days: the next day, and the day after, and then four more, and then the Ball. Nine days. By the time he reached the gate, Rowan's name was still in his mind, circled twice on a list of people who would stand in one room under lanterns on the twelfth of December.
