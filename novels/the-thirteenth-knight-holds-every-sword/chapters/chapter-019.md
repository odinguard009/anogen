# Chapter 19: The East Kitchen

The east kitchen was the size of a classroom. Three ovens stood along one wall, and a long table ran down the middle. The cooking club had covered the table in flour before Lance Nine arrived. Rowan had volunteered the Lance for the Ball-week bake in the middle of lunch. He had volunteered it before asking anyone.

"The Ball needs cakes," he told them. "Four hundred small cakes, and we are the ones making them."

"The club captain wrote four hundred on the board, and she drew a star beside it," Eden said. "She also wrote 'no explosions' underneath."

"We are?" Felix asked.

"Yes." Rowan tied an apron on and tied it in a knot that would not come undone, and then he asked Clara to untie it. "I said yes for all five of us."

Lysette stood in the doorway with a clipboard and a white apron over her dark coat. "I heard volunteering," she said. "I am here to inspect the kitchen."

"You are not on the cooking club," Rowan said.

"I am on the Committee, and the Committee inspects anything that can catch fire." She wrote a number on her form. "This room has three ovens and one fire blanket, which is not enough."

"One is enough if you use it," Rowan said.

"Nobody in this room knows how to use it," Lysette said.

"We can read the label."

"The label is on the blanket," Lysette said, and she wrote a number on the form.

Seraphine came in behind her with Felix by the sleeve. "I brought him," she said. "He is good with his hands."

Felix looked at the flour on the table. "I am good with a bow."

"You tied a knot at breakfast," Seraphine said. "That is hands." Felix folded the apron again into a shape with five corners. "It is a mistake," he said.

"It is a very good mistake," Seraphine said, and she wrote in her book.

Clara set a bowl on the table and measured flour into it with a cup. Her hands were steady, and the bowl was full to the line she had marked. Eden stood at the end of the table with a knife and a pile of dried fruit, and he cut each piece into four equal parts, and he kept the parts in four small heaps.

"Why four heaps?" Rowan asked.

"Four hundred cakes," Eden said. "Divide the fruit first."

Rowan stared at the heaps for a moment, and then he went to find a larger bowl. He came back with a pot that had held soup the day before. "This will do," he said.

"It will taste of soup," Felix said.

"Not if we add enough sugar."

Rowan looked at the heaps, and then at the bowl of batter, and then at the clock over the ovens. "That is a good way," he said, and he tipped half a bag of sugar into the bowl and stirred it with a spoon.

The first tray went in, and the second tray went in, and the third tray stayed on the table because Rowan had forgotten to grease it. Clara reached for the oven with her bare hand, and the handle was hot, and she pulled back and put her hand under her arm.

"That is a burn," she said.

"Can you heal it?" Felix asked.

"Yes." Clara closed her eyes for two breaths, and the red mark on her fingers went away, and she opened her eyes and took a tray from the table. "I cannot heal a soufflé. Somebody has to watch it."

"I can watch it and fold an apron," Seraphine said.

"You cannot watch anything and fold an apron," Felix said.

"You are standing in my light," she said, and she moved him one step with her shoulder.

"I will watch it," Seraphine said, and she stood in front of the oven with her hands behind her back and her head on one side, and she did not watch it at all. She was watching Felix, who was trying to fold an apron into a square.

Lysette walked down the table with her form in one hand and her pen in the other. She checked the ovens, and the fire blanket, and the bucket of water by the door, and she wrote a number beside each thing. When she passed Eden she stopped and looked at his four heaps of fruit.

"Why four?" she asked.

"Four hundred cakes," Eden said.

"Show me the sum."

He told her the sum, and she wrote it on the form, and then she wrote another line underneath. "That is the first sensible thing in this room."

"I am writing it down as a safety measure," she said.

"Fruit is not a safety measure."

"Fruit in equal heaps is a plan, and a plan is a safety measure." She wrote the word "plan" on her form and underlined it.

"It is only fruit."

"It is still a sum." She moved down the table, and her apron brushed the corner of the oven, and she did not notice the small brown mark it left.

Felix finally folded the apron into something that had four corners, and Seraphine took it from his hands and held it up. "That is a perfect square," she said.

"It is not a square."

"It is a square if I say it is." She made a mark in a small notebook and smiled at him. "That is forty."

"Forty what?"

"Jokes you have made me laugh at." She put the notebook away. "One more before the Ball, and I need a bigger book."

"Ask me now," Felix said.

"No. It has to be funny."

"Then it will not happen."

Felix opened his mouth, and then he closed it, and he went back to cutting string with a pair of scissors. Seraphine watched him for a moment, and then she ate a piece of raw fruit from Eden's third heap, and she did not say anything about it. Rowan put the fruit heap back in order while nobody was looking. He counted the pieces and put two back where Seraphine had taken them. Eden watched him do it and said nothing, and Rowan finished the count and looked pleased.

* * *

The third oven began to make a sound like a kettle, and Rowan opened it to see what was wrong. A cloud of hot flour came out and hit the ceiling. The tray in his hand tipped, and the batter went across the table in a white wave.

The oven door fell off.

A jet of flame came out of the oven mouth and ran toward the bowls. The flour in the air turned orange, and the cooking club captain shouted from the door. Clara put her hands up and threw water from the bucket, and the water missed the flame and went across the floor. Rowan pushed the table, and the table did not move, and Felix pulled him back by his apron strings.

Eden had one hand on the **[Exa]** case at his back, and the lance came into his hand black and cold. He knocked once on the iron ring, and it folded into the tray-sized door-shield with the ring on the outside, and he put it between the flame and the bowls, and he held it with both hands. The blast hit the shield and went over their heads. The shield rang once, and the flame went out as if someone had closed a door on it. Eden held it there for two breaths, and the heat came through the steel into his gloves, and then the air behind it went quiet.

Eden unfolded the lance and put it back in the case before anyone turned around.

The kitchen was full of flour and smoke. No one was hurt, and the oven door lay on the floor with a dent in it. Rowan's apron had a burn at the hem, and Clara had already healed it, and Seraphine's notebook had a smear of batter across the cover.

"That was luck," Rowan said, and his voice was thin with surprise. "The flame stopped in the middle of the table."

"It hit the water," Felix said, and he pointed at the floor where the bucket had spilled.

"There is water on the floor and a fire blanket on the hook," Lysette said. "Write both lines in the report."

"Water does not stop flame like that." Rowan looked at the table, and at Eden, and then at the shield rack on the wall where every shield was still hanging. "Where did it go?"

"Into the flour," Eden said. "Flour burns fast and then it is finished."

Rowan looked at him for a while, and then he accepted it. Rowan accepted most things once they had been said plainly. He picked up the oven door and set it against the wall, and he began to scrape batter off the table with a knife. Felix scraped the other end with a knife. Seraphine held the bowl for him, and she did not drop it, and he did not thank her. Clara checked the oven with the back of her hand and said it was cooler, and the cooking club captain took the fire blanket down and folded it correctly.

Seraphine came over with her notebook and looked at the cover, and then at Felix. "That was forty-one," she said.

Felix stopped scraping. "I did not say anything."

"You dropped the oven door and looked at the ceiling." She turned the notebook so he could see the mark she had made. "I counted it, and it failed."

"Then take it back."

"I do not take marks back." She shut the notebook and walked to the door with her head high, and her apron was covered in flour.

Lysette stood by the bucket with her form in her hand and a line of flour across her white collar. Rowan came past her with the oven door, and he stopped and looked at the collar, and his mouth opened.

"Lysette, there is flour on your collar."

"You are holding the door upside down," she said, and she took it from his hands and turned it over, and she wrote another line on her form. "That is a hazard, and it is going in the report."

Rowan looked at the door, and then at her collar, and then at the door again, and he forgot what he had been going to say. "Yes, vice-chair," he said, and he went back to scraping the table.

* * *

The east kitchen was still warm at the end of the afternoon. The four heaps of fruit had become one heap, because Seraphine had mixed them while looking for the notebook. Eden counted the cakes that had survived: ninety-one, all of them small, and half of them burnt at the edge. Rowan counted them twice and got ninety-one both times, and then he saw the cake in Seraphine's hand and got ninety-two, and she ate it before he could write the number down. Seraphine made a mark in her notebook and looked at Felix, and Felix looked at the ceiling, and Rowan asked everybody to count again, and nobody did.

Clara put a cake in her mouth and closed her eyes, and then she opened them. "It is a cake," she said, "and it is not a soufflé."

Felix ate one and said nothing, and Seraphine wrote in her book, and Rowan took the last cake from the tray and held it up to the light as if it might tell him something.

Eden counted again and said nothing. Lysette came back with the finished form and set it on the table, and she did not look at any of them. Seraphine leaned against the table beside her with her flour-covered apron and smiled in a way that was sweet and careful. She waited for the answer.

"Lysette," she said, "who are you going to the Ball with now?"
