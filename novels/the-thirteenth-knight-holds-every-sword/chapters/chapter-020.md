# Chapter 20: The Bend in the Line

> "A prince learns to win. A king learns what winning costs." - Aurenmark nursery maxim

The war council met in a room with no windows, which Valerius had always thought was a mistake in a palace with so many good ones. The map table stood in the middle under three lamps, and the map of Verandel was pinned to it with brass nails at the corners. Coloured cords ran across it from the Wall to the coast, and little paper flags marked the towns that had sent letters that week. Some of the flags were red and some were blue, and one town near the bend had three flags stuck into the same inch of paper, which meant nobody had decided which report to believe.

The king's chair at the head of the table was empty, and a clerk had put a cup of water beside it that nobody had touched. Valerius had seen his father drink from that cup at six councils in a row, and he had never once seen the king read the papers in front of him at the same time as somebody spoke.

Valerius sat at the west side because that was where his father had sat when he was learning, and nobody had moved his chair since. Count Merrow sat opposite with a cup of tea in both hands. He was a narrow man in a brown coat with grey hair cut close to his head, and he had first put a map in Valerius's hands when the prince was ten. He had been kind then, and he was kind now, which was why Valerius let him say things that he would not have accepted from another minister.

"The southern line holds," the War Minister said. "The Wall has had three probes in a week and lost no ground."

"Three probes near the same stretch," Valerius said.

"Huskborn go where the snow is thin."

"Then the snow is thin in one place."

A minister at the far end of the table said something about supply, and the man beside him said something about the Concord vote, and neither of them looked at the map. Two younger ministers near the door spoke quietly to each other. Valerius heard one word from them, twice, and the word was Host.

He looked up. "What host?"

The room went the way rooms go when a subject has been discussed before the meeting began. The War Minister cleared his throat. The minister with the supply papers suddenly found a number in them that needed his whole attention.

"Nothing, sir," the youngest of them said. "A rumour from the Wall."

"What rumour?"

"That there is a hidden host behind the line." The young minister said it quickly, as if speed made it less important. "Men who do not appear on any roll. They say the ground moves at night in one sector."

"A hidden host of our own?"

"No, sir. Not ours."

Valerius looked at Merrow. Merrow took a sip of tea and looked at the map, and the silence lasted long enough to be an answer of its own.

"Write me a paper on it," Valerius said. "With the names of the men who say it, and the sector."

"Yes, sir."

"Not a summary. A paper."

The clerk at the end of the table bent his head and wrote, and his pen scratched loudly in the quiet. Valerius watched the young minister's ears go red. He had not meant to frighten the boy. He had meant only to see, for once, what it looked like when someone in this room was asked for a fact instead of a comfort, and he found that it looked a good deal like fear.

The young minister wrote it down. Valerius went back to the map.

* * *

The cord that marked the front ran north to south and then, past Verandel, bent east for three inches before it went north again. Valerius had looked at that bend for four years. Nobody had ever explained it to him. The manuals said the line followed the high ground, and the bend crossed a low valley where the river ran, which meant the manuals were wrong or the valley was not as low as it looked.

"What is here?" he asked, and put one finger on the bend.

The War Minister leaned over. "That is the Verandel bend."

"I can see that it is a bend. Why is it there?"

"The ground."

"The ground is flat there. The river runs through it."

"Then the river." The War Minister straightened. He looked relieved to have found a word that cost him nothing, and he repeated it under his breath as if he meant to keep it. "It was drawn in the last war. The order came from the palace, and the maps were changed."

"Who signed it?"

"I cannot say, sir."

"Can you say who carried it?"

"I carried a message." The War Minister put two fingers on the map and then took them away. "I did not open it."

"Convenient."

"It was the war, sir. A great many people did not open things."

"Which order?"

"I was a captain then, sir. I carried a message, not a decision."

Valerius kept his finger on the bend. He could have asked the king, but the king had been in the room when the order was given, and Valerius had watched his father's face that morning in the small room with the cold table. Theodric had been kind and tired, and he had lifted two fingers toward a shoulder and stopped before they landed. Valerius had known even then that the kindness was real and the answer would not be.

"My father looks tired," he said, to nobody in particular.

Merrow set his cup down. "He carries a war."

"Other kings have carried wars."

"Yes." Merrow's voice was mild enough that the other ministers kept talking. "But he carries this one, and he was not made for it. His father was. That is a hard thing to be the son of."

Valerius heard the words as he always heard Merrow's words, with the surface first and the other meaning underneath it a beat later. Merrow never said a thing straight when a gentle shape would do. Valerius had once asked him why, at fifteen, and Merrow had said that straight words broke more teeth than gentle ones, and he had been right.

"He cannot even look at a map without apologising for it," Valerius said.

"He looks at you the same way."

That one landed without any softness at all, and Valerius's hand came off the map. Merrow had poured more tea and was watching the steam, as if he had not spoken.

"Do not be angry with me," the count said. "You asked me to tell you what I saw when you asked me to teach you. I saw a boy who could not win against his father. Then I saw a man who could win against anyone in this year, and who still fights as if he is afraid of being found out."

Valerius said nothing. The duel was ten days old, and the touch had come on the bell, and he had known at once that Eden had timed it. He had known and he had not said so, because saying it would make it real, and because a prince who could not make a boy try was worse than a prince who lost.

"He held back," he said.

"Yes."

"My father knows who he is."

"Yes."

"So do I." Valerius looked at the bend again. "He will not say it, and I cannot make him, and everyone in this school pretends there is nothing to say."

"Everyone except Hollen."

"Hollen writes everything down." Valerius's mouth moved. "She has written a question about him twice and crossed it out both times. I know because she does not hide the book very well."

Merrow smiled at his tea. "She is a good judge of a locked door."

Valerius noticed, as he sometimes did without wanting to, that Merrow's cup had not gone down. The tea had been poured when the meeting began and it was still full, and the steam had long since stopped. A man who held a cup in both hands for an hour and never drank from it was a man who needed something to hold. Valerius filed the thought away unfinished, the way he filed most things about Merrow, because finishing it would have meant deciding what it was.

"I am not trying to open a door."

"No? Then what are you doing, Prince Valerius?"

Valerius did not answer that. The clerk came round with a tray and took the cups away, and the young minister's new paper lay on the table upside down with the ink still wet. The three flags on the bend had not moved. Valerius reached out and, without asking, took one of the three paper flags out of the map and put it in his pocket. Nobody stopped him. It was a small thing, a scrap of blue paper on a pin, and he told himself it was only so that someone would have to explain the missing one. He knew it was also so that he would remember he had been in the room.

The other ministers were finishing their papers, and one of them asked whether the council would sit again in the new year, and the War Minister said yes, in the new year, as if that settled the bend and the rumour and the low valley all at once.

Merrow stood and gathered his papers with the care of a man who had taught a prince not to crease a map. He came round the table and put a hand on Valerius's sleeve for one moment, which was the most he ever allowed himself. Valerius was ten again, with a pencil too big for his hand and a map of the Wall spread flat on a desk in a room that smelled of the same tea. Merrow had shown him the bend that day and said that a line on a map was a promise someone had made, and that promises sometimes took a strange shape before they were kept. Valerius had asked who had made that promise, and Merrow had turned the page and taught him the names of the rivers instead.

"Do not spend the whole winter on this bend," he said. "It has waited four years. It can wait one more."

"Then what should I spend it on?"

Valerius thought of Eden on the bell, moving his hand a finger's width and letting the touch arrive with the sound. He thought of the six words at the stairs in November, and of the fact that the boy had never once told him anything he did not have to tell. He had hated that for five years, and he had not known until the duel that part of the hate was because Eden never needed him.

"The Ball," Merrow said, when the silence had gone on long enough. He did not smile, which made it sound more serious than the war. "You are the council president, and there will be four hundred people in a glass hall with a hundred lanterns and one band. Half of your year will be guests and the other half will be trying to impress them. If something goes wrong in that hall, it will be your name on the paper, not the War Minister's."

"The council does not guard the Ball."

"No. But the council writes the rules for the people who do." Merrow stepped back with his papers under his arm, and he asked the question as mildly as he had asked all the others, while he was already turning toward the door. "Which hall do the lanterns hang in, by the way? The glass atrium, I suppose. I have never been to one of your school dances."

"The glass atrium," Valerius said. He said it because Merrow had asked in the mild voice he used for maps and promises, and because the prince had answered that voice all his life. Outside the room an officer waited with a stack of papers and a face that wanted a decision, and Valerius stepped past him without taking them.

He did not think about the answer twice.
