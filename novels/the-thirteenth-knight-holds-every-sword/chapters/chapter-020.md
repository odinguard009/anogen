# Chapter 20: The Bend in the Line

> "A prince learns to win. A king learns what winning costs." - Aurenmark nursery maxim

The worst part of the wrist was not the pain. Valerius could still feel the exact place where the lance had touched him, a spot the size of a coin on the inside of his arm. It hummed faintly, like a bell that someone had struck and then held still.

Wren had hit him there on purpose. Valerius had gone over it forty times, the angle and the step and the turn, and no one learns that in a school yard. And then the fool had let himself lose by a finger's width and gone home to bleed in private, as if nothing had happened.

*My father knows who you are.* He had said that to Wren in the corridor, but it was not completely true. His father knew something. Once, at dinner, Valerius had said Wren's name, and his father had gone very still. Then he had said, "Eat your soup, Val."

Valerius had planned to ask his father today. Then he saw the war room and his father's face, and he did not ask.

* * *

Seraphine caught him on the tower stairs. She had come from somewhere in a hurry, and she smelled of burnt sugar.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you rub your wrist and glare at a wall. You did it through the whole duel. Does it hurt?"

"No."

"Liar. Did he hit you?"

"He did something." Valerius looked at his hand. "I don't know what. That's the problem."

"You always say that. Every time you fight Wren, you're upset that you didn't win, and then you're upset that you're upset." She made a face. "I told you. He's just a sleepy boy with a lance."

"No one who's that sleepy is that exact."

"Then he's an exact, sleepy boy." She patted his sleeve, and a little flour fell from her hand onto it. "Go and be a prince at your father. I'm going to find out who Lysette is taking to the Ball. She says she hasn't decided, and nobody hasn't decided."

"Why do you care?"

"Because it's interesting!"

She went off down the stairs, humming. Valerius brushed the flour from his sleeve and went up.

* * *

The war room was in the oldest tower of the palace, a round stone room with no windows and a smell of cold candle wax. A table as big as a wagon stood in the middle, and the Concord map lay across it, painted by hand. It showed the coasts and the gray lands across the sea. Red pins marked the Hush front, which is the black fog that eats land, and blue pins marked the coasts. One small gold pin stood in the city where they sat.

The ministers had gathered like crows in a field. There were nine of them, and Valerius was the tenth. He was the youngest, and the only one who had needed to be told where to stand.

King Theodric sat at the head of the table. He looked tired, but not the way kings are supposed to look tired, with big gestures. He looked like a man who had stayed up all night with a sick animal. His eyes were a little pink at the edges, and his hand lay on the map as if it held down a page in the wind.

"Sit down, son," he said.

Valerius sat. A servant put a warm cup in front of him and left before he could say he did not want it.

"Tell me," said the king to a thin man with a gray mustache. "The report from the south."

"The wall holds, my king." The thin man cleared his throat. "Three nights ago a pack of them tried the east gate. We drove it off. We lost eleven. The Hush lost, we think, about forty."

"You think?"

"They leave very little to count."

"And the front?"

"It has not changed."

It was a perfectly ordinary answer, and Valerius watched it settle over the room like dust. Pens scratched. Someone coughed. Someone turned a page that did not need turning. Then, because he had not slept and because the gold pin was right in front of him, he looked at the map properly for the first time.

It took him a while to understand what he saw.

The front is a line along the edge of the dead lands. It starts at the great wall in the north, runs along the far shore of the sea and goes around the shoulder of the next continent. There is a red pin, then another red pin, then another, and the line should be rough and uneven, like a coast. It was.

Except here.

Around the southern coast of Verandel, the red pins curved inward, away from the water, for a stretch about as wide as his hand. Then they bent out again on the far side. It looked as if the front had been walking along, met something in the road and gone around it.

"Why does it bend?" said Valerius.

No one answered. It was the kind of quiet that happens when nine men decide at the same moment that they would rather read something.

"The line," said Valerius. "Around Verandel. It goes in."

"That is a drawing mistake, my prince," said Hartenfels, a round, cheerful man with ink on his cuffs. "The mapmakers sometimes smooth the pins."

"Is that true?"

"It is as true as the reports, my prince."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is what I can answer."

Valerius looked at him, and Hartenfels smiled. It is hard to be angry at a man who is both honest and useless. So Valerius turned to the mapmaker instead, a small woman in the corner who held a rolled chart like a shield.

"Is the bend a mistake?"

"No, my prince," she said, and then she looked as if she wished she had not.

The room moved a little, a shoulder here and a boot there. Valerius knew the sound of a room that decides not to speak, because he had grown up inside one.

"It's in the field reports," said the mapmaker, in a very small voice. "It comes from fourteen watch posts, and we checked twice. The Hush throws itself at the wall. Everywhere else it creeps forward a little each year. But it has never crossed the sea to our southern coast, not once in nineteen years. No one can say why."

Valerius put his finger on the first red pin where the line began to curve and followed it slowly to the last. He had come in thinking the bend was a mistake. All his life he had expected mistakes to be small and stupid, and now he saw that this one was neither. It was the neatest part of the whole map.

"Whatever pushes them away," he said, "it has done it longer than I have been alive."

"By one year," said Hartenfels helpfully. "You are eighteen."

"Thank you. That helps a lot."

"Some of us," said an old minister quietly, to his hands, "remember what marched nineteen years ago."

"Careful," said the king, in a very pleasant voice.

"I only meant—"

"I know what you meant."

And there it was, the thing the room had been holding back. Valerius heard it rise from the ministers' chairs one word at a time, in the half whisper of a rumor.

*The Void Host.*

Everyone knows the story. Nine legions, a million knights in black armor with white thread for faces, marched once nineteen years ago and then were gone. The world has never stopped asking where. Half the Concord believes Verandel hides them, and the other half believes Verandel lost them. A third group is smaller and much more frightening. They believe Verandel does not know what they are.

Valerius felt the whole table turn toward him, very slowly. He could feel nine pairs of eyes on his collar. He was the heir, so he was the one who should know.

He did not know. That was the trouble.

His heart beat hard enough to hear. He sat very still with his hands flat on his knees and put on the face he had practiced in a mirror at nine years old, calm and polite and a little bored. A prince does not flinch in a room full of men who wait for him to flinch.

"There is no Void Host in this room," said Valerius. "I would have noticed."

A nervous laugh went around, and Hartenfels smiled again.

The king said nothing. His eyes had gone far away, to some place behind the map. Valerius looked at him and thought: *He knows. He knows why it bends.* Then he had a cold, small feeling, like a coin dropped down a well. *And he is afraid of it.*

* * *

"A word, my boy."

It was the count, Lucan Merrow, and he said it as warmly as a man who holds a door open.

They stood in the cold stone hall after the others had gone. The count was sixty years old, with silver hair and a limp from some old war, and he had a way of looking at you as if you were the only person in the room. He had taught Valerius to ride a horse and to read a treaty. He had sat with him through the worst night of his childhood and had not said one useless word.

"You've had a hard week," said the count. "I heard about the duel."

"It was a loss. For Wren."

"For Wren." Merrow smiled a little. "A boy who loses by that much knows exactly how far he is from winning. I would watch him."

"I mean to."

"Of course. But, Valerius." The old man put a hand on his shoulder. "I watched you in there. You saw the bend. Good. Most of that room has looked at it for years and called it ink. But be careful what you do with the things you see. A king does not chase every shadow. He chooses which ones to follow."

"It is not a shadow. It is three hundred miles of coast."

"It is a map." Merrow's voice stayed soft. "Your father is tired, and the Concord is looking for a reason to fight. In six days you have a ball. Five hundred young people will be in a glass hall, and half the foreign guests will watch. Which of those will get worse if you start asking about the Void Host?"

Valerius opened his mouth and closed it again.

It was a fair question. That was what bothered him. It was always fair.

"Worry about the Ball instead," said Merrow, and patted his arm. "Guards. Who is invited. Who carries what. It is a small thing, but small things are what a prince can really do. The big ones can wait."

"I suppose."

"Good lad." The count started down the hall, then stopped and turned halfway, as if a thought had just come to him. "Oh. One thing. It is only my own interest. Which hall will the lanterns hang in this year?"

"The glass atrium, the big front hall. The same as always."

"Ah," said Merrow gently. "Of course. The atrium."

He walked away down the hall with his small limp, in no hurry at all.

Valerius stood alone for a while. His wrist still hummed. Somewhere below, a door shut, and when the sound died he understood something. He had told the count nothing that was not in the Ball's own notice.

He could not think why that made him cold.
