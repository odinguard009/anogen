# Chapter 20: The Council

The palace courtyard was wet with morning rain. The guards at the inner door checked Valerius's name against a list before they let him pass. A page led him along a corridor of brown wood and tall windows. The thick carpet hid the sound of his boots. The war council room was on the ground floor of the palace. Its long table had been polished until the maps looked like water on it. Valerius came in at nine with his gloves on and his blade left outside. He took the chair that had been his father's before him.

He had spent the night after the match thinking about a boy who would not strike first. The boy had not explained why, and no answer fit the facts. Eden Wren was rank eight, slept on a roof, and scored highest in his year. He moved like a man taught to struggle before he learned to read. Valerius had checked the academy records and found a paid school place paid by the palace and a family page that was empty. He had written those two things on a sheet of paper and locked it in his desk. Writing them down made them real. His father had used this chair for eleven years and had never looked at a map without asking who had paid for the string. Valerius had asked that question in the morning. The clerk said the string came from the stores man, who took orders from the king's secretary. That was a chain of hands, and no name at the end of it.

The match had not cleared the matter. A boy who waited for a bell on another man's touch was either a coward or a man keeping a larger appointment. Valerius had watched him stand still under a blade twice, and it was not cowardice. He hated the hiding, and he hated more that nobody else in the school seemed to notice it. A clerk put a cup of tea beside his elbow and took it away again when the king's secretary asked for silence. Valerius had spoken to the steward on and in. The steward walked beside him with a list in his hand.

"How many doors?" Valerius asked.

"Four, and two of them are double."

"And the lanterns?"

"I will send the plan to your rooms, sir." A footman called the steward away just then, and the plan had not come.

Count Merrow sat two places down with a paper knife in his hand. He smiled across the table and they had met at a dance. He was a small man with a kind face and careful sleeves. Every minister in the room turned to him when he spoke. They did not turn that way for the king, and Valerius had noticed it before. Merrow never raised his voice, and he never asked a direct question. He said a thing twice, and the second saying sounded like an answer someone else had given.

"Valerius," Merrow said. "You look better than the last time I saw you. The academy agrees with you."

"It gives me a great deal of exercise," Valerius said.

"Then it is doing its job." Merrow turned a page of his notes. "We are all grateful that you could come today. The map needs a soldier's eye."

The map lay in the middle of the table, and it was newer than the one in the king's study. A line of red string ran across the southern border and turned north at the forest of Verandel. Then it bent west in a long curve that ended nowhere. Three brass pins held the bend in place, and each pin had a number written on a slip of paper beside it.

A minister with a grey beard tapped the bend with his finger. "That curve is new. Two weeks ago the line ran straight through the lower valley."

"The reports say the front stalled," another minister said.

"Stalled means we do not know where the enemy is," the grey-bearded minister said. "The string bends, and the garrison redraws it, and we are given a number. I want the number with a name attached."

"The adjutant's name is attached," the king's secretary said. "It is the same adjutant who sent the last six."

"Then ask the adjutant why he moved the line." Valerius's voice went across the table, and the ministers stopped talking. "A line on a map is a claim. Somebody made that claim, and somebody signed it."

"Stalled is a word we use when we do not know what happened." The grey-bearded man looked at Valerius. "Perhaps the captain of the Wall can tell us."

"The Wall captains send reports to the garrison, not to me," Valerius said. "I can tell you where the line was drawn, and not why it was drawn there." He looked at the bend. "The string turns round Verandel, but nothing on this map explains why the forest matters more than the valley."

"Veil activity," someone said from the far end.

"Grey in the water," said another.

"Grey in the water is a Husk in the water," said the minister at the far end. "That is not a front."

"It is when the men who fish the river are gone," the grey-bearded man said.

The whispers went around the table in a low wave, and Valerius let them run. He had learned that ministers repeated a thing until it sounded like evidence, and then they wrote it down. One of the whispers reached him clearly: "the Void Host". Another minister said the words again with his hand over his mouth. The man beside him looked toward the king to see if they had been heard.

King Theodric sat at the head of the table with a folded paper in front of him, and he had not touched his tea. His face was thinner than it had been at the last audience, and the skin under his eyes was grey. The phrase came from a clerk in the adjutant's office, written in the margin of a supply bill. Now three ministers were saying it and they had seen the thing. Valerius had first heard it in a tavern near the Wall, where a soldier said the grey could not be killed and that something older was moving under the ice. He listened to the whispers with his head tilted, and then he asked for the ledger. A clerk brought him a book with a blue spine.

"Three hundred and eleven men lost in the last rotation," the king said, and he read from the book without raising his voice. "The adjutant wrote that number himself, and he does not write numbers twice." He shut the book. "Somebody will tell me what the bend around Verandel means, or somebody will tell me why I must wait."

The table went quiet. Valerius looked at the map and at the king's hands. The king's right hand held the folded paper too tight at one corner. He had known the king all his life, and he had never seen him hold a paper like that.

Count Merrow put down his paper knife. "Your Majesty, the bend is troubling, but it is not a puzzle we can solve in this room. The Wall has held for five hundred years on reports that were not perfect. I suggest we give the garrison a fortnight and ask for a fresh survey."

"A fortnight," the king said.

"A fortnight is a small price for an honest answer." Merrow smoothed his sleeve. "And while we wait, there is the Ball. The Duke Argentine arrives on the eleventh, and the foreign delegations on the twelfth, and every one of them will ask about the Wall at supper. I would rather have a security plan than a guess about a curve."

The king looked at the map for a while, and then he looked at Merrow, and then at Valerius. "You are the senior matchlist of the year, and you will be in the hall with the rest of us. Tell me what you would guard."

"openings, windows, and the food," Valerius said. "In that order."

"Good." The king sat back. "Then speak with the palace steward, and take one older student with you. The council will reconvene after the Ball."

The ministers folded their papers and stood, and the room filled with the sound of chairs. Valerius stayed with his hand on the map edge, and he looked at the bend around Verandel again, because it still had no explanation on it.

Merrow came and stood beside him. He moved quietly, and he did not touch the map. "You are thinking about the bend."

"I am thinking about the number of men who died at it."

"Three hundred and eleven." Merrow said it as if he were sorry. "That is a great many sons and brothers, and the king feels every one. He has begun to read the ledger twice."

"He read it once today."

Merrow looked at the king for a while, and then at the table, and his face kept its kind shape. "Once is enough for today," he said. "Reading it twice would not add a name."

"No," Valerius said. "It would only tell us that he had read it."

"Then he will read it twice tomorrow." Merrow watched the king leave the room with his secretary. The king's shoulders were bent under the weight of his coat. "We must be patient with him."

Valerius turned from the map. "You said the same thing in March, about the Veil in the lower city."

"And I was right. The Veil was ended."

"By the Thirteenth Vigil." Valerius kept his voice even. "Not by the council, and not by patience."

Merrow's smile did not move. "The Vigil does its work quietly, and it is better that way. The world needs a few doors that open without a committee watching." He picked up his paper knife and set it down again. "You dislike quiet."

"I dislike a room where the answers arrive before the questions."

"Then you will dislike the Ball as well." Merrow walked toward the door, and he stopped with his hand on the frame and looked back over his shoulder. "It is a kinder room. There is music, and the lanterns are beautiful, and nobody asks about the Wall unless the king asks first."

Valerius followed him into the corridor, and the doors of the council room shut behind them with a soft sound.

"You have never asked me about a hall before," Valerius said, and he stopped walking. A footman passed them with a tray of clean cups and went back into the council room. "You asked me about the Wall in the spring, and about the winter convoy in October."

"Those were questions about a soldier," Merrow said. "This is a question about a party."

"You do not ask about parties."

"No," Merrow said, and he looked delighted. "But I am learning."

Merrow walked on, and Valerius stayed where he was for a moment with his gloves in his hand. He had asked the steward about the halls twice, and the steward had not answered the second question. Now Merrow had asked the same thing in a soft voice, in a corridor with no witnesses. There was no reason for it, and that was the reason he would remember it.

Merrow waited with his hands folded in front of him, and he had the patient look of a man who had asked a question he already knew the answer to. Valerius had seen that look on his father once, on the night a treaty was signed in this same palace.

"There is something else I wanted to ask you," Merrow said, and he said it mildly, as if it were about the weather. "The Duke Argentine will be at the Ball, and the king will want the hall watched properly. Which hall do the lanterns hang in?"
