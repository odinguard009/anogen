# Chapter 20: The Bend in the Line

> "A prince learns to win. A king learns what winning costs." - Aurenmark nursery maxim

The war council met in a room with no windows, which Valerius had always thought was a mistake in a palace with so many good ones. The map table stood in the middle under three lamps, and the map of Verandel was pinned to it with brass nails at the corners. Coloured cords ran across it from the Wall to the coast, and little paper flags marked the towns that had sent letters that week. Some of the flags were red and some were blue, and one town near the bend had three flags stuck into the same inch of paper, which meant nobody had decided which report to believe.

The king's chair at the head of the table was empty, and a clerk had put a cup of water beside it that nobody had touched. Valerius had seen his father sit at that table for years. The king always listened with his eyes on the cup, and he never once read the papers in front of him.

Valerius sat at the west side because that was where his father had sat when he was learning, and nobody had moved his chair since. Count Merrow sat opposite with a cup of tea in both hands. He was a narrow man in a brown coat with grey hair cut close to his head, and he had first put a map in Valerius's hands when the prince was ten. He had been kind then, and he was kind now, which was why Valerius let him say things that he would not have accepted from another minister.

"The southern line holds," the War Minister said. "The Wall has had three probes in a week and lost no ground."

"Three probes near the same stretch," Valerius said.

"The Hushborn attack where the snow is thin."

"Then the snow is thin in one place, and they know it."

A minister at the far end of the table said something about supply, and the man beside him said something about the Concord vote, and neither of them looked at the map. Two younger ministers near the door spoke quietly to each other. Valerius heard one word from them, twice. The word was "Host".

He looked up. "What host?"

The room went quiet. The War Minister cleared his throat. The minister with the supply papers found a number that needed all his attention.

"Nothing, sir," the youngest of them said. "A rumour from the Wall."

"What rumour?"

"That a hidden army stands behind the line." The young minister said it quickly, as if speed made it less important. "Men who are on no list. Soldiers say the ground moves at night in one place."

"An army of ours?"

"No, sir. Not ours. No one knows whose."

Valerius looked at Merrow. Merrow took a sip of tea and looked at the map, and the silence lasted long enough to be an answer of its own.

"Write me a paper on it," Valerius said. "With the names of the men who say it, and the sector."

"Yes, sir."

"Not a summary. A paper."

The clerk at the end of the table bent his head and wrote, and his pen scratched loudly in the quiet. Valerius watched the young minister's ears go red. He had not meant to frighten the boy. He had meant only to see, for once, what it looked like when someone in this room was asked for a fact instead of a comfort, and he found that it looked a good deal like fear.

The young minister wrote it down. Valerius went back to the map.

* * *

The cord that marked the front ran north to south and then, past Verandel, bent east for three inches before it went north again. Valerius had looked at that bend for four years. No one had ever explained it to him. The manuals said the line followed the high ground, and the bend crossed a low valley where the river ran, which meant the manuals were wrong or the valley was not as low as it looked.

"What is here?" he asked, and put one finger on the bend.

The War Minister leaned over. "That is the Verandel bend."

"I can see that it is a bend. Why is it there?"

"The ground."

"The ground is flat there, and the river runs through it. A good line would not bend there."

"It was drawn in the last war." The War Minister straightened. "The order came from the palace, and the maps were changed."

"Who signed that order?"

"I cannot say, sir. I was a captain then. I carried a message, not a decision. I did not open it."

"A great many people did not open things in that war?"

"Yes, sir. A great many."

Valerius kept his finger on the bend. He could have asked the king. But he had seen his father that morning, in the small room with the cold table. The king had been kind and tired. He had lifted two fingers toward his son's shoulder and stopped before they landed. Valerius knew that the kindness was real and that an answer would not come.

"My father looks tired," he said, to nobody in particular.

Merrow set his cup down. "He carries a war."

"Other kings have carried wars."

"Yes." Merrow's voice was mild enough that the other ministers kept talking. "But he carries this one, and he was not made for it. His father was. That is a hard thing to be the son of."

Merrow never said a thing straight when a gentle way would do. Valerius had asked him why, years ago. Merrow had said that straight words broke more teeth than gentle ones.

"He cannot even look at a map without apologising for it," Valerius said.

"He looks at you the same way."

That was not gentle at all, and Valerius took his hand off the map. Merrow had poured more tea and was watching the steam, as if he had not spoken.

"Do not be angry with me," the count said. "You asked me long ago to tell you what I see. I see a young man who can beat anyone in this year, and who still fights as if he is afraid of being found out."

Valerius said nothing. The duel was ten days old. The touch had come on the bell, and he had known at once that Eden had timed it. He had not said so, because saying it would make it real.

"He held back," he said.

"Yes."

"My father knows who he is. So do I." Valerius looked at the bend again. "He will not say it, and I cannot make him, and everyone in this school pretends there is nothing to say."

"Everyone except Lysette Hollen."

"She writes everything down." Valerius's mouth moved. "She has written a question about him twice and crossed it out both times. She does not hide her book very well."

Merrow smiled at his tea. Valerius noticed that the cup had not gone down. The tea had been poured when the meeting began and it was still full. A man who held a cup for an hour and never drank from it needed something to hold. Valerius put the thought away unfinished, as he did with most things about Merrow. Finishing it would have meant deciding what it was.

"I am not trying to open anything."

"No? Then what are you doing, Prince Valerius?"

Valerius did not answer. The clerk came round with a tray and took the cups away, and the young minister's new paper lay on the table upside down with the ink still wet. The three flags on the bend had not moved. Valerius reached out and, without asking, took one of the three paper flags out of the map and put it in his pocket. No one stopped him. It was a scrap of blue paper on a pin. He told himself it was so that someone would have to explain the missing flag. He knew it was also so that he would remember he had been in the room.

The door at the end of the room opened, and the king came in. The ministers stood. He did not sit. He rested one hand on the back of his empty chair and looked at the map in silence, and Valerius saw how tired his eyes were. They were red at the rims, and the skin under them was dark.

"Continue," the king said. He looked at his son, and for a moment he seemed about to say more. Then he turned and went out the way he had come, and the room let out its breath.

The other ministers were finishing their papers, and one of them asked whether the council would sit again in the new year, and the War Minister said yes, in the new year, as if that settled the bend and the rumour and the low valley all at once.

Merrow stood and gathered his papers with the care of a man who had taught a prince not to crease a map. He came round the table and put a hand on Valerius's sleeve for one moment, which was the most he ever allowed himself. Valerius was ten again, with a pencil too big for his hand and a map of the Wall spread flat on a desk in a room that smelled of the same tea. Merrow had shown him the bend that day. He had said a line on a map was a promise someone had made. Valerius had asked who made it, and Merrow had turned the page and taught him the names of the rivers instead.

"Do not spend the whole winter on this bend," he said. "It has waited four years. It can wait one more."

"Then what should I spend it on?"

Valerius thought of Eden on the bell, moving his hand a finger's width and letting the touch arrive with the sound. The boy had never once told him anything he did not have to tell. Valerius had hated that for five years. Only after the duel had he understood why. Eden never needed him.

"The Ball," Merrow said, when the silence had gone on long enough. He did not smile, which made it sound more serious than the war. "You are the council president, and there will be four hundred people in a glass hall with a hundred lanterns and one band. Half of your year will be guests and the other half will be trying to impress them. If something goes wrong in that hall, it will be your name on the paper, not the War Minister's."

"The council does not guard the Ball," Valerius said. "The school guards and the city watch guard it."

"No. But the council writes the rules for the people who do. It says how many guards stand at each door, who checks the guest list, and who may carry a blade. A prince who knows the doors of a hall knows the hall."

"I have read the rules."

"Then you know them better than I do." Merrow stepped back with his papers under his arm. He asked the next question as mildly as he had asked all the others, already turning toward the door. "Which hall do the lanterns hang in, by the way? The glass atrium, I suppose. I have never been to one of your school dances."

"The glass atrium," Valerius said. He said it because Merrow had asked in the mild voice he used for maps and promises, and because the prince had answered that voice all his life. Outside the room an officer waited with a stack of papers and a face that wanted a decision, and Valerius stepped past him without taking them.

He did not think about the answer twice. It was only a hall, and everyone in the school knew its name, so the answer gave nothing away.
