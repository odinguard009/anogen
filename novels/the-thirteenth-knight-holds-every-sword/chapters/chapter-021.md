# Chapter 21: The Locket

Invitation week began with a pile of paper on the Committee bench. Prefects walked the halls with bundles of cream envelopes held flat against their coats. Each envelope was an invitation to the Ball, the school dance on Saturday. By the second bell the whole year knew that Seraphine had invited someone at the south tables. Three people asked Felix who it was, and Felix gave each of them a different wrong name.

Lysette stamped every envelope with the Committee bell and wrote the giver's name on the back. The rule said that the answer had to be written on the same paper as the question. There was a box by the north door for answers that nobody wanted to hand over in person. Eden had never put anything in the box, and he did not start now.

On his way across the court he passed the glass atrium, where a crew of workmen on ladders were hanging paper lanterns from iron hooks in the roof. The atrium was a long hall of green glass panes and black iron ribs, built a century ago by a headmaster who had liked to watch the rain from inside. Four hundred people would stand under that roof on Saturday. Eden counted the doors out of habit. There were three on the court side and one at the far end behind a stack of chairs. Then he counted the ladders, because a man on a ladder is not looking at the floor.

A foreman stood below with a list, calling out the hooks. Each lantern had a number, and each number matched a hook. Eden stopped at the glass wall for a moment. If something went wrong here on Saturday, four hundred people would have to leave through four doors, and one of those doors was blocked by chairs. He did not move the chairs. A boy who moved chairs in a ball hall would be asked why. He only noted the door, the way he noted all doors, and walked on.

Seraphine stood at the south table with an envelope in her hand. The envelope was cream and had a silver seal on the back, and she held it at an angle so that Felix could see the seal but Eden could not.

"I am asking you to the Ball," she said. "As a joke, mostly."

"Mostly," Eden said.

"A small part of me means it." She put the envelope on the table by his cup. "You may keep the envelope even if you say no. It has a good seal."

Felix looked at the seal. "Is that a trick?"

"No. It is a seal." Seraphine sat down. "He will say no."

"Then why ask?" Felix said.

"To see if he says no quickly or slowly."

Felix put his book flat on the table. He did that for fires and for questions he did not want to answer. "I would like it noted that I am not part of this."

"You are the audience," Seraphine said.

"I did not buy a ticket."

"Nobody buys a ticket for a joke. They only stay to see if it is funny." She looked at Felix for a moment longer than the joke needed, then looked away. Eden saw it. Felix, who was turning the pages of an open book, did not.

Eden picked up the envelope and turned it over. The seal showed a broken crown pressed into wax, and the wax had cracked at one point. "No," he said.

"Quickly," Seraphine said, to no one. "Thank you." She took the envelope back, then left it on the table. "Keep it. The seal is still good."

Lysette came past with a form in her hand and stopped beside the table. She looked at Seraphine's collar, where the silver thread had slipped half an inch out of line, and her pencil moved before she had decided to move it.

"Your collar," she said.

"It is a dance collar," Seraphine said. "It is meant to be crooked."

"The rule says the silver thread must lie straight. Yours is out by two threads." Lysette wrote on the form. "I am writing you up."

"You have written me up eleven times this term."

"Twelve now." Lysette walked away with her book half open.

Felix watched her go. "She is angry."

"She is busy," Seraphine said. "Those are close."

Eden said nothing. Felix looked at him. "You watched her collar for a long time."

"One second."

"Four," Felix said, and shut his book. "I counted." He opened it again at once, and Eden saw that it was upside down.

* * *

Eden went up to the roof garden after the last bell with the cream envelope in his coat and the Ashen card in the same pocket. The evening had gone cold enough that the stone of the herb beds kept the last of the sun, and the broken telescope had a rim of frost on its iron stand. The dwarf apple tree had two green apples now, both hard and small, and the bird nest in the telescope had blown down and lay in three pieces on the grass. Lysette sat on the bench by the herb bed with her gloves off and her black book on her knee. She had been crying, and she had stopped only a moment ago. Her eyes were still red at the edges. Her book lay open on a blank page with one line at the top and nothing under it.

"Did Seraphine ask you to the Ball?" she said.

"Yes."

"What did you say?"

"No."

"That is the right answer." She picked a dead leaf off the thyme and put it in her pocket. "She asked me too. As a joke, mostly."

"Are you going?"

"I have not decided." Lysette put her book down. "I have no partner. Rowan asked Clara a month ago, and Clara said yes, and the whole year knows. I do not want to stand at the wall and watch them dance."

"You could stay here."

"Then I would hear the band and wonder what the room looks like." She wiped her nose with the back of her hand and reached for her gloves, then stopped. "I have no partner. That is the whole trouble. I have said it four times and it has not got smaller."

Eden said nothing. He sat on the stone edge of the herb bed, which was where she had sat when she found him asleep, and he did not take the envelope out of his pocket.

"Can you dance?" she asked, after a while, in the flat voice she used for forms.

"No."

"Not at all?"

"I know where the feet go. I have never put them there in time."

"That is the same as not at all." She almost smiled, then looked at the dead leaves in her palm instead. "I can. I was taught by a woman with a ruler. I am very correct and very bad. It is a sad combination."

Lysette's hand went to the collar of her coat and came back with a silver locket on a thin chain. It was a small oval, no bigger than a coin, with a smooth edge and a hairline scratch across the front. It was shut. She wore it under her collar on one day of the year, and the chain had a link that had been mended with a smaller ring.

"Today a clerk said that the Thirteenth Vigil had been seen near the exchange," she said. "I closed my hand over this before I knew I was doing it."

"Why?"

"I do not know. It has done that since my brother came home from court." She held it flat in her palm and did not open it. "He could not dance."

Eden waited.

"He had been away for two years. He came home in the autumn and he walked into the hall and everyone was glad, and he stood on the edge of the floor and watched. The band played four tunes that night, and the second one was slow enough for a court dance. My mother had made him a coat with a high collar, and he kept pulling at it because it was new." Her thumb moved over the scratch on the locket. "Someone asked him to dance, and he said no. He said he had forgotten how. But he had been a good dancer. Everyone knew it." She stopped and took a breath that did not help. "After that he stopped coming to the hall at all. He ate in his room and walked in the court with his collar up. My mother wrote that House Hollen would act as if it had not happened. I was fourteen. I thought she meant the dancing."

Eden did not say that it had not been the dancing. He had honest answers ready in his mouth, and every one of them opened a door he had kept shut for five years. He had no useful sentence, so he stayed where he was. Below the roof, the school band practised one slow tune with the windows closed. He did not leave until she had finished speaking, and then he stayed a little longer.

"My mother says we keep this for the day we can open it," Lysette said. "She takes it out once a year and I take it out once a year, and neither of us opens it. I have decided that is worse than looking."

She put the locket back under her collar. The chain caught on a button, and she turned it twice before it lay flat. Eden watched the oval go out of sight. He noted the mended link, which was smaller than the others and brighter where someone had worked it with a tool.

"I did not ask what was in your envelope," she said. "I wanted to see if you would tell me. You did not, and I did not ask twice. I am learning to do that." She picked up her book and stood. She walked to the door and stopped with her hand on the hinge. "I am going to the Ball."

"Why?"

"To prove I do not care." She said it into the door, not back at him. "That is a bad reason. Do not write it down."

"I will not."

She opened the door and went down one step, then came back and put her hand on the frame. "If I go, I will see Rowan dancing with Clara. I will stand near the wall and I will not cry. In the morning I will be glad I went. That is what I tell myself." Her hand left the frame. "You may come too. As a joke, mostly."

Then she was gone down the stairs, and the band below got one bar further before it stopped and started again from the top. Eden took out the cream envelope and read the seal once more in the last light: a broken crown cracked at one point, pressed into wax that had gone soft at the edges from being held too long. He put it in his coat and did not use it as a bookmark. The Ashen card was already doing that job.

On his way down he passed the glass atrium again. The workmen had gone home, and the lanterns hung unlit from their hooks, a hundred paper moons in the dusk. Eden stood at the doorway and looked at the floor where four hundred people would dance. He thought of Lysette in a correct dress, standing near the wall. He thought of Rowan and Clara in the middle of the floor. He thought of a list on a brass clip in Bell Lane, with Rowan's name circled twice, and of two words in a stranger's hand: alive, night of lights.

The Ball was in five days. He did not want to like it. He found that he was already counting the doors again.
