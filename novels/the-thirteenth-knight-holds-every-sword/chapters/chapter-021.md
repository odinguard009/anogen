# Chapter 21: The Crooked Collar

"Say it again, but slower," said Felix.

"It's the same words, Felix," said Seraphine. "Slower won't change them."

We stood on the landing of the Long Stair in the early morning. Few students were about yet, and the stone was so cold that I felt it through my boots. It was five days before the Lantern Ball. Down in the yard, workers hung banners, and a first-year on a ladder was losing a fight with a lantern hook.

Seraphine had run up the steps two at a time. She stopped in front of me with her chin high and her hands behind her back, like a girl about to read a poem.

"Eden Wren," she said. "Will you come to the Ball with me? As a joke. Mostly."

"Which part is not the joke?" I said.

"The part where you say yes."

I looked at Felix, who studied the stair rail as if it had said something rude about his family.

"I'll say no," I said.

"You haven't heard the offer!"

"I heard it, and you said it twice."

"There's a second half. I get you a seat by the window and a plate of the little smoked things. I stand next to you, so nobody else asks you to dance." She counted on her fingers. "That's a very good offer, and it's almost a rescue."

"I'll be on the roof."

"On the roof?"

"I like the roof."

"It's a ball, Eden, not an exam. You can't skip it and say you were studying."

"I wasn't going to say that."

"Then what were you going to say?"

"Nothing, because I'd just be on the roof."

Seraphine turned slowly to Felix. "Did you tell him?"

"Tell him what?"

"That I would ask."

"No."

"Someone did, because he isn't even surprised."

"I'm surprised," I said. "I just have a flat face."

Felix gave me a long, flat look, and I gave him one back. In four years he had never told my secrets to anyone, and I was fairly sure this wasn't the start. But checking a thing is easier than trusting it, and checking cost me nothing.

He raised both hands a little. "I told her yesterday that you would say no," he said. "That's all I said."

"He said it with pity," said Seraphine.

"I didn't."

"You sighed."

"I don't sigh," said Felix. Then he sighed, and it sounded like a book hitting the floor.

Somewhere below us there was another sound. I had heard it for a minute without knowing what it was. It was a thin hum, a few notes long. It went up when it should have gone down, and then the same notes came again, worse.

Rowan sat on the third step down with his back to us and his elbows on his knees. He was humming the waltz for the Ball, very quietly, like a man who does not want to wake a baby. His boot tapped the stone, one, two, three. On every fourth bar it stopped, and he said "ah" under his breath and began again.

I have seen Rowan lift a fallen beam and argue with a goat, but I had never seen him quiet.

"Rowan," said Seraphine.

His head came up, and his face went red from the collar up, slowly and evenly, the way a glass fills with wine.

"I wasn't doing anything!"

"You were humming."

"That's not doing something."

"It was the waltz," said Felix. "In the second half you skip the turn."

"I know I skip the turn!" Rowan dropped his voice and looked down the stairs. "I asked Clara. Yesterday, after kitchen duty. She said yes before I finished the question, so I think she was being kind. Now I have five days to learn to walk in a square."

"You don't have to be good," said Seraphine. "You only have to be loud and brave."

"That's what scares me."

Behind me, the stair creaked.

* * *

I knew the steps before I turned, because they were quick and even and a little sharp on the heel. Lysette had come up from the yard in her Committee coat, buttoned to the throat, with a small black notebook in her left hand.

She had heard us. I could see it on her face, which was careful and blank, like a door locked from inside. I didn't know how much she had heard, but it was enough.

"Good morning," she said.

"Morning!" said Rowan, much too loudly. "We weren't doing anything."

"I know, and I'm not here for you." Her eyes landed on Seraphine and stayed there. "Princess. Your collar."

Seraphine touched her neck. "What's wrong with my collar?"

"It's crooked."

"It's..." Seraphine looked down. The left point of her collar sat a finger higher than the right. But you had to stand in one exact spot, with the light behind you, to see it. "That's nothing."

"It breaks school rule fourteen. Collars must lie flat and level." Lysette had already opened the notebook, and her pencil was moving. "Name, year, offense."

"You know my name."

"For the record."

"You've never written me up!"

"I've never seen it crooked."

"You walked past me yesterday with flour in my hair!"

"That was a kitchen, not a hall." The pencil stopped, then started again, a little harder. "I need your signature."

Seraphine took the notebook and read the line. Then she looked up at Lysette, and her face was pure joy. She looked like a child who has learned that the thing under the bed is real.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, this is wonderful."

"Please sign it."

"I will. I'll put it in a frame."

"You will not put it in a frame."

"I'll hang it in the Ball hall, next to the lanterns."

"Sign it, Seraphine."

It was the first time I had heard Lysette use her name without the title. She seemed to notice it as it left her mouth. A tiny crack ran across her face, and she fixed it at once, but it had been there.

The pencil slipped from her fingers and rolled across the landing. Felix had waited all week for a job, so he picked it up and gave it back with a short bow.

"Thank you," said Lysette, without looking at him.

"You're welcome," said Felix. Then he said to Seraphine, "Sign it. She'll cry if you don't."

"I am not..."

"Not you. I mean the first-year on the ladder, because he's been listening."

We all looked down. The boy had stopped hanging the lantern. In fact he had stopped doing anything, and he held the hook in one hand with his mouth a little open.

"Back to work," said Lysette.

He went back so fast that he nearly fell off.

Then the stair creaked again, and Isolde came up from the yard. She is the president of the cooking club, a girl in her third year. Since the day of the flour, she had looked like someone coming home from a long war. She had a roll of paper under her arm, and she took us all in with one tired look. She saw Seraphine with the notebook and the vice-chair with the pencil. She saw the Dawn Boy red to the ears, Felix staring at the ceiling, and me.

"The bill for the kitchen," she said, to nobody. "From the Bursar, the school's money man."

"We'll pay," said Rowan.

"You can't pay." She unrolled it, and it kept unrolling. "It says Lance Nine owes the school four hundred and twelve crowns. That's for beams, two ovens, a mixing bowl that I'm told was historic, and..." She squinted. "'Emotional damage to the pantry.'"

"The pantry has feelings?" said Rowan.

"The pantry has me," said Isolde, very seriously. She rolled the bill up again, slowly, as if it hurt her. "It isn't due until after the Ball. The Bursar likes romance. He says nobody should get a bill before a dance." She looked at Seraphine, then at Lysette, then at the notebook. "Is someone being written up, and can I do it too? I'd like to write up the Bursar."

Nobody answered.

"No," she said. "I thought not." She went down the stairs with the bill under her arm. I thought it was the most useful thing anyone had done all morning.

Seraphine signed with a big swing of the pencil and gave the notebook back. Lysette tore out the page, folded it twice and put it in her coat. I don't know why she kept it, because a Committee copy belongs in the Committee book.

"Five days," said Seraphine to me, as she turned to go. She was herself again, and her chin was up. "The offer is still open, Eden, roof or no roof. I'll leave you a plate."

"Of the little smoked things?"

"I'll leave you two."

She went down with Felix one step behind. I heard her say, "Did you really sigh?" and him say, "It was a cough."

Rowan got up. His mouth opened, and for a moment I thought he would say something to Lysette. Then he looked at the ground and ran down the stairs, humming, still wrong.

That left the two of us on the landing, with a ladder and a boy who was carefully not listening.

"Lysette," I said.

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"Good." She put the notebook in her pocket. Her hand was steady. "I have rounds."

She walked up the stairs, not down. That was the wrong way for rounds, and I didn't mention it.

* * *

She found me on the roof that night. I had known she would.

I had been there since sunset. The wind was cold but dry, and the little garden was staying green in the wrong month, as it always does. The rosemary had a new shoot, and a thistle stood by the water barrel looking proud.

I heard the trapdoor and did not turn around, because I had learned that if you turn, she stops climbing.

She came up slowly with a watering can in one hand. She waited a moment at the top of the ladder, and then she walked past me to the beds. She began to water the lilies in a very straight line.

"It's not dry," I said.

"I know."

"You watered it yesterday."

"Eden, please."

I closed my mouth. She kept pouring until the can was empty, and she held it tipped over until the last drops fell. Then she put it down by the barrel and sat on the bench at the far end. I was at the near end. There was a hand's width of wood between us, and neither of us touched it.

"I don't have a partner," she said.

"Okay."

"I haven't had one all week, not once. I told Seraphine I hadn't decided, but that wasn't true. I knew I wouldn't ask anyone. And nobody would ask me, because I'm the girl who writes up Seraphine."

"Someone might."

"Who? You?"

"I'll be on the roof."

"I know," said Lysette. I couldn't tell if she was laughing. "I'm so sorry for you. It must be hard to be so easy to guess."

She pulled up her knees and rested her chin on them, which I had never seen her do. Far below, one window in the east wing was lit. A violin tuned up in there, and then someone practiced the waltz.

"Corentin took me to the Ball," she said. "Every year, until he went to serve at the royal court. He hated dancing. He counted under his breath the whole time, and I stepped on his foot on purpose. He'd say, 'Lysette, the beat.'" She was quiet for a while before she went on. "I haven't been since."

My left hand lay in my lap. I looked at the glove, and then at nothing.

"That sounds like a good brother," I said.

"He was a very annoying one."

I said nothing more, and that wasn't kindness. Every sentence I had was either a lie or a knife. Long ago I had decided which one I could live with. So I sat, and the violin tried the same four bars again, got them wrong and stopped.

"You're not going to say anything useful," she said.

"No."

"That's a relief. Everyone else is useful at me." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, fast, like someone hitting a fly. "Rowan asked Clara."

"I know."

"I'm glad, I really am. She's kind, and so is he, and they'll walk in a square. It will be sweet and awful, and I'm not sad." She took a breath. "Why do I keep saying it as if someone asked?"

I said nothing. After that she talked for a long time about small things. She talked about the garden and the Committee's money, and about a second-year girl who cried in the office because her sister had gone to the war. She talked about her mother, who had written twice this week about the family papers. Her voice went up and down and sideways. I stayed, and I did not look at her when it cracked, and I did not look away either. Around the eleventh bell she ran out all at once and sat with her hands loose around her knees.

The cold had come into the bench and my rib hurt, but I would have stayed another hour.

"Thank you for not being useful," said Lysette.

"Any time."

She stood and held the empty can against her chest, and her jaw had changed. I knew that jaw, because she wore it when she had decided something against her own good sense.

"I'm going," she said.

"To the Ball?"

"Yes, on my own, in a dress. I'll walk in, in front of everyone, and stand in the center of the hall." She lifted her chin. "To prove I don't care."

"Prove it to who?"

"Anyone who's looking."

She went down the ladder with the can. I heard her steps on the stairs, quick and even, until the door shut and only the violin was left.

I sat there a while, and the thistle did not move. In five days, five hundred people would look at her, and all of them would be inside. I would be on the wrong side of the glass.
