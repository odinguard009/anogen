# Chapter 21: The Locket

Seraphine asked Eden at lunch on Monday, and she asked it in the middle of a joke, so that nobody could tell where the joke ended. "Wren," she said, "will you be my partner at the Ball? As a joke, mostly."

Eden put his spoon down. "No."

"As a joke, mostly," she said again, and she waited for the sentence to turn into something else. She put a hand on her heart. "I am asking the whole school. You are the only one who says no before I finish the sentence."

"Then ask someone who says yes."

"They all say yes." She looked around the table. "Even Felix says yes, and he says it in a voice that makes it sound like a funeral."

Felix kept his eyes on his bread.

"No," Eden said.

Felix looked at his bread, and Rowan looked at Felix, and Clara looked at Seraphine, and the table went on being a table. Lysette sat at the end with her black book open, and her pen stopped in the middle of a word.

"Why not?" Seraphine asked, and she smiled while she asked it.

"I do not dance."

"I do not either, and that did not stop me asking."

"Then we would stand in a corner together."

"That is exactly what I meant." Seraphine put her elbows on the table. "You are no fun at all."

"You knew that when you asked."

Lysette stood up so quickly that her book shut on its own, and she walked to the next table where Seraphine's collar had slipped one corner out of place. "Your collar is crooked," she said.

Seraphine put a hand to it. "It is meant to be like that."

"The uniform has a straight collar."

"Then the uniform has no imagination."

"I am writing you up." Lysette wrote a line in her book, and her pen made a hard mark on the page. "Crooked collar during the lunch hour."

"I will wear it like that tomorrow as well."

"Then I will write it again."

A prefect came past and asked what the second entry was for, and Lysette held out the book so he could read it, and he read "crooked collar" and looked at Seraphine's collar, which was now very straight.

"It was crooked," Lysette said.

"It is not now."

"That is the point of a report," Lysette said, and the prefect went away with a confused face.

Seraphine watched him go and then leaned across the table. "You are writing me up because of him," she said, and she pointed at Eden with her spoon.

"No," Lysette said.

"Then point me at the line where you wrote his name."

There was no line with his name on it, and Lysette shut the book, and she carried her tray to the sink.

Lysette walked back to her seat and sat down, and she looked at the soup, and then at the wall, and then at her book, and she did not open it again. Eden watched her from the corner of his eye, and he said nothing, because there was no sentence he could say that would be useful.

Invitation week started that afternoon. The academy put a green poster on the board by the hall, and it had a picture of the east hall with lanterns drawn in gold, and a line at the bottom that said "formal dress, no exceptions." Rowan stood in front of it for ten minutes, and then he went to find Clara, and he came back with her and asked Eden to judge the colour of a ribbon she had brought from home.

"It is blue," Eden said.

"It is green," Rowan said.

"It is blue in this light."

"You see," Rowan said to Clara, and Clara held the ribbon up to the window and said nothing.

Felix came past with a black coat over his arm and a face like a man going to a trial. "Eaten today?" he asked.

"Yes," Eden said.

"Then you are ahead of me." Felix put the coat over his shoulder. "Seraphine says I must dance with her once, and she says it in a way that makes it sound like an order from the king."

"Is it?"

"She wrote it on a card."

* * *

Eden was on the roof after evening class when Lysette came up the stair. The roof garden was bare under the frost, and the herbs had been cut back, and the water in the tank had a skin of ice at the edge. She wore her dark coat with the white collar, and she carried something in her hand at her throat.

She sat on the bench beside him and put her book on her knee, and for a while she looked at the city without speaking. A tram went along the lower road, and its lights moved between the roofs, and the wind came over the wall in short pushes. The herb bed at the far end had been tied down with string for the winter, and straw covered the mint, and frost had caught the edge of the tank in a white line. Eden had brought a second coat because the forecast said snow, and he had not needed it. He sat with his back against the post and listened to Lysette turn the pages of her book without reading any of them.

"I have no partner," she said.

"For the Ball," Eden said.

"For the Ball." She turned the small thing in her fingers, and he saw that it was a silver locket on a thin chain, and that it was shut. "It is the first year I have worn it in public since my mother gave it. I wear it once a year, and I shut it, and I put it back in the box the next morning." She held it up, and there was a hairline scratch across the front and one link that had been mended. "That is the whole story of this thing."

"Does it open?" Eden asked.

"Yes."

"Do you ever open it?"

"Not in front of people." She turned it once, and the mended link caught the light. "My mother opens it on the anniversary, and I sit in the room and look at the wall. There is a portrait inside, and I know what it is, and I have not decided whether to look at it as a woman or as a girl who had a brother."

"Why wear it this year?"

"Because everyone will be there, and my mother will see the pictures in the paper, and she will want to know why I wore it." Lysette put it back under her collar. "So I am wearing it, and I am going to stand in a room full of people, and I am going to be photographed with it."

"Yes," Eden said.

"Is that all you have to say?"

"I do not know what else to say."

"Good." She looked out at the city. "That is better than saying something kind."

The wind moved the string of her braid, and she did not put her book down, and she did not look at him.

"Do you know what the Black Thirteen are?" she asked.

"No," Eden said, and it was true, and it was not the answer he would have given if he had been choosing one.

"Thirteen knights in black armor. They came back with the king after the last war, and nobody knows their names, and nobody knows whose orders they take." Her hand went to her throat, and her fingers closed over the locket under the collar, and they stayed there. "My brother came home from court and could not dance after that. He stood at the edge of the room all night, and he told my mother it was the heat. It was December."

"He was twenty when he came back from court," Lysette said. "He had been at the capital for six months, and he had learned to dance, and my mother had ordered a coat for him with silver buttons. He came home in that coat and stood by the wall at every party, and when the music started he turned the button on his cuff round and round with his thumb."

Eden said nothing. The city below kept its grid of yellow windows, and the clock in the tower struck the half hour, and he stayed on the bench with his hands in his pockets and his boots on the low wall.

"People say the black knights killed him," Lysette said. "They say he was a traitor and the Thirteenth did what traitors deserve. My mother writes letters about it, and she leaves out the part that matters. She writes that the house will act as if it were not, and I am supposed to know what that means without being told." Her hand stayed closed over the locket. "He could not dance, and he was my brother, and I do not know what happened in that room."

Her brother's name was on a list in the court register, and Eden had seen that list once in Ysolde's office, and he had looked away from it before the second line. He knew what the list meant, and he knew what Lysette thought it meant, and the space between the two was a room he could not enter with a sentence.

Eden looked at her hand and then at the city. There were three true sentences he could have said, and all three of them would have ended the quiet she was sitting in, so he said none of them.

"Do you know why I asked Seraphine about the collar?" Lysette asked.

"No."

"Because she was smiling at your table, and I wanted to write something down." She shut her book and held it with both hands. "I did not want to write the thing I was actually thinking."

"Which was?"

"That you would go with her if she asked again." She said it flatly, as if she were reading a number from a form. "You would say no, and then you would think about it, and by Thursday you would go, because she would ask in a way that made it seem like a joke and not a request."

"I said no."

"You said no once." Lysette stood up from the bench, and she took her book with her, and she did not look at him when she spoke again. "I will go after all. Not with anyone. Just to prove I don't care."

"Prove it to whom?"

"To the room." She walked to the stair, and then she stopped with her hand on the door, and she turned back. "And to myself, which is worse."

"You will be cold in that hall."

"I will wear the locket." Her fingers went to her throat again, and then she took them away. "It is the only thing in this school that was mine before I was old enough to argue."

She went down the stair, and Eden stayed on the bench until the roof door had stopped moving. Above him the sky was clear and cold, and below him the city kept its yellow windows, and he thought about the black armor and the man who could not dance, and he wrote nothing in his notebook.

He went down the stair at last, and the hall was dark, and the green poster on the board showed its gold lanterns in the half light. He stopped in front of it for a while, and he read the line about formal dress, and then he walked past it to the store room where his coat hung, and he took the key from his pocket and opened the door.

The store room was cold and empty, and his drill lance stood in its rack with dust on the blunted cap. He put his coat on a hook and sat on the crate by the door, and he listened to the pipes in the wall until the evening bell rang over the court.
