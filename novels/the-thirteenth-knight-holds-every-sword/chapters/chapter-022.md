# Chapter 22: The Dye Works

Ysolde gave the order in a corridor under the palace on Tuesday morning, and she did not break her stride. "Dye works tonight, before shift change. There are children in the side room, and they are not part of the cell."

"How young?" Eden asked.

"One of them is nine, and she has a sister in the bakery." Ysolde turned a card over. "If a child is hurt, the Crown wins without firing a shot."

"Understood."

"Use Vesk for the front. The cell knows the shadow he is wearing, and it will take an order from it." She stopped at a door and looked at him. "Do not let him laugh."

"He cannot."

"Good. What about the shift change?"

"Twenty minutes, and then a man comes with a cart and takes the jars away. Be out before the cart."

Under a wall of blue brick, the drainage tunnel came out, and Eden stepped into the yard at the third bell. Wet lime and old dye filled the air, and foam moved on the vats in slow circles.

Vesk waited in a vat's shadow with his hands in his pockets. His coat was grey now, and his eyes had gone a flat brown, and his voice came out with a stranger's weariness.

"You are late," Vesk said.

"The bell is early," Eden said.

"It always is." Vesk lifted one hand and pointed at the back room. "Six leaders in there, and four children in the side room. The children are from the refugee rows."

Umra closed over Eden without a sound. Black plate covered his chest and arms, and the white-thread slit in the helm made one bright line across his face. Edges grew sharp in the yard: the rim of each vat, the gallery rail, the line where the lamp light stopped on the stone. He drew **[Exa]** from his shadow, and the lance came into his hand cold and plain. He knocked once on the iron ring, and it folded into the tray-sized door-shield, and he held it close to his chest.

"**[Mott]**," Vesk said, and then he stopped, because the shadow he wore could not say that word. He showed Eden the back of his hand instead, and the grey coat sat on him like a skin that had been cut for another man.

Half a beat behind Vesk moved the borrowed shadow, and and his brown eyes blinked less often than his own, and his voice was the voice of the dead Ember in the lane. It would hold while the body stayed unseen and that Ember kept quiet. If the real man spoke in the back room, the coat would split and show the small figure inside it. Vesk could not laugh in it, and he had never laughed well in anything.

"Shift change in twenty minutes," Vesk said in the borrowed voice. "Tell me when you are through, and I will give the order to stand down."

"Will they obey you?"

"They obeyed him." Vesk looked at the back door. "They are frightened men, and frightened men obey a coat."

Eden looked at the vats, and at the gallery above them, and at the two men by the door with grey bands on their sleeves. "Which of them had the real coat?" he asked.

"The one in the lane," Vesk said. "His body is under a crate, and his mouth is shut. Keep it that way."

"If he speaks?"

"Then you will see me very clearly."

They crossed the yard, and two Embers at the back door saluted Vesk because the shadow on his shoulders was one they knew. He gave orders in the flat brown voice. "Back room now. Count off, and leave the side room alone." The Embers moved to obey, and they did not look at the black shape behind him.

Inside, a lamp with a turned shade lit a long table. Four men sat with their coats off, and two stood beside a shelf of glass jars packed with grey dust. One man had a grey line running up his neck under his collar, and the line moved when he swallowed.

"Who is this?" the man with the grey neck asked, and he looked at Vesk.

"My guard," Vesk said.

"I did not order a guard."

"You did, two minutes ago." Vesk put one hand on the table. "Write it in the book if you like, and we will lose another minute."

He looked at the book and sat down.

"Write my name as well," Vesk said.

"What name?"

"The one on the coat." Vesk put the book in front of him and turned to the page with the roll call. "You wrote it yourself last week."

The man wrote it, and his hand was slow, and the Cinder at the end of the table whispered the number seven again.
 A Cinder at the end of the table counted the men in a low voice, and he reached six and then stopped, because seven shadows stood on the wall.

Eden stepped in with the shield up. The man nearest the door had his collar open at the throat and his glove loose at the wrist, and there was a narrow strip of skin between the two. Eden put the lance point through that strip with no more force than a needle. The man's hand went to his throat, and he was on the floor before the shield moved again.

"**[Draught]**," Eden said, and his voice was quiet.

The **[Draught]** needed one exact opening: collar and jaw, or glove and wrist, where armor had no part. It gave no room for a swing, and it gave no second gap in the same man. It stopped a point at the throat as well as it gave one.

A second man reached for a jar of dust. Eden caught his wrist on the shield edge. The jar fell and broke on the stone, and grey dust went into the air. Vesk's borrowed voice cut across the room with an order to hold position. Two men froze, because they had heard that voice give orders before. The fine dust hung in the lamplight and settled on the jars. The smell grew sharp enough to taste.

"Stand down," Vesk said.

"We do not know you," a man at the window said.

"You know the coat." Vesk's borrowed voice went flat. "That has always been enough."

Both hands went on the table, and the man at the window did not move.

"Nobody move," Vesk said.

The third leader was the man with the grey neck. He came across the table with a knife, and his veins showed dark at his throat. He reached Eden. Eden put the shield into the knife and turned the man's arm. The lance found the gap between glove and wrist. The knife fell. The grey in the man's neck went darker for a beat, and then he sat down. Both hands lay open on the table.

The dust stung Eden's nose. He knew that smell: dry and thin, with a edge like cold metal. It was the smell that clung to a Veil after it fell to dust, and it was not the same thing, and he did not let himself think it was. The nose remembered before the mind could correct it.

"How many?" Vesk asked from the door.

"Three down," Eden said. "Two frozen. One by the shelf."

"Keep the door clear."

A fourth leader went for the broken glass. Eden put a boot on it and turned him with the shield. The lance point found the strip between collar and jaw. The man fell against the shelf, and three jars went over. Grey dust lay on the table in a thin line.

The fifth leader was the Cinder who had counted the shadows. He put both hands up and stepped back from the table, and his face was young and wide, and he was not much older than the children in the side room. Eden stopped with the point at his chest.

"Sit down," Eden said.

The boy sat down.

"Why did you count the shadows?" Eden asked.

"The man told me to count everyone who came in."

"And?"

"There were seven." The boy looked at the floor. "I did not say it fast enough."

Eden held the shield between the room and the door, and the iron ring was cold under his glove, and the board kept a small dent from the first knife.

Eden wiped the table with a cloth from the shelf, and he wiped the lance and put it back in his shadow, and he stacked the broken glass by the wall where no child would step on it. He counted the six leaders: three still, one by the shelf breathing hard, one frozen by the door, and the Cinder at the table with his hands up.

"Side room," Vesk said, and he went ahead with the borrowed step.

Four children sat on the floor around a bowl of bread, and the youngest had dye on his fingers to the wrist. They looked at Vesk and then at Eden, and none of them screamed.

"Are you from the Committee?" the girl with dye on her fingers asked.

"No," Eden said.

"Then take us home."

"When it is safe." He kept the shield across the doorway. "Stay with the bread until then."

The girl looked at the bread, and then at him, and she broke her piece into four equal parts.
 Eden put the shield across the doorway so that it blocked the view of the back room, and Vesk told them in the borrowed voice to stay where they were, and they stayed.

"Are you the guard?" the youngest asked.

"No," Eden said.

"Then why are you holding a shield?"

"Because the door is cold."

The youngest considered that and went back to his bread. Vesk shut the door from the outside, and the borrowed voice said something low and kind to the children through the wood, and Eden did not ask him what it was.

When he went back into the back room, the man by the shelf was still breathing. Grey veins showed on his neck, and one had reached the corner of his eye, and his laugh came out thin and wet.

"You're late," the man said. "The Crown already has the hall."

Eden stopped with his hand on the door. The laugh went on for two beats and turned into a cough, and the grey at the man's eye spread under the skin. Eden did not answer, and he went out to the yard, where the shift bell was ringing at the gate.

No gate man stood there yet, and the yard was empty, and the vats kept their blue circles in the lamplight. Eden walked to the tunnel mouth and stopped, and he looked back at the back room door and at the side room with the children behind it. A girl's voice came through the wood, low and steady, and he could not hear the words.
He closed the door and went.

Vesk came out behind him, and the grey coat slid off his shoulders. It lay on the stones like a shadow that had been left behind. He stepped out of it, and his own small shape came back, and his eyes were lamp-yellow again. He did not put the coat back on, and he left it lying on the wet stone, and the grey of it faded until it was only a shadow shape with no edges. Eden looked at it for a moment, and he waited until the children's voice stopped. Then he went.

"They will find the body in the lane," Vesk said.

"Then the coat ends tonight," Eden said.

"I cannot wear it again," Vesk said. "He talked in his sleep, and that is why his mouth was shut."

"You did well."

"I cannot laugh," Vesk said, and he looked almost sorry. "That is the worst part."
