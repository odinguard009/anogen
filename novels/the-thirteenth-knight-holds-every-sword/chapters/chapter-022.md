# Chapter 22: The Smell of the Dust

The dye-works stood at the end of a lane that ran with blue water, and the water had a skin on it that moved in the wrong direction. Vesk waited under an arch with a man's shadow draped over his arm like a coat, and he was not wearing his own face.

"I took it from the man at the gate," he said. His voice was the dead man's voice, thick and slow. "He had been dead five minutes. That is close enough."

"Close enough for what?"

"For **[Mott]**." Vesk pulled the shadow over his shoulders, and his grey coat went the brown of the dead man's coat, and his yellow eyes went the pale colour of the dead man's eyes. The borrowed shape settled over him like a hood. "It holds while the real man is quiet. If he speaks, it falls off. He is dead, so he cannot speak."

"How long?"

"As long as the body stays where I left it and nobody finds it." Vesk's new mouth did not move like his own. "Do not make me laugh."

Eden looked at the dye-works. The building was four walls of wet brick with a slate roof and a chimney that had not smoked in years, and a cart stood outside with two empty dye barrels on it. One window on the ground floor stood open, and the smell that came out of it was not dye. It was the smell of the exchange cellar after the Veil had died: fine, cold, and dry in the throat. He had smelled it once in November and he had not forgotten it.

"Children inside," Vesk said. "Three or four. Cinders. They sleep in the back room."

"Then they leave first."

"I will order them out." Vesk's borrowed face turned toward the lane. "An Ember can tell a child to go home. A grey man in a coat cannot."

"What if the body is found?"

"Then it falls off in front of three armed men." Vesk turned up his borrowed collar. "Do not let the body be found."

"The bridge was watched," Eden said. "That is what I will tell them."

"You have never used that line."

"It is Vesk's line. You said it first."

"I said it to you once on a stair." The borrowed face folded into something like a grin. "Use it properly."

* * *

The cellar of the dye-works had three rooms and no lamps, only a red bulb over the inner door that made everything look cut out of meat. Water dripped from a pipe into a bucket by the stairs, and each drop took the same time to fall into the dark water. Vesk went in through the front, and Eden went in through the shadow of the outer door, and the plan was one line: the children out, the leaders one at a time, the room wiped. Barrels stood along the west wall with labels chalked on them, and two long tables held bundles of cloth and a set of scales. A boy of about twelve slept on a pile of sacks in the back room with his hand open on a crust of bread.

Vesk went in first. He walked with the dead man's heavy step and slapped the outer door twice with the flat of his hand, which was a signal Eden had never seen but had watched Vesk practise in the lane.

"Wake up," Vesk said in the thick voice. "All of you. Out the side door and up the lane. Two minutes."

A girl sat up on the sacks. "Is it a drill?"

"It is a drill." Vesk did not look at her. "Take the little one and go. No talking."

She got up, shook the boy by the shoulder, and gathered two younger children from behind a barrel. The boy's crust fell on the sacks and he did not go back for it. The girl counted them under her breath, once, and took the smallest by the sleeve. They went through the side door without once looking at Eden, because they were looking at the man who had ordered them, and he was an Ember to them. When the last child was out, Vesk closed the door and turned the bar.

"Four," he said. "No one saw you."

Three men waited in the middle room. The one at the scales had a narrow face and a grey line running up his neck into his jaw. The other two stood by the inner door with short blades and their coats buttoned to the throat. None of them looked at Vesk twice, which told Eden what he needed to know: the borrowed shadow worked.

"You are late," the man at the scales said.

"The bridge was watched," Vesk said, in the dead man's voice.

"By whom?"

"By the school." Vesk picked up a bundle of cloth and set it down again. "Two prefects and a girl with a book."

The man at the scales grunted and turned back to his scales. That was the first leader, and Eden ended him without crossing the room. He had come in through the shadow of the outer door and had stood behind the barrels for four minutes, which was long enough to know the weight of the man's head and the exact moment he put both hands on the scale pan. Eden put **[Exa]**'s point through the gap between the man's collar and his jaw, where the grey line ran, and pushed up. **[Draught]** needed one opening, and the openness of the man's neck was the whole of it. The point stopped at the throat and the man's hands came off the scales, and the weights went on tilting by themselves for a second after he was still.

The other two turned. Vesk was already between them and the body, still wearing the dead man's face, and he said, in the thick voice, "Hold. He has been cut. Check the door."

"Who cut him?" the man nearest the scales asked.

"One of the school boys," Vesk said, and put the thick voice through the room like a verdict. "He followed us from the bridge. Check the door."

One of them checked the door. That was the second, and Eden took him as he turned back, with the lance point through the same kind of gap at the base of the skull where the coat collar stood away from the neck. He folded him down onto the sacks without letting the body hit the floor.

The third man was faster. He drew his blade and came at Vesk, because the borrowed face had just given an order and then the order had stopped making sense. Vesk stepped back into the red light, and the brown of his coat went grey at the edges, and the dead man's voice came out of him in a crack.

"He is dead," Vesk said, in his own voice for one word.

The borrowed shape fell off him like a sheet sliding down a rail, and he was small and grey again with his yellow eyes bright. The man with the blade saw two men where there had been one and stopped for half a step, and half a step was all Eden needed. He put **[Exa]** through the gap at the wrist where the man's glove did not meet his coat, and the blade went out of his hand.

"Do not kill me," the man said.

Eden looked at his neck. The grey veins showed under the skin in a fine net, thicker at the throat, and the smell came off him in the heat of the room: the same dry cold smell as the dust in the construct's core and the dust on the exchange carpet. Eden knew it now, and knowing it did not make it less strange. It was the smell of a place that had no air in it.

"I have seen that," the man said, and touched his own neck. "It does not mean what you think."

"No," Eden said. "It means something else."

"Do you know what it means?"

"Not yet."

"Then why are you looking at me like that?"

Eden did not answer. The third man's mouth shut, and the red bulb went on humming, and Vesk stood between the two of them with his own face back and his hands open at his sides, and the brown of the dead man's coat lay in a heap on the floor behind him.

Eden did not ask what it meant, because a dying Ember was not a witness and a guess was not proof. The man went down on his knees with his hands open, and Eden put the point of **[Exa]** at the place where the grey lines were thickest, and there was no sound except the red bulb humming above them.

When it was over, Vesk wiped the scales and the door bar and the two places on the floor where the bodies had been. He worked without hurry and used a cloth from his own coat, and when he had finished he put the cloth back in the same pocket.

"The children are up the lane," he said. "They think a drill ended early."

"Good."

"You found the smell." Vesk's yellow eyes went to the grey on the dead man's neck and back. "I smelled it in the exchange that night and did not say anything."

"You should have."

"You would have asked what it was." Vesk pulled his own grey coat straight. "I did not know." Eden wiped the lance and folded it back into his shadow. The room smelled of cold dust under the dye, and he opened the high window two fingers to let the smell out, then closed it again because a draught would be noticed in the morning. The three bodies were laid where the barrels made a wall, and with the red bulb left burning they looked like men who had fallen asleep after a shift. Nothing in the room said how many people had been there an hour ago.

He checked the back room. The crust of bread was still on the floor where the boy had dropped it, and the four children were gone up the lane. He put the crust on the table so it would not look like a child had been interrupted.

The last Ember was on the floor by the inner door with his eyes open and his mouth still working. Eden knelt because the words came from a man who would not have much time, and because the rule was to listen before you left a room.

"You're late," the Ember said, with blood in his throat and something like pleasure in it. "The Crown already has the hall."
