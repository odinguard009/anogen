# Chapter 23: The Voices

The second safehouse had a new lock on its front door and brown paper on its windows. The street held one lamp at the corner. Eden came up the alley at the second bell and stopped under the lamp. The house had no other light, and the row of shops behind it was shut.

The order had come from Ysolde that morning with a key laid on her desk. "The cell knows we are looking," she had said. "Gorrath will go with you."

"He does not like stairs," Eden had said.

"He does not like locks either, and he came anyway." She had put the plan face down. "Find the Ball plan, and do not take a prisoner. There will be children within two streets of that house."

Gorrath waited in the alley with his shield across his chest and his one free hand on the wall. "There is a bell wire on the stair," he said.

"I saw it."

"Then I will answer it."

"Can you?"

Gorrath thought about that. "I have answered bells before, and I have always been wrong."

Umra closed over him in the shadow of the awning. The black plate was cold at his chest, and the helm slit made one white line across his view. The air went grey-blue around the row. He drew **[Exa]** from his shadow and knocked once on the ring. The lance folded into the tray-sized shield, and he carried it at his hip as he went up the step.

The front door opened into a hall with a stair on the right and a row of coats on pegs to the left. Nobody stood in the hall. A board had been set across the lower stair with a bell wire tied to it. The wire ran under the door into the back room. Eden put one boot on the bottom step and stopped, and he waited for the house to show him what it wanted.

It showed him three shadows on the wall, a fourth by the coats, and a fifth behind the stair rail. The cell had been expecting the Vigil, and it had placed its people where a single black figure would have to pass. A man coughed in the back room, and a chair scraped the floor. Then the house went quiet in the way a house goes quiet when everyone listens.

Eden did not go up the stair. He put his back to the wall beside the door and knocked twice on the hard edge where the lamplight met the dark under the coats.

A black opening appeared in the shadow behind the pegs. Gorrath came out as a shape rather than a man, with broad shoulders and a shield held low. He did not speak in his own voice, and he did not need to. The **[Knock and Answer]** was something he did with shadows, not with a mouth.

One of Gorrath's voices came out of the shadow under the coats, tired and calm: "One."

A second voice came from behind the stair rail, and it was high and quick: "Two."

A third came from the lamp's own dark side: "Three."

Then the voices came from every shadow in the hall at once. There were thirteen of them, some slow and some flat and some so calm they sounded bored. They called numbers in a roll call and answered each other across the hall. The sound of thirteen men filled a room that held only two.

The **[Knock and Answer]** needed thirteen shadows to stand where Gorrath put them, and it needed his effort to hold them in place. It was a roll call spoken by one man in thirteen voices. When Gorrath was not careful, the counting went wrong, and he was the first to admit it. Tonight the count came out straight: one, two, three, four, and on up to thirteen, with a different tired tone in every word.

In the back room a man said, "They have the stair."

"No," another said. "That is the hall."

"Then who is on the stair?"

Nobody answered, because everybody in the house was looking at the wall where the shadows were speaking. The safe held three jars of dust and a folded letter, and the letter was addressed to a name that Eden did not know.

"Where is the plan for the Ball?" Eden asked.

The man with ink on his fingers looked at the table and then at the window. "There is no plan."

"Then why is there a safe open on your table?"

"Because we were writing a letter."

"To whom?"

The man shut his mouth, and Gorrath's voice came from the inkpot on the table, tired and close. "He will tell us in his own time." A chair fell over, and a man ran into the hall with a short blade. He stopped in the middle of the room because there were too many armed men in it. He could see thirteen shadows and two real people, and he had no way to tell which was which.

"Hold," Gorrath said from the coats, and the word sounded like an old sergeant with a cold.

The man held.

"That is thirteen," the man at the window said.

"There are only two of you," Eden said.

"There were thirteen a moment ago."

"Then count again."

The man did not count again.

"What are you?" he asked.

"Sixteen," Gorrath said from the coats, and the other voices laughed in twelve different ways, and the man put his blade on the floor.

Eden stepped out from the wall with the shield up, and he went to the back room while the cell watched the hall. Two men sat at a table with papers in front of them, and a third stood by a safe with his hand near a bell wire. The safe was open, and a folded plan lay on top of a stack of papers.

The third man pulled the wire. Nothing happened, because Gorrath's tired voice had already answered from the bell's own shadow. "No," it said, and the man let go of the wire.

Eden put the shield edge against the table and swept the papers into it. He folded Exa back into the lance and set the point at the safe. The two men at the table stood up slowly, and one of them had ink on his fingers. He kept looking at the hall where the thirteen voices were counting.

"Down," Eden said, and they went down.

Gorrath came in behind him with the shield on his arm. He let the voices fade one at a time until only one remained, and then that one stopped. The hall shadows went back to being shadows on a wall. The house seemed small and ordinary again, with a fallen chair and five men sitting where they had been told.

Eden unfolded the plan on the table. It was a copy of the east hall's layout drawn in black ink, with the lanterns marked in blue and the doors numbered one through four. Two names had been written in the margin in a wide, careful hand: "Rowan T., guest to collect" and "Seraphine A., guest to collect." A line had been crossed out under them and replaced with "13/12, wrong night, confirm." The plan gave the date as the thirteenth of December, one day after the Ball.

"He has the hall," Eden said.

"For the wrong night," Gorrath said, and he read the line again with his head on one side. "Or he wants us to think it is the wrong night."

"Which is it?"

Gorrath looked at the plan for a while, and then at the five men sitting quietly with their hands on the table. "Ask them," he said, and he sounded tired.

Eden asked, and the men did not answer. Gorrath asked again in the voice of a man who had been asking questions for a very long time, and still they did not answer.

"We will take the plan," Eden said. "You can tell the Crown you lost it."

"The Crown does not accept lost plans," the ink-fingered man said.

"Then tell him a door took it."

The man looked at the hall shadows, and for the first time he looked afraid.

Eden left the papers on the table and took only the plan. He did not take the jars of dust, because the children lived two streets away and the vats had already taught him what dust could do. Gorrath watched the five men while he worked, and not one of them moved.

Gorrath shrugged, and the shield rang once when he set it down. "Then we take the plan and go."

"Are we finished?" Eden asked.

"For tonight." Gorrath picked up the fallen chair and set it on its feet. "Leave the house tidy. A tidy house makes people think nobody was here."

Eden put the plan inside his coat and went back through the hall. He unlaced the left gauntlet because the strap had pressed a line into his wrist, and he turned the glove over to shake it out.

A small shape had been stamped inside the leather, no bigger than a nail. It was a feather, with one line down the middle and three short lines on each side, pressed so deep that the leather had gone dark around it.

Eden held the gauntlet up to the lamp and looked at the mark, and then at the dead black glove on his other hand. He had never seen the inside of this gauntlet before. It had been issued with the harness, and there was no maker's mark on any other piece.

"Gorrath," Eden said. "Did you see this when the harness was issued?"

"No."

"Who makes the harness?"

"The forge issues it, and a clerk signs for it, and nobody asks the forge who sewed the leather." Gorrath looked at the mark. "A mark like that is put there by someone who wants to find it again."

"Then he will find me again."

"Yes," Gorrath said, and he did not make it a joke.

Gorrath came up behind him and looked at the feather, and said nothing.

At the palace, Ysolde read the plan twice and put it in her drawer. Then she took a form from a different drawer and filled in two lines. "The Justiciar Seal guard is doubled for the Ball," she said. "Twelve men at the east hall doors, and four on the roof. That is my order, and it is already written."

"Then I will not be there," Eden said.

"You will not." She looked at him across the desk, and her face gave nothing away. "You have done enough for one week, and the east hall will be full of people who know your name. Stay away from it. That is also an order."

Eden put the gauntlet on the desk and showed her the feather stamped inside. Ysolde looked at it for a while, and then she put her hand over it.

"Where did you find that?"

"Inside my glove."

"Then do not show it to anyone else." She slid the gauntlet back across the desk.

"It was in the glove when I put the harness on," Eden said.

"Then somebody expected you to look inside it."

"Why?"

"Because a mark that is found is a message, and a message is meant to make you ask who sent it." Ysolde looked at his face. "You have asked me, and I have told you not to ask anyone else. That is the only answer you will get from me today."

"And stay away from the Ball."

Eden put the gauntlet back on before he left, and he pulled the strap tight, and the feather pressed against the inside of his wrist. He went down the back stair with his hand closed over it, and he did not take the glove off again that night. He waited.
