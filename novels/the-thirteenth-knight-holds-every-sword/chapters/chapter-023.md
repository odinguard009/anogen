# Chapter 23: Thirteen Shadows

> Tallow Row, the old tannery. Nine men, maybe ten. They know a black mask is coming. Lanterns in the upper windows mean they are ready. The paper you want is in the book room, behind the pits. Bring the paper. Leave the men alive if you can. - Y.

I read the note twice under a dripping gutter, and then I burned it on my glove.

Tallow Row was a street of low sheds in the lower city, and it stank. It smelled of lime, wet hide and old fat, and the rain made it worse. The tannery sat at the end of the row, black against a gray sky. Every upper window had a lantern in it, and every lantern was lit.

"That is a lot of welcome," said Vesk, from my shadow.

"They want me to see it."

"And you are going in anyway."

"The paper is inside, and the Ball is in two days." I looked at the lanterns. "They expect one man, so they will watch for one man."

Gorrath's voice came out of the wall behind us, low and slow, like a cart on a wooden bridge. "You always say it like it is a plan."

"It is a plan."

"It is a door with a hope on the other side."

I stepped back into my own shadow.

The cold came first, up from the ankles, like stepping into a river. Then the plates closed. Matte black, one over the other, from my boots to my hips, then ribs, then shoulders. The gauntlets came last and locked with a small click. The helm slid down over my face, smooth, with one thin white thread where my eyes were. I did not hear it. Umra makes no noise, and that is why the Black Order likes it.

My arms felt heavier, and my steps felt lighter. The rain hit the plates and ran off without a sound. My fingertips went white and numb inside the glove. That was the Frost, and it was always the price.

I walked out of the alley and down the middle of the street.

No one shouted. A cart horse in a yard lifted its head and went back to its hay. I opened the side gate of the tannery and walked in as if I owned it.

The main shed was long and dark, with a stone floor wet from the pits. Hides hung from poles over my head, hundreds of them, gray and stiff. Between the pits stood a table with one lamp. The lamp went out when I took my third step.

The door shut behind me. Bolts slid. Shutters banged along both walls, and then came the click of crossbows being raised.

"That's him," said a man's voice, flat and tired. "Black mask. Same as the dye-works."

I stood still and counted by sound. Four men were above me on the rafters, three behind the pits, and two at the far door. One stood at my back, so close that I could smell his coat. That made ten. The man who spoke had a brown thumb. I could see it on the crossbow, and I knew him now. He was the gray-coat who had watched Rowan.

"Take off the mask," said the man with the brown thumb.

"No."

"We have ten bolts, friend. You have one neck."

"I counted."

That was not the right answer, and I knew it as I said it. A bolt hit the stone beside my foot and rang. Another struck my shoulder plate and bounced away with a flat clack, and the hit pushed me half a step. It did not hurt, but the sound made my ears ring.

I ran.

The floor was slick with tallow, so I did not run well. I went left between two pits, and a short black lance came out of my shadow into my hand because I needed it. A bolt hit the hides above me, and a whole row of them fell down. One landed on the man behind the pits, and he yelled and sat down in a bucket.

That was not part of the plan, but I was glad of it anyway.

Then my boot found the slick spot, and I slid. I went ten feet, straight and calm, with my lance held out, like a man on a frozen pond who has decided not to mind. At the end I hit a pole and stopped.

"Nice," said Vesk, in my ear.

"Say nothing."

"I said nothing. I am a shadow."

The bolts came again. Two clipped my back, and one tore across my arm guard with a shriek of metal. I dropped behind a pit wall. There were ten men and one of me, and the shed was theirs. I had known that when I came in, and if I am honest, I came in because I was proud of it. One man, no help, the paper in my hand before the bell: a clean job.

But the pride was costing me, because my left hand was dead to the wrist and I could not feel the lance.

So I did the thing I had come to do. I knocked.

Not on a door, but on the black plate of my own shield arm, three times, slowly. A small iron ring on it rang like a far bell.

"Gorrath," I said. "Thirteen."

"Thirteen," said the wall.

The floor under the nearest shadow opened a crack the size of a hand. No one saw it, because no one looks at shadows. It worked like the mouth of a speaking-pipe, and a voice came out of it.

"North rafter, ready. I have the east side. Sixteen bolts, no wind."

The Embers froze, and one of them stopped breathing.

"Gate is ours," said a different voice, from a different shadow, farther off. It was brisk and young. "No one leaves."

"Roof is ours too," said a third voice, a woman's, dry as paper. "Send the signal if the mask nods."

Gorrath is a big, shy man who cannot lie to save his life, but he can do any voice you like. He had done the Black Order's voices for years from behind walls, so no one had guessed he was one large man in a corner.

"We are in the pits," he said, in four voices at once, it seemed. One sounded bored and one sounded proud. "Someone hold the eaves. I think I broke a tile."

"Do not say that," said a drowsy voice.

That one was Vesk. I did not ask.

"And the last two are at the stairs," said Gorrath. He gave the last one an old man's cough, so real that one Ember actually looked around for a chair.

Thirteen shadows, thirteen voices, and I stood in the middle with a dead hand and a lance. It worked because every shadow in that shed could be a door, and a door can hold a man who is not there.

"You with the brown thumb," I said quietly. "Thirteen agents. You have ten bolts. Which of us do you want to be wrong?"

No one moved. Rain ticked on the roof, and somewhere a crossbow string creaked and then went slack.

"There was only one at the dye-works," whispered a man on the rafters. "They said it was one."

"They said what they wanted," said the man with the brown thumb. His voice shook now. He looked at the shadow by the lamp and the shadow by the pit and the shadow in the corner, and I saw him decide. He put the crossbow on the stone. "Put them down," he said. "Put them all down. We are not dying for a card."

One by one the bolts hit the floor. The man in the bucket stood up with a hide still on his head, and no one laughed. I took the Ember at my back by the collar, found a knife in his belt, and kicked it into the pit.

"Hands on the wall," I said, and they put their hands on the wall.

"How many are you?" the man with the brown thumb asked at last. His voice was almost polite. "In truth."

"Enough."

I tied the ten of them with their own belts, and it took a while. Gorrath's voices kept talking from the dark to keep them quiet. One voice talked about the weather. Another told a joke with a bad ending. No one looked at the shadows after that, and no one wanted to know.

* * *

The book room was behind the pits, a closet with a stove and a desk that smelled of wet ink. The paper sat in a drawer under a loaf-shaped lump of ash wax. I broke the wax and read it by Vesk's small light.

It was a list in a neat hand, eleven lines long. Most of it was numbers, lamps, doors and the names of three servants at the glass hall. Then, near the bottom, I found this:

*Guests to collect: R. Tallis. S. Aurenmark. Take them quiet. Night before the Ball, ninth bell.*

I read it again.

"The night before," said Vesk. "That is tomorrow night."

"Yes."

"The hall is shut tomorrow. They cannot even get inside it."

"Yes."

"So why plan it?"

I did not have an answer, and that bothered me more than ten crossbows had. I folded the paper and put it in my belt, because Ysolde would want it and Ysolde would have an answer, or a guard.

Then I took off the left gauntlet to warm my hand.

It was hard work, because my fingers would not close. The plate came away from the wrist, and I turned it to the lamp. Inside the cuff, where the plate meets the palm, there was a mark. It was a tiny feather stamped into the metal, no bigger than a grain of rice. The lines were so fine that I had to tilt it twice to see them.

I had worn this armor every week for years, and I had never seen that mark.

"What is it?" said Vesk.

"A smith's stamp, I think."

"The Door does not have smiths."

"No." I put my thumb on it. It was warm, unlike the rest of the plate, which was cold. "It probably came with Umra."

"Gorrath's does not have one."

I put the gauntlet back on and said nothing for a while, because I did not like any of my answers.

* * *

Ysolde's wagon was waiting at the end of the lane, dark and wet, with a canvas roof and two gray horses. She sat inside with a lamp turned low. She was a thin woman with a flat voice, and she had read a thousand reports like mine. Soldiers carried the ten Embers out in a line, and not one of them looked at me.

I handed her the paper. She read it in the time it took me to climb up.

"The night before," she said.

"I noticed."

"Careless of them."

"That is what Vesk said. I do not think they are careless."

"They are ten men in a tannery who surrendered to a mask." She folded the paper and set it on her knee. "I will double the Golden Seal at the glass hall. Tomorrow night, and the night of the Ball. I will put two more on the Tallis boy and the princess. You will do nothing."

"Nothing?"

"You will stay away from the Ball." She looked at me, and the lamp lit half her face. "No roof, no mask, no door. You have done enough for three weeks. You are tired, you are cold, and your hand shakes."

It did not shake. I held it still on my knee.

"The Black Order has been seen at two places this week," she said. "The Crown is learning your shape. If you are at the Ball, they will learn it again, in front of five hundred people. I want you in your bed."

I thought about the paper, and the wrong night, and Rowan humming the waltz off-key. I thought about the roof above the glass hall, and the green panes of it, and a girl who had decided to go alone.

"Understood," I said.

Ysolde waited, and the rain filled the gap.

"That was fast," she said.

"You gave an order."

"You have never once done that."

"There is a first time."

She looked at me for the span of a breath, and I looked back. She is paid to notice things. She put the paper in her coat, and she did not say anything else.

I got down from the wagon and walked home through the lower city, and my left hand slowly came back, one finger at a time. I had said yes in one breath and I would keep it for exactly as long as it suited me. It would cost me whatever she did next.
