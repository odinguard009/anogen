# Chapter 24: The Eleventh Bell

Two hours and forty minutes until the lanterns went out. I knew because the program said so.

I had read it three times on the stairs, over a boy's shoulder, and he never saw me. *Eleventh bell: the lanterns are put out, one by one, for the last dance.* It was an old school custom, and it was pretty. The Ember's book had said something else: *Rowan, before the last dance.* A man who plans a kidnapping does not choose a night with lights. He chooses the one minute with none.

So I was on the roof. I had promised Ysolde I would stay away from the Ball, and the roof was not the Ball. It was fifteen feet above it.

The glass atrium sat on the east side of the school. It was a big hall with a roof of glass panes in an iron frame. The panes were small, each one the size of a book, and the frame between them was just wide enough to walk on. The dusk was going gray-blue, and a bit of moon showed through a cloud. Below me, forty-one lanterns hung on long chains, all lit. They threw yellow light up through the panes and made my hands look warm.

I had walked the lattice twice already. Every beam had a shadow, and every shadow had a use. The moon made long thin ones on the floor, like the bars of a gate.

"Doors count," whispered Vesk, from my shadow. "I make eleven good shadows. Twelve if you like the chandelier."

"I like the chandelier."

"It's the biggest thing in the room. Of course you do."

It hung over the middle of the dance floor. It was a black iron ring as wide as a cart wheel, and it held thirty candles. Its chain went up through a hole in the roof right beside my boots.

That was the preparation. It was not much. I wore my school uniform, and the black armor waited inside my own shadow. I sat in the cold with my knees up and listened to a band of nine people play a waltz through a floor of glass.

I was alone, and it was fine, because I had spent a lot of nights alone on this roof.

The band played one slow song, and then another. I saw Seraphine below in pale green, laughing with her chin up. Felix was beside her, trying to look like a man who had not been dancing with her for twenty minutes. I saw Valerius at the edge of the room, straight as a post, with a glass in his hand that he did not drink. I saw Rowan, bright in a blue coat, counting under his breath. He was holding Clara's hand as if it were a very full cup.

Just after the eighth bell a head came up the iron ladder at the end of the roof.

"Do not look," said a voice. "I am in a terrible state."

I looked. Lysette climbed over the edge in a long golden gown with one shoe in her hand, and her hair had come half down on one side. Her face was red and her eyes were wet, and she was furious about both.

"You told me you would be on the roof," she said.

"I am on the roof."

"I know. That is why I came." She sat down on the lead walkway with a thump, and the gown went everywhere. "Do not say anything kind. I will fall apart."

"Your hem is torn."

"That is not kind. That is a fact. Thank you." She wiped her cheek with her glove and left a gold smear. "I danced once. With a general's nephew. He talked about horses the whole time, and I stepped on him on purpose."

"Was it good?"

"It was the best moment of my night."

She looked through the glass. Below us the waltz went on, and Rowan turned Clara in a slow circle. He stepped on her foot. She did not even blink, and he said something that made her smile for the first time that I had ever seen.

"He learned," said Lysette. "He really did. Three weeks. I watched him on the stair, and he was awful. Now look at him."

"He is better."

"He learned it for her. Not for me." She said it very plainly, as if she were reading it off a card. "I am not crying about it. I am crying about the dress. It cost forty crowns."

"It is a good dress."

"Stop that."

I stopped. The wind moved the chains below, and a few lanterns swung. The band started another waltz, and I felt it in my feet through the iron.

"Are you going to stay up here all night?" she asked.

"Probably."

"Is it cold?"

"Yes."

"Good. I deserve it." She pulled her knees up in the gold dress, and she sat close enough that her sleeve touched mine, which she had not noticed. I did not have to say anything, and that was the best thing about her.

"The Golden Seal found ten men tied up in a tannery," she said, after a while. "People say the Black Order did it. A whole squad of black masks, they say." She turned her head. "An old friend of my father told me at dinner. He said they walked in through the walls."

"That seems unlikely."

"I thought so too." She looked at me. "You look tired."

"I always look tired."

"More than that. You look like you have been carrying something." She frowned. "Did you hear anything?"

I almost said it. It was right there, the whole thing: *They wrote down the wrong night, and I am here on the right one.* My mouth had actually opened. A second more, and she would have asked what I meant, and I would have had to answer.

"I heard that the Golden Seal is on every door tonight," I said instead. "So you are safe."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is what I know."

She held my eyes. She is a girl who can hold a look like a hand around a wrist. Then, slowly, she let go of it and laughed, a short wet laugh, and looked back down.

"You are a bad liar," she said. "I like that about you. It is the only thing I like about you tonight."

"Thank you."

"It was an insult."

"I know."

* * *

The ninth bell passed. A dancer below knocked over a table of glasses, and the whole room laughed. Lysette took the one shoe she had carried up and put it on. "I should go down and be seen," she said. "Before they say I ran away." She tried to stand, and she stepped back.

Her heel came down on the gap between two panes.

The glass creaked under her.

"Not there!" I shouted.

It came out loud and high, nothing like me, and it cracked across the roof like a dropped plate. I grabbed her by the elbow and pulled her onto the iron. The glass held, but a little crack ran through one pane, no longer than my thumb.

We stood there, breathing. Below us no one looked up, because the band was loud and the floor was full.

"That pane is cracked," I said. "Do not step on it again."

"You can tell me that calmly now?"

"I am calm now."

"You were not calm a moment ago." She said the next part slowly. "You shouted."

"Yes."

"You never shout. I have watched you all term. You have never shouted."

"I am not good at it."

"No." She was staring at me, and her cheeks went red again, and I did not think it was the crying. "Please go on being bad at it. I think I liked it."

I did not know what to say, so I brushed my sleeve and became a flat, quiet person again. It took some effort.

The tenth bell rang, and then the half hour came, and the roof began to tick with cold. Lysette leaned on the frame beside me. We did not talk, because neither of us wanted to.

Then the eleventh bell began.

It was a deep bell, and it hummed through the glass into my ribs. The band stopped in the middle of a bar. Below, the crowd made a soft sound together, half a cheer and half a sigh, and the lanterns went out one at a time.

A servant with a long pole moved down each chain, from the doors to the middle. One. Two. Then three, four, five. Each time, the hall got a little darker, and the moon on the floor got a little brighter. By twenty the dancers were just shapes. By thirty the music was only a violin, one thin line, and I could hear Rowan whisper a count.

I saw them when the last lantern was still lit.

There were four men in ash-gray coats on the iron lattice at the far end of the roof, not forty feet from me. They walked on the beams like cats, and they did not look at the glass. They looked at the big chain going up through the hole by my boots.

"Now," I said, very quietly.

"What?" said Lysette.

"Look at Rowan."

She looked. It was the only thing she would have looked at anyway, in the dark, with the violin going. I took one step back into my shadow.

The last lantern went out.

A man cut the chain.

I heard it go. The ring fell with a long iron scream and a rush of cold air, thirty candles tumbling in the black. Right under it, in the moonlight, stood a girl in pale green with her hand on a boy's arm. She looked up, and I saw her face turn white.

The ring's shadow lay on the floor beside her. It was big and round, with sharp edges, cast by the moon through the glass, and it was getting bigger as it came down.

I put my hand flat on the pane and knocked once with my knuckle. The shadow of the ring was my door.

The shadow opened all at once, with no crack, only a soft black gap the width of a wagon. The chandelier did not hit the floor. It dropped into the door like a coin into a well, and the only sound was a low, deep thump. I shut the door. The loose chain swung, and the candles never touched the floor.

My left hand was white with Frost to the wrist.

"What was that?" said Lysette. She had turned, too late. "Did something fall?"

"The chain."

"Where did it go?"

"I think it is on the floor." I stood in front of the pane, so she could not see the hole in the room. "I think the dark made it hard to see."

Then the screams started, thin at first and then wild. The doors of the atrium slammed from the outside, one, two, three, six. A heavy bar dropped across the main entrance. Gray lamps flared along the walls. In their light I saw twenty men and women in ash coats come out of the side halls with crossbows and short swords. The Golden Seal guards at the doors were already down, tied and gagged. Someone had known where to put them.

Seraphine stood in the middle of the floor, with her hand shaking and nothing falling on her. Felix had his arm around her. A voice rose over the noise, a big, clear voice, a man's.

"Everyone sit! Everyone down, and no one bleeds!"

I did not know that voice.

A tall Ember, with a gray hand on his sword, stepped into the middle of the room and turned his head slowly, looking at every face. He shouted up into the iron.

"Where is the Thornmere boy?" he said. "Where is Wren? We came for you, brother! Show yourself, and we will go easy on the rest!"

My stomach went cold, and beside me, Lysette had stopped moving.

"Wren," she said. "Thornmere. That is you."

"Yes."

"Why are they calling for you? Why do they know your name?"

I opened my mouth, and for once I had no answer ready.
