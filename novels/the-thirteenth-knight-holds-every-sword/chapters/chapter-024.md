# Chapter 24: The Eleventh Bell

The roof above the glass atrium had a low iron rail and a view down into the dance through two panels of cloudy glass. The glass had been cleaned that morning and then clouded again by the breath of four hundred people below, so the dancers moved like figures seen through a mist. The iron rail was cold enough that Eden had put his gloves back on, and a fold of his coat had frozen to the stone where he lay. Eden lay on his coat with his chin on his arm and watched the lanterns move below him like a second, slower crowd. The band played in the north corner, and the sound came up through the glass thin enough that he could count the beats of the drum without listening.

A waiter went by below with a tray of glasses and stopped to straighten one, then another, as if the lamps might judge him. Eden watched the man for the length of three beats and marked that he came from the east door each time and never once looked up.

He had not gone down into the hall. Ysolde had told him not to attend, and the roof was not the hall, and the distinction was the kind that mattered to him.

A door opened on the roof stair. Lysette came out in a dark blue gown with a white collar, and her long white hair was pinned up with the thin braid laid across the top like a crown. One strand had come loose at her forehead and she had not pushed it back. She stood in the doorway for a breath with her hands closed at her sides, then walked to the rail two steps from him and looked down through the glass.

"Rowan is dancing with Clara," she said.

"I know."

"They have danced four times. I have counted."

"Yes."

She sat down on the iron rail with her gown held away from the stone, the blue silk pooling at her feet, and for a moment Eden thought she looked like a person who had been dressed by someone else for a part she had not wanted. She had polished her shoes. Somebody, he thought, must have told her that a Committee vice-chair attends a Ball in polished shoes, and she had obeyed, even now. The band below stopped for one bar, and in the gap a girl laughed somewhere in the hall, and then the violins started again.

"I promised myself I would not cry," Lysette said. "I have kept that promise for an hour and ten minutes."

"You are doing better than me."

"You never promised."

"No."

Her mouth moved at that, almost a smile, and then it did not. She looked through the glass at the dance floor, and her hands went still on the rail. "Did they light?" she asked. "The lanterns."

"Yes."

"All of them. From the first bell." She wiped one cheek with the back of her wrist and was careful not to touch her face where the powder might come off. "You asked me that once about Rowan's lantern. I have been waiting to ask it back."

"Yes. They lit."

"Then that is something." The band below began a fast tune, and two prefects moved through the crowd carrying trays of cups, and a girl in a yellow dress turned in a circle by herself until a boy laughed and took her hand. Lysette watched all of it through the glass. "Why are you up here?"

"It is quiet."

"You always choose quiet." She brushed the loose strand of hair off her forehead and it fell back. "Even when there is something to see. Especially then."

"I am seeing it from here."

"From a roof." She said it without heat, which was worse. "I am going to ask you one question and you may answer it badly."

"Yes."

She opened her mouth, shut it, and put her hands flat on the rail. "No. Not tonight."

The band stopped for a second time and the room below clapped. Lysette did not ask the lantern question again. She turned her head and looked along the roof rather than at him, which was how she said the next part. "Everyone is down there. The Committee is down there, and I am up here, and I am not sure which of those is worse."

"The band is better from here."

"That is a stupid thing to say."

"Yes."

The band reached the end of a tune and the room below clapped, and in the quiet that followed the clock tower rang the eleventh bell. It rang once, and every lantern in the glass hall went out together.

The atrium below went dark except for the moon through the roof glass and two work lamps left burning near the kitchen door. Eden's view through the cloudy panels went from gold to grey in one beat. On the dance floor, people stopped and laughed, because a ballroom going dark was the kind of thing that happened at the end of a dance and not in the middle of one.

Then the chandelier above the centre of the hall came loose.

It did not fall at once. It swung once on its chain with a sound that went through the whole room, and the two work lamps threw its shadow up across the glass roof in a wide dark arc. People below looked up and saw a black shape pass over the dance floor, and some of them laughed because it looked like a trick of the Ball.

Eden saw its shadow before he saw the thing itself, because the moon threw the shadow across the glass roof in a long dark shape with a clean edge where it crossed a strip of light from the kitchen lamp. The chandelier swung down toward a cluster of students near the north table, and Seraphine was under it with her hand in Felix's sleeve and her face turned up to the dark.

Eden knocked twice on the glass beside his hand.

The Door opened in the chandelier's shadow where it lay across the roof glass: a black rectangle with a bright edge, no bigger than a doorway but growing as the shadow fell. Eden needed the edge to hold, and the kitchen lamp gave it one. The chandelier went through the opening with a crash that shook the panels under his cheek, and the Door shut on its chains, and the chains broke with a sound like a handful of coins dropped on stone. Below, the students saw a dark shape cross the floor and vanish into a patch of shadow under the north table, and the chandelier was gone from above them with no place for it to have gone.

His left arm went cold to the elbow and stayed there. He flexed the fingers twice and counted the beats of his breath until the feeling came back.

Seraphine looked up at the empty space where it had been. Felix had her by the elbow and was pulling her back from the north table with the stiff care of a boy who had never touched anyone in his life and had decided that tonight he would learn. He did not say a word. His book was not in his hand, and Eden realised he had never seen Felix without it. Her hand stopped shaking, and she put the other hand over it, and said something to Felix that the roof could not hear.

A shout came from the west gallery. Eden kept his eye on the hall through the glass. Men in dark coats had come out of the gallery stairs with black cards held in their hands, and the cards had broken crowns pressed into the paper. They did not run. They walked to the edges of the room and stood there with their hands at their sides, and seven more came through the kitchen door behind the two work lamps.

From his place on the roof Eden counted them again, because the first count had been too fast. Fourteen. Two at each door, three in the gallery, and the rest spread through the crowd so evenly that they had plainly walked the floor before tonight. They carried no banner and wore no mask. That was the cruelty of it. Anyone in the hall could have seen those faces at a market or a ferry or a chapel door, and none of them would have been remembered.

The cards were black and soft at the edges, and each one had a broken crown drawn in ash on the front, the same mark Eden had kept in his book since the lunch line in November. The men held them out like invitations, and the students nearest them took a step back without being told, because everyone in the school had heard what the Ashen Crowns were and nobody had expected to see one in a ballroom.

One of them raised a short blade and put it against a prefect's neck, and told the room, loudly and quite politely, not to move. The prefect's tray of cups shook, and not one cup fell.

The band stopped. Two hundred people stood still under the roof glass, and the only thing moving in the hall was a grey shape on the floor where the chandelier's shadow had been.

Lysette was on her feet at the rail with both hands on the iron. "The Ashen Crowns," she said. "They are in the hall."

"Yes."

"Seraphine is under the north table."

"She is standing."

"Is she hurt?"

"No."

"Then why is her hand shaking?" Lysette asked.

"It was shaking before."

Lysette looked at him once, hard, through the clouded glass. "You knew the chandelier would fall."

"I knew where its shadow was."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the one I have." She did not ask again, which was the thing she had promised herself not to do.

"Rowan is by the band." Lysette's fingers went white on the rail. "Clara is with him."

"I know."

From below came a voice that carried through the glass without any trouble, because it had been made to carry: "Stay where you are. We want two guests. We will take them alive."

"You with the spear, put it down," the same voice said. "You beside the spear, sit down. Nobody leaves by the kitchen door."

"Take me," Rowan said. His voice came up thin through the glass but every word was clear. "She has not done anything."

"Rowan," Clara said. "Sit down."

"I am sitting down."

"You are standing in front of me."

"I am trying to sit down in front of you."

Lysette looked down at the dark room, then at Eden, then back at the room. She did not ask him what he was going to do, and she did not step away from the rail. Her hands were flat on the iron and the iron was cold enough to hurt, and she left them there.

"They know someone is on this roof," she said.

"Yes."

"They do not know who."

"Not yet."

"Then stay up here." She said it to the glass, not to him. "If they find out, I want to know who told them, and I want to be the one standing next to you when they do."

Eden kept his hand flat on the glass and did not answer, and the band below began to play one note and stopped again.

She took in a breath and let it out slowly, and said nothing after it. Below them a man in a dark coat counted the students by the north table in a voice with no feeling in it, and the moon threw his shadow up the glass in a long thin shape that stopped just short of her hand.

An Ember near the north table turned his head up toward the roof, as if he had heard a sound in the shadow rather than seen a man. He lifted a hand and pointed, and his voice went up through the glass with the pleasure of a man who had solved something.

"There!" he shouted. "The Thornmere boy. Wren. We thought he was ours."

Below him, Lysette stopped moving altogether.
