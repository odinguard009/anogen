# Chapter 26: The Apple Tree

The hall emptied in a slow line, and the hostages went out in pairs with Seal guards. The Embers went out with tied hands and no grey bands. Eden followed the wall rather than the middle of the room, and he kept his collar up. Nobody looked at him twice.

Valerius stood at the north door with his gloves in his hand. He was watching the place on the boards where the dark figure had been.

"You are hurt," Eden said as he passed.

"It is a cut," Valerius said. "It will close."

"Good."

Valerius turned his head. "Do you know who cut it?"

"No," Eden said.

"Then you are the only person in this hall who does not." Valerius looked at him for a moment, and then he walked out into the corridor with his shoulders square.

The roof door was unlatched when Eden reached it, and he went up slowly. The bell was counting past midnight, and the hall below was still loud. The band had started again after the smoke cleared, and its sound came up through the glass in a thin thread.

Lysette sat on the bench beside the water tank, and her silver shoes stood on the low wall to dry. She had taken the pins out of her hair, and it hung over one shoulder in a pale line. The locket lay against the white cloth at her throat.

She did not stand when he came out. She looked at him for a while, then at the door behind him, then at the roof, and then at him again. She had been crying, and the powder on her cheeks had gone wet in two places. She had wiped them with her hand and made the marks longer. Her gown was grey at the hem from glass grey dust, and one silver pin had fallen out. Her hair had come down on the wrong side.

"Were you up here the whole time?" she asked.

"No," Eden said. "I was downstairs." He did not say where downstairs was, and Lysette did not ask.

He said nothing else. He stood with the roof door at his back and his hands in his coat pockets. He let the question end where it had been asked. Below them the band played four bars of a dance tune and lost the second violin and found it again.

Lysette took the answer as she took a number on a form, without arguing with it, and she put her hands in her lap. "I saw a gold light on the stage," she said. "I saw a shape with it. I could not see a face, and I could not see armor. I cannot tell you what I saw, because there was nothing to see."

"No," Eden said.

"That is not a useful answer."

"It is true," Eden said. "It is not useful."

"There is a difference," Lysette said, and she said it as if she were writing it down.

"I have no better answer."

"It is the second time you have said that tonight." She wiped one hand across the bench and then put it back in her lap. "I am going to write it down, and I will read it again in the morning, and I will still not know what it means."

"Write what you saw."

"I wrote it down already."

"Then write it again in the morning, when you are not cold." Lysette held the book against her knees, and she looked at the dark cover as if it might have changed. "I have written six lines tonight, and not one has a name."

"I did." Lysette looked out at the city, where the palace lamps made a row of yellow squares. "I wrote: a dark shape and a gold light. I crossed out the dark and I could not think of a better word, so I put it back." She turned to him. "Why did you cross it out?"

"Because I could not see it either."

The wind came over the wall and moved the loose hair across her face. She pushed it back with one finger, and then she sat with her chin up. Eden stood beside the bench instead of sitting down, because her gown took most of it.

The band below changed to a slow tune, and the violin came in late. The drum kept the beat alone for six steps. Lysette listened for a moment, and then she stood up.

"Dance with me," she said.

"I do not dance."

"You told Seraphine that as well."

"She asked as a joke."

"This is not a joke." Lysette held out one hand, palm down, with her fingers straight. "You are wearing a coat and boots, and there is no band up here, and nobody can see us from the hall. The glass below was dark where the lamps did not reach. It is only a roof and two people who cannot dance."

"Seraphine will see us if she comes up."

"Seraphine is dancing with Felix, and Felix is counting under his breath, and neither of them will stop for anything." She kept her hand out. "One tune."

Eden put his hand in hers, and it was small and cold at the fingers. He kept his gaze on the low wall, not on her face. She put her other hand on his shoulder and stepped close, and he stepped back, and she stepped after him.

"You are on my foot," he said.

"On purpose," Lysette said.

They moved across the gravel in four slow steps and then stopped, because neither of them knew the tune. The band carried on below them, and the smoke had gone. The palace lamps kept their row of squares along the wall. Lysette's hand was still on his shoulder, and she did not take it away. Eden kept his feet where she put them.

"That is the worst dance in the history of the academy," she said.

"Yes," Eden said.

"I have wanted to do that since November."

"Since the roof?"

"Since the morning after," she said. "You were standing at the gate with your coat buttoned wrong, and you asked if I had slept. I wanted to tell you to go and sleep yourself. Instead I asked if you were cold."

"You did."

"You said no, and you stood there with your hands in your pockets. I wrote a line about it that night."

"Since the first day you gave me a small cloth and asked if the light had lit." She looked at his coat pocket, and then at his face, and then away. "I have kept it in my pocket every day since, and I never washed it."

Eden stopped moving. "I thought you would wash it and give it back."

"I told you I would wash it, and I did not say I would give it back." Her fingers tightened once on his shoulder and then loosened. ""It has a stain."

"I know."

"You said you would wash it."

"I know." She looked at him steadily. "I am going to wash it, and then you will have no reason to ask for it back."

It is a poor small cloth, and it is mine now."

The band below began the slow tune again from the top, and they stood in the middle of the roof garden. The gravel crunched under their boots when the wind pushed them. After a while Lysette took her hand from his shoulder and stepped back one pace. She kept the other hand in his until the tune ended.

"Go to sleep," she said. "You look worse than the roof."

"I sleep here."

"Not tonight." She pointed at the bench with the silver shoes, and then at the flat space beside the herb bed. The straw had been laid down there. "The straw is dry, and I am too tired to climb the stair twice."

Eden looked at the straw, and then at her, and then at the sky. The stars were clear, and the city below had gone quiet in patches. He was colder than he had been an hour before.

"What about the small cloth?" he asked.

"You will find it in the morning," Lysette said, and she sat down on the bench and put her shoes back on. "I keep my promises in my own time."

"That is a lawyer's answer," she said, and she picked up her book and walked to the stair. "I will see you at breakfast, and do not be late."

"I am never late," Eden said.

"You are always late," Lysette said, and she went down without waiting for him.

He lay down on the straw with his coat over him and his boots still on, and he looked at the sky through the thin herb bed frame. He listened to the band while it finished the tune. Lysette sat on the bench and wrote two lines in her black book. She did not read them aloud, and she did not look at him again.

The war dream did not come. The board at the edge of nothing did not appear, and the Dragon did not complain about the lower gates. No banners moved in a sky without air. He slept until the bell struck six. The straw had kept the warmth of the day in it, and his coat was dry when he opened his eyes. The roof smelled of mint and cold stone. and when he woke the roof was grey with light and the city below had started its morning trams.

A blackbird stood on the herb bed frame with its head on one side. The straw beside him was pressed flat where Lysette had sat. Two grey gloves hung from a branch of the apple tree with their fingers down, and the morning light had caught one edge.

Eden got up and walked to the tree. The mint bed had been watered overnight, and the string between the posts held a line of small drops, and a snail had crossed the path in the night and left a silver line. Eden looked at all of it, and he knew that she had been up before him, because the straw beside the tank had been smoothed by her hand.

The gloves were hers, and they were still damp at the cuffs. He took them down before the wind carried them over the wall. Something white showed in the right-hand glove, and he turned it over. A folded square of cloth lay in the palm of the leather.

It was his mother's small cloth, and the silver feather in one corner was clean and bright, and the brown stain from November was gone. It had been washed and folded along the old creases, and it smelled of soap and the mint from the herb bed. One stitch at a corner had come loose, and she had mended it with a different thread, and the new thread was white against the old grey. He put his thumb on the stitch and followed it to the end, and then he folded the cloth again along her creases.

Eden held it in both hands for a while, and then he put it in his coat pocket. He carried the gloves to the bench where the black book had been, and there was no book there now. Lysette was gone, and only the trams were coming up the hill. He stood with the gloves in one hand and the small cloth in the other. He looked at the bench, the water tank, and the herb bed where he had slept. The apple tree had dropped three small apples in the night, and he picked one up and put it in his pocket beside the cloth.

The stair was cold and the top banister had come loose. He put the apple in his pocket beside the cloth, and he went down the stair with the gloves in his hand, and he did not look back at the tree.
