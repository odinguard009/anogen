# Chapter 27: The Reading

The morning paper came off the steam press at six. The boys outside held it up before the ink was dry. Eden came out of the kitchen stair and saw the front page over a older student's shoulder. Three lines covered the Ball, and one headline said the Vigil had acted without law.

He took a copy from the pile and read it at the gate. The press in the print shop had a steam whistle that went off at six, and the boys were still laughing at it. The girl who sold buns had wrapped three copies around her tray. The paper said fourteen armed Crowns had held the east hall, and one person in black had cleared it. That person had left before the guards arrived. It said the king would ask questions. It did not say that a chandelier had gone, or that a grafted woman sat on the stage. It did not say that forty people had walked out of the hall on their own feet.

On the third page a column with a black border remembered the "Hollen affair of PR 110." It gave no detail, and said the Seal had closed the file. Eden read the year twice. He counted his pulse after the second reading because the stomach below his ribs had gone cold. The count was eighty-one, and the morning average was sixty-eight. He wrote both numbers in his notebook and put the paper in the bin. A older student asked why he had bought a paper if he was going to throw it away. Eden said he had wanted to read the year. The older student asked which year, and Eden told him, and the older student walked away.

Lysette was already at the gate with a paper of her own, and she had folded the third page inward so that the black border did not show. Lysette Hollen saw him look at the fold, and she folded it once more.

"Is it true?" she asked.

"The paper says the hall was cleared."

"That is not what I asked."

"I do not know what else it says." Eden looked at the fold in her hand. "Why did you fold it?"

"Because I have read that column before, and I know what it leaves out." Lysette put the paper under her arm. "My mother writes to me twice a year, and she never mentions my brother. Then a paper writes a column about a year and a file, and neither says a name. That is how a house stays quiet."

"Does it work?"

"No," she said. "I have not been quiet once since I learned to read."

Valerius came past them with his papers in a folder and his cut cheek covered with a strip of cloth. He looked at Eden, and then at Lysette, and he did not speak to either of them, and he walked on.

"He is angry," Lysette said.

"Yes."

"Because you held back in the duel?"

"No," Eden said. "Because I was right about holding back."

* * *

Finals ran for four days, and the rooms were hot with ink and paper. The first exam was on the three parts of a simple spell, and the second on the sixteen orders and their seal colours. The third was a problem in mana engineering with a lamp that would not light. Eden wrote in the same hand he used for the nap record and checked each page twice. He left ten minutes early while the rest of the year was still writing. Eden wrote the same kind of answers he had written all year: short, plain, and complete, with the sum shown beside the answer when there was a sum. Okoru walked between the desks twice and stopped at Eden's paper, and he tapped it with one finger, and then he walked on.

On Friday the board went up with the ranks in black ink, and the crowd stood before it with their bags. Nobody stood close to the first three sheets. Eden came out of the stair with Lysette beside him and waited at the back while Rowan read his number out loud to anyone who would listen.

"Three!" Rowan said, and then he read the line again. "I moved up. I moved up two places."

"Yes," Clara said, and she smiled without looking at the board.

Felix came out of the crowd with his folder under his arm, and he found Eden by the wall. "Eaten today?" he asked.

"Yes," Eden said, and it was true.

"Good." Felix looked at the board. "Did you see yours?"

"Not yet."

"Then come and see it."

Eden walked to the board with the others and read down the fifth sheet until he found his name. The number after it was eight, and there was a line under the number that had not been there in the term before. Under his name, in smaller letters, the register had printed two words. The first was **[Knight]**. The second was **[Black Lancer]**. He read the two words once, and then he looked away and put the folder under his arm.

Lysette had found her own name on the fourth sheet, and she stood very still while she read it, and then she shut her folder with a snap. "Rank four," she said. "I will take it."

"You should take it," Felix said. "You worked for it."

"So did he," Lysette said, and she looked at Eden, and then at the board, and then away again, and she did not say what else she had worked for.

Some first-years had copied the two words under Eden's name into their notebooks, and one asked what a Black Lancer was. Okoru said it was a reading on a board, and the first-year wrote that down as well.

Okoru stood at the end of the board with his stick under his arm. "Read it and go," he said. "The reading is on the page, and it is not a message from anybody. The board has no opinions."

"My board has three opinions," Rowan said, and Okoru told him to go and carry Clara's bag, and Rowan went, and Clara let him.

* * *

That night the roof garden was quiet, and the apple tree had six apples left after the birds took the rest. Eden sat on the bench with his notebook open, and Lysette came up the stair with a small square of paper in her hand and sat down beside him.

She put the paper on her knee and unfolded it, and then she folded it again.

"I opened it," she said.

"The locket?"

"Yes." She held the locket out on its chain, and the little oval lay open in her palm, with the lid turned back. "I opened it last night after you went to sleep, and then I shut it again, and I wore it today under my collar so that nobody would see."

Eden looked at the portrait. It showed a young man with a straight nose and a face that was neither boy nor man. His hair fell over one eyebrow, and he was not smiling.

"Is that your brother?" Eden asked.

"Yes." Lysette shut the locket and held it in her fist. "He was twenty when they took the picture, and he had just come home from court, and my mother made him sit in the chair for an hour because she wanted the light right. He complained the whole time, and he would not take his coat off, and you can see the silver buttons on one side."

"I can."

"I looked at it for a long time last night, and I was afraid I would feel something I could not carry to breakfast." She put the locket back under her collar. "This morning I could carry it, so it was all right."

The wind moved the apple tree, and one apple fell and rolled across the gravel and stopped against the bench. Eden looked at the tree for eleven seconds, and he counted them, and then he looked back at her.

"Why did you look at the tree?" Lysette asked.

"Because the apples are still there."

"They would be there anyway."

"Not if the birds took them."

Lysette laughed, one short note, and she put her notebook on the bench between them. "You are the only person who can answer a question and say nothing at the same time," she said. "I have decided that I like it, and I will not ask you to stop. She leaned back against the post and looked at the city, and the wind pushed a loose strand across her face. She did not brush it back, and Eden did not reach out. He kept his hands in his pockets and counted the lamps along the palace wall, and there were twenty-two."

"I will try to be clearer."

"No," she said. "Do not."

They sat until the tower bell struck nine, and then Lysette went down the stair with her book under her arm and her head up, and the locket shut under her collar. Eden stayed on the bench and wrote one line in his notebook: "Rank eight. Read once." He put the notebook in his coat and went down after her. The hall was dark and the corridors were empty, and the portrait of an old queen on the stair had a crack through one eye that he had never noticed. He stopped and looked at it, and then he went on, and he counted the steps because a stair was a thing with a number.

* * *

The summons came on Saturday morning in a grey envelope with a Seal mark at the corner. Eden opened it at the gate, and the paper inside was short. The Justiciar Seal had opened an inquiry into an unknown knight on Ball night, and the first hearing was on Monday. Under the line a clerk had typed the words "please attend," and a hand had added a small mark beside them that Eden did not recognise.

Ysolde read the letter in her office and put it face down on the desk. "It is not an inquiry into you," she said. "It is an inquiry into a person they cannot name, and they want to see who comes through the door."

"Should I go?"

"No." She looked at him over her cards. "You were told to stay away from the Ball, and you went. That is one thing you did not do as you were told. Do not make a habit of it. She wrote a line in her book and underlined it, and then she turned the book so that he could see: 'Ball night - attended.'"

"I will stay away."

"Good." Ysolde turned a card over and then put it back in the deck. "And take your small cloth with you. You left it on the roof twice this week, and the second time a girl carried it down."

Eden put his hand in his coat pocket, and the cloth was there, folded along Lysette's creases. "I did not leave it the second time," he said.

"No," Ysolde said. "You gave it to her and you did not know that you had."

He went out through the side door with the grey letter in his pocket, and the city was grey and cold, and the trams were running on time. A man at the corner was selling hot chestnuts, and the smoke went straight up in the still air, and Eden bought a paper cone because it was the only thing he had done that morning that nobody could write down. At the gate of the academy a group of first-years were reading the paper again, and the headline about the lawless knight had been pinned to the board with four brass pins.

He ate one on the step. The headline was four words long, and Eden read it once, and then he walked on with the grey letter in his pocket.
