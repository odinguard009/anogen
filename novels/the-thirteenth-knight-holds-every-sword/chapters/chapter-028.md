# Chapter 28: The Loud House

The veinline train left Solmarch at eight and took the north rail by nine. Felix pressed his face to the window for the whole first hour. Felix Ashgrove had never been on a veinline train before, and he asked about every town they passed. The engine had a deep hum that came up through the floor, and the vein-steel rails sang at every join. The towns along the line went past in rows of grey roofs. The hum came from a core in the engine and kept ice off the rails in winter. Eden said it had stopped once when he was small and the train sat in the snow for six hours while the Duke wrote a song.

Eden sat with his coat folded on his knee and his boots on the bag under the seat. He had slept on the roof the night before and he intended to sleep through the visit if the house would let him. He knew it would not.

"Your family is coming to meet us at the station," Felix said.

"Yes."

"How many?"

"There will be five at the platform and three in the carriage, and one of them will be a horse that nobody has introduced me to yet."

Felix looked at him. "Is it a trap?" he asked. "No," Eden said. "It is a house." He looked at him. "You have been here before."

"Once, when I was thirteen, and I did not speak to anyone."

"Why not?"

Eden looked out at the fields. "I was told not to. My father told me, because I was to be a commoner until I chose not to be, and a commoner could not know a duke. That is the whole of it, and it is not a riddle. I will answer what I can while we are here."

The station master had put out a carpet for the Duke and then taken it back, because the Duke had already crossed it in his slippers. The Duke laughed at the man's face. Felix laughed because the Duke laughed.

At the station a footman in grey livery opened the carriage door, and a small woman with a ladle took Eden's bag before he could lift it. "You have lost weight," she said, and she hit his coat with the ladle, once, on the shoulder. "Greta will fix that."

"Greta," Eden said, and he smiled, and the woman's face went red with pleasure, and she pretended to box his ear with the ladle and missed on purpose.

A tall man in a coat with the crest on the pocket came down the step. He picked Felix up by the elbow with one hand and set him three feet to the left. "That is the step, Felix Ashgrove, and it is wide enough for two people, so stand where I put you."

"Bertram," Eden said.

"Young master," the butler said, and he said it only when they were alone, but Felix was close enough to hear, and he did not ask about it yet.

The Duke came last in slippers with silver crests and a beard combed into two points. He caught Eden in both arms and lifted him off the platform. "The Lad Who Napped Through a Siege!" he boomed, and three footmen winced together. "There is a new verse about the Button War, and Greta has forbidden me to sing it in the kitchen, so I will sing it in the carriage."

"No," Eden said.

"No?" the Duke said, and he looked wounded, and then he winked at Felix as if the two of them were sharing a joke. "He is very stern, and I have written six songs about his sternness."

The carriage road ran along the lake, and the water was grey and flat. The driver took the corners at a speed that made the plates rattle. Lunch was served while they moved: soup, then fish, then bread pudding. Felix ate two portions, because nobody in that carriage would ask him to stop.

Greta sat beside the fish tureen with her ladle across her knees and watched the Duke pour tea into Eden's cup. "That is my cup to pour," she said.

"I poured it first," the Duke said.

"You poured it badly."

"It is tea, woman, not a treaty."

Bertram came in from the front seat with a small pot in his hand, and he put it beside the Duke's cup without a word. The Duke looked at the pot, and then at Bertram, and then at Eden. "That is a better tea, and I resent it," he said, and the two went on being rivals for the rest of the road. Greta poured a second cup and put it in front of Eden without looking at the Duke, and the Duke poured a third and put it beside the second. "You are both competing over a boy who does not drink tea," Greta said. "He drinks it when I pour," the Duke said. "He drinks it when anyone pours. That is not the same thing. "I am going to write a song about the tea," the Duke said. "Please do not," Eden said. "That is the second thing you have asked me not to do today," the Duke said, and he looked pleased."

The carriage stopped so suddenly that the fish plates slid. The driver put his head through the glass and said there was something in the road, and Bertram said that he had seen it, and the Duke asked whether it was large. Bertram said it was three men and a gun, and the Duke put down his spoon.

Bertram put down his cup. "Bandits," he said, and he was already out of the door.

A man in a brown coat stood in the road with a short gun, and two more stood by the trees. The one in the road held out a hand for the horses. The Duke put down his spoon.

"Do we stop?" Felix asked.

"Greta," the Duke said, "is the soup finished?"

"Almost," Greta said, and she lifted the ladle.

The bandit raised the pistol, and it went click, and then click again. He looked at the gun with his mouth open. Bertram stood beside him with a hand on the man's sleeve. His boots were muddy to the knee, and no one had seen him leave the carriage.

"Twice," Bertram said, and he said it to the gun, and then he took it out of the man's hand and broke it over his knee and gave the pieces back.

The Duke stood up with his napkin still in his hand. "Gentlemen, you have chosen a road at a bad hour, and my cook has not finished her soup. Wait there until she has, and then Bertram will explain the rest to you."

The three bandits waited. Greta served the soup, and one man began to cry into his bowl. The Duke asked him whether he had a mother, and the man said that he did, and the Duke said that was a good thing. "Are you going to leave them tied to the tree?" Felix asked. "No," the Duke said. "Bertram will cut them loose in an hour, and by then the road will have taught them a lesson." "What lesson?" "That a pistol which misfires twice is a sign." "Are they paid?" Eden asked. "Yes," Bertram said. "By someone who knew the hour the carriage would pass, and that is the part I do not like. "They will freeze," Felix said. "It is December." "They will be warm enough by the time Bertram cuts them loose," the Duke said. "Bertram?" "Yes, sir." "How long?" "An hour," Bertram said, and he looked at the clock."

By the time the soup was finished, the bandits were tied to a tree with their own belts, and their gun lay in three pieces in the road. Bertram came back into the carriage and sat down, and his boots left mud on the floor.

"You were outside for eleven minutes," Eden said.

"I was checking the road," Bertram said.

"You had mud on your boots at dawn as well. "You saw that?" "I see a lot." "Then you know I went to the village." "Yes." Bertram said nothing for a moment, and then he said, "A man there was asking about the trains north, and I wished to hear his name before he heard ours.""

Bertram looked at the mud on his boots and then out of the window. "The road is wet in the morning," he said, and he folded his hands, and that was the end of that answer.

Felix had not said a word during the whole thing, and his eyes had gone from the bandits to Bertram and then to the Duke, and then back to his soup.

At Silverfell the house was warm and loud from the door inward, and the twins Pim and Odessa raced down the stair to take Felix's bag. They argued about which of them had reached him first. A bell in the hall rang once while the Duke was laughing at something Greta said. Eden stopped on the step, because he had never heard that bell before, and the sound went on after everything else went quiet.

"New bell?" he asked.

"Old bell," Bertram said. "It rings when the Duke laughs, and only then." He looked at Eden's face. "It has done that since you were small."

At supper the Duke raised his glass and said something about a son who had grown into a fine quiet man, and then he started a second sentence and got as far as "your mother had w..." before Greta's ladle fell off the table and hit the floor with a crash that made the glasses jump. The Duke looked at the ladle, and then at Greta, and then at his glass, and he said, The ladle is tired, and went back to his fish. "I have not said anything," the Duke added. "No," Greta said, and she picked up the ladle. "That is what worries me."

Eden put down his knife and looked at the table. Pim and Odessa had stopped talking at the same moment, and Bertram was pouring wine into a glass that was already full. The Duke's beard had gone still in the middle of a sentence.

Felix looked down at his plate, and then up at Eden, and then at the adults who were all looking at their plates.

Felix ate honey-rye bread at the kitchen table and told Greta that the bandits had been very polite about it, and Greta said that she had noticed, and that was why she had given him a second piece. He took the second piece and ate it slowly, and nobody watched.

Later, when the house was quiet, Eden sat on the window seat in the east room with the window open and the lake air coming in cold. The lake was black, and a lamp on the far shore moved once and stopped, and a fish jumped in the dark and made a ring that Eden watched until it was gone. Felix put his coat on the seat beside him and looked out at the water. His face was calm in and Eden had learned to read, which meant that he had a question and had not decided how to ask it. Below them a cart went along the lake road with two lamps, and the lamps made a pair of yellow dots that moved slowly and stopped at a gate. "Is your father always like this?" Felix asked. "No," Eden said. "This is a quiet day." Felix looked at him to see whether it was a joke, and Eden looked back, and neither moved for a while. Felix came and stood beside him with a coat over his arm.

"Your family is very loud," Felix said, "and you are very quiet. Which one is the act?" He waited.
