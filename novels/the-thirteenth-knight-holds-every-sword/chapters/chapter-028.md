# Chapter 28: The House at Seven

> "Silverfell serves dinner at seven, assassins at eight." - House Argentine staff saying

The train to Silverfell ran on a veinline, which was a line of mana laid under the ground, and the engine hummed instead of rattling because the rails were vein-steel. Felix put his hand on the window and felt the hum through the glass.

"I did not know trains could be quiet," he said.

"They are not quiet. They are steady." Eden kept his book shut on his knee. "The noise is under the floor."

"That is worse." Felix took his hand away. "I like a train that tells me where it is."

The engine hummed for two hours through grey fields, and then the fields became hills and the hills became snow. A woman in the next seat knitted a scarf the colour of a roof tile and counted her stitches under her breath, and when the train crossed a bridge she stopped counting and crossed herself, then began again at the same number. Felix watched her until she noticed, then looked out the window at the snow. At the station a manservant in a black coat waited with a carriage and did not seem surprised that the train was late or early or whatever it had been. He put Eden's bag on the carriage without touching it with both hands, which was the first odd thing about him.

"Bertram," he said, to nobody in particular.

"Eden," Eden said.

"Yes." Bertram's face did not change. "The Duke is in the west room. Dinner is at seven. The bandits are on the carriage road."

Felix looked up from the window. "Bandits?"

"Yes, sir." Bertram shut the carriage door. "Two cartloads. They have been there since dawn."

* * *

Silverfell was a wide stone house with a roof that had too many chimneys and a hall that could hold a small army standing at ease. The hall had a row of portraits along one wall and a rack of old spears along the other, and a long table in the middle that nobody used because the family preferred the small room. A fire burned in the hall grate even though the house was warm, and two dogs slept in front of it, one lying across the other's back. The Duke came out of the west room as they entered: a big man in a brown coat with his hair standing up at the back and a smile that arrived before the rest of him.

"There you are!" he said, and put one hand on Eden's shoulder and one on Felix's, and pulled them both into the hall. "Felix, this is my house. Eden, this is also my house. Nobody is to be formal until after the fish."

"My lord," Bertram said from the door.

"Bertram, you are allowed to be formal. You are the only one who does it properly." The Duke turned to a woman in a grey dress who had come in from the kitchen with a ladle in her hand. "Greta, the boy has brought a friend."

"He is not a boy," Greta said. "He is eighteen and thin."

"He is my boy." The Duke's smile went softer. "Is the soup on?"

"It is on. The bandits are on the road." Greta looked at Bertram. "Are they still on the road?"

"They were at dawn," Bertram said. "I went out to look. Their horses were muddy."

Eden looked at Bertram's boots. They were polished to a shine, and there was dry mud at the seam where the leather met the sole, just above the heel. Bertram saw him looking and put one foot behind the other.

"The mud is not on the boots," he said.

"No."

"Good." Bertram moved the foot again. "It is not."

Dinner was served in the small dining room because the Duke said the big one made him feel like a duke and he had enough of that at court. Soup first, then fish, and a dish of potatoes that Greta put in the middle of the table with the authority of someone who had made them. Felix sat beside Eden with his book on his lap and did not open it, which was how he said he was listening.

A servant came in during the fish. "My lord, the bandits have stopped the carriage road."

"Have they?" The Duke put down his fork. "Are they hurting anyone?"

"No, my lord. They have stopped the road and asked for money."

"Then they are doing it wrong." The Duke stood up. "Bring me my coat."

Greta put the ladle down. "You are not going out there."

"I am going to speak to them."

"You will invite them in."

"Only if they are polite." The Duke went to the hall and came back with a coat over his arm, and he was humming as he put it on, one note over and over, and at the third note a bell in the chapel rang once by itself.

Everyone stopped. The Duke stopped humming too, and looked at the ceiling as if the bell had been a servant with a message.

"Did that happen before?" Felix asked.

"No," Eden said.

"Ah." Felix put his book on the table. "I will remember that."

The Duke laughed, one big laugh that filled the room, and went out to the carriage road with Bertram behind him.

* * *

The bandits were three men on tired horses with a cart across the road. They had a short gun and a long knife and the expressions of people who had been doing this for a week and were already bored with it. The Duke walked up to them with his hands open and Bertram stood two steps behind with his hands folded.

"Good evening," the Duke said. "You have chosen a bad road. It leads to my house and my house is difficult to leave."

"Money," one of them said.

"Of course." The Duke took a purse from his coat and held it out. "But first, are you hungry? We are eating fish."

"There is soup as well," a servant said, from behind the Duke.

"There is soup," the Duke agreed. "I am not making a threat. I am offering soup."

The man with the knife looked at the house, then at the cart, then at the road going uphill into the snow. "We have eaten," he said.

"Then you will be gone before the fish goes cold."

The man with the gun looked at the purse, then at the Duke, then at the gun, and pulled the trigger because that was what he had been told to do if a man came close with a purse. The gun made a small clicking sound and did nothing. He pulled it again. Nothing. Bertram's mouth moved, which might have been a smile and was not one, and he reached out and took the gun from the man's hand with two fingers on the barrel.

"Twice," Bertram said. "That is a poor habit."

The man with the knife dismounted and came at the Duke, and the Duke caught his wrist in one hand and turned him toward the cart, where the third man was trying to untangle the horse's reins. Greta came out of the house with her ladle still in her hand and stood on the step, and the man with the knife looked at her, decided the step was not worth the trouble, and sat down on the cart.

"Nobody is hurt," the Duke said, to the road in general. "That is the important thing." He gave the purse to the man with the gun, who took it without looking at it. "Go home. The road is icy past the bridge."

They went. The cart was still across the road, so Bertram moved it, one end and then the other, and the Duke came back into the house rubbing his hands and asking whether the fish was ruined.

"Fish does not care about bandits," Greta said. "Sit down."

"I will sit down." He sat. "Tell me about school. Tell me everything. Has Eden been eating?"

Felix, across the table, opened his mouth to say something and then did not. Eden kept his eyes on his plate. The Duke looked from one boy to the other and put a piece of fish on Eden's plate without being asked.

"Your mother had w—" he said, and stopped in the middle of the word, because Greta's ladle hit the side of a dish and made a sound like a small bell. The Duke looked at the ladle. Greta looked at the tablecloth. Bertram, standing at the door with the gun in his hand, put the gun behind his back.

"Had what?" Felix asked.

"Warm feet," the Duke said. "Your mother had warm feet. She always sat on my side of the fire." He took a breath and began to tell Felix a long story about a winter when the roof had leaked, and the story went on until the tea came and then past it.

The tea war started when Greta said the Duke had made it too strong and the Duke said it was the right strength for a man who had just faced three bandits, and Greta said three bandits on tired horses did not count as a day's work, and the Duke said they did if you included the walking to the road and back. Felix took a side and then changed it when Greta refilled his cup. Eden drank his tea and said nothing at all, which Bertram noted by taking the cup away before it was empty and bringing it back full.

* * *

Felix found Eden at the window of the east room after dinner with the snow coming down past the glass. The room had a narrow bed, a desk with a drawer that did not close, and a washstand with a pitcher that had a chip in it shaped like a moon. A candle burned on the mantel because the household had decided the east room was cold, and the flame leaned toward the door every time someone walked past in the hall. The house was still loud behind them: the Duke arguing with Greta about the tea, a servant laughing at something Bertram had said in a voice too low for anyone to hear.

"Your family is very loud," Felix said. "You are very quiet. Which one is the act?"

Eden watched the snow hit the window and fall away. "Both."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only one I have." He turned from the window. Somewhere below them the Duke began a story about a winter and a leaking roof, and his laugh went up through the floors and hit the ceiling and came back quieter, and Felix put his book in his pocket because there was no point reading with that much noise in the house.
