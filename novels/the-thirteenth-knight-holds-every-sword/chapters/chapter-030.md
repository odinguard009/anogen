# Chapter 30: The Letter

That house was loud at breakfast and quiet by ten. A fire in the study had been laid too high. The Duke stood with his back to the fire until his coat was warm, and then moved away. Warmth made him want to sit down. and the Duke sat in the small study with the bandit's orders spread flat on the desk. A page had been torn from a household ledger, and the writing was a careful hand with a hook at the end. That paper listed the hour the carriage would pass the north road and the number of men to stop it.

"Two men would have been enough," the Duke said. "He sent three, and one of them had a pistol that did not work. That is a message from a man who has never had to use a pistol himself."

Bertram stood by the door with his hands behind his back. "The coachman says the road was wet, and the pistol was wet as well."

"Then the man who wrote this did not know about the rain." The Duke put the paper down and looked at it. "He knew the hour, and he did not know the weather. That is a person who reads a timetable and stays indoors."

"A person in this city," Bertram said. "Or a person who has never been in this city," the Duke said. "The road is named, and the hour is written in the academy's own clock time. A stranger would have to be told that." A finger tapped the paper. "Whoever wrote this has been close enough to read a school timetable and far enough away to miss the rain."

"Or a person with a friend who sends the hour by letter." The Duke turned the page over and read the other side, which was blank. "Where did you find the man who paid them?"

"In the village, and he was gone by the time I reached the inn." Bertram put a scrap of cloth on the desk. "He left his glove."

The Duke looked at the glove without touching it. It was grey wool with a seam at the thumb, and there was a small mark embroidered on the cuff. The colour was so pale it was almost white. His own hand went near it and then away.

"Another hour for the thread," he said. "And then burn it." "The seam is cut on a machine," Bertram said. "There are two in the village, and one belongs to a man who mends the school mail bags." "Then do not burn it until I have seen the man." "Yes, sir." Bertram folded the glove in a square of cloth and put it in his pocket."

"Yes, sir."

A second letter had come from the academy in a thick envelope with the school crest on the back. The Duke opened it at the study door and read it twice before sitting down. It was a form about attendance, and it listed sixty-one absences. A vice-school head whose name he knew had signed it.

"Strent," the Duke said. "Halvard Strent graded my son's essays for two years and wrote me three letters. In all three he said how clever the boy was." His eye went over the line again. "Now he signs a form with sixty-one absences and no note about an exemption. He sends it to a father not named on the Wren file. "It went to my town house," the Duke said. "Not here, where the staff would see the crest, and not to the academy office, where a clerk would file it. Somebody chose an empty room in a house I use four times a year." "To be read by you," Bertram said. "Or by whoever opens my post when I am not there," the Duke said."

"Perhaps the academy does not know who you are," Bertram said.

"The academy knows exactly who I am, and a clerk bowed when he gave the form to me." The Duke folded the letter along its crease. "Somebody wanted me to read a number, and wanted me to read it from a man's hand I recognise."

Then he stood up and walked to the window, and the lake was grey and flat. A boat with one oar was moving across it in a slow circle. That morning he had rowed the boat, and he had been happy in it. The happiness lasted until he came back to the house and found the two papers.

"A father is a useful thing to have," the Duke said, and his voice was quiet now. The staff did not hear it often. "A father reads a number and thinks his son is in trouble. Then the son says he is fine, and the father wants to believe him, and so he does. That is the whole trick of it."

Bertram said nothing.

"Open the Wren file," the Duke said.

Bertram took a key from his pocket and crossed to a cabinet in the corner. With the key he opened a drawer and took out a thin folder with a grey label. Bertram put it on the desk and stepped back.

The Duke opened it. The thin file held a paid school place letter, a page of marks, a medical exemption, and one form with a palace stamp. There was no birth page and no family page and no note about a mother. The guardian line said only Household in a typed hand that did not care.

He read the file twice, as he had read the form, and then a third time. There was a line in the academy letter that he had skipped.

One line was in a smaller hand at the bottom of the second page, under the signature: "Please note that the boy's late mother was never entered on the school register. The Committee would be grateful for the name to complete its record."

Carefully he put the letter down, so that the corner did not bend, and sat for a while. He had read the line four times, and each time the small hand had written the same thing. Each time the word mother seemed placed there for him and nobody else. For eleven years he had kept a promise, and he had kept it badly except in the letter of it. Not once had he said her name in a room where his son could hear. He had songs about boot polish and a beard, but not six words about the woman who made the helm.

"Bertram," he said. "How long has the staff known about the letter?"

"We know what the household knows," Bertram said. "Greta saw the crest and put it on your desk, and Pim read the address, and Odessa saw your face. They have not discussed it."

"They will discuss it by supper."

"Yes, sir."

He put the grey letter beside the open file and read the two hands again. They were not the same.

"Who wrote the line at the bottom of this?" he asked.

"Not Strent," Bertram said. "He writes a heavy hand, and this one leans left, and the letters are small."

"Then somebody added it after he signed." The Duke shut the file and put his hand on it. "A person who knew the register would know we never gave a mother's name. Someone who wanted that name would write it in a small hand and hope a father answered."

"A trap," Bertram said.

"Yes, or a door." The Duke stood up. "A trap is a thing you walk into, and a door is a thing you choose. I would like to know which one this is before I answer."

He put the file back in the cabinet and locked it, then took the key out and put it in his coat. He had never been good at leaving a locked thing where it belonged.

"Where is the helm?" he asked.

"In the chapel, sir, where you left it last night. You asked for an hour alone with it, and the staff have kept the door."

The Duke walked out of the study and across the hall into the cold chapel, and closed the door behind him. The helm sat on a stand before the altar in its open case, and its silver caught the one burning lamp. The case smelled of oil and old iron. Liora had made the silver and hammered a small ridge into the brow. She said a helm should have a place for a hand. He had spent eleven years learning to put his thumb there without looking.

He did not put it on. He stood before it with his hands behind his back and hummed a tune with four notes down and one up. The tune filled the chapel and emptied it, and an hour went by without a word. He thought about the line on the form and the boy who had written nothing in eleven years, and about the word mother. He hummed the four notes down and the one note up, and then again. The tune was the only sound in the chapel apart from the lamp, and it went out through the open door.

Outside, the yard had gone quiet, and a horse stamped once in the stable, and a bird moved in the ivy. Bertram did not knock again, because he had been told to wait an hour, and he counted an hour and the Duke did.

At the end of that hour Bertram opened the door, and the Duke stood where he had been left with his hat in his hand and a calm face. That lamp had burned low while he stood there, and the silver gave back a thin line of light. There was a mark on the stone where his boot had turned an hour before and stayed.

"The horses are ready," the Duke said, and then he saw the map on the desk and asked for it. Bertram put the form in his coat, and the Duke walked out without looking at the helm again." the Duke said.

"Which horse, sir?"

The Duke looked back at the helm, then at the door, then at the butler. He gave the tired smile the household knew better than to trust.

"The one that can reach Solmarch before the gates close," he said. "And bring me a map of the academy roads. I have never liked the look of that form."

The north road was to be watched, he said, and the academy gates checked at dusk. Then he sat down at the desk and began to write a letter that he would not send in the end. Four lines went on the page and three were crossed out, and the fourth said a father must know his son's timetable. A father who did not know it was no use to anyone. He put the letter in the drawer and locked it, but he left the key in the lock. He had never done that before.

The hall clock struck four while he was writing, and the house went on being loud around him. He did not look up until the ink was dry.

He read one line twice and put the paper face down on the desk, and he did it twice more before he was sure. The words would stay where he had put them. A clock ticked in the study and the fire settled, and the lake kept its grey surface. Nothing moved except the smoke above the lamp. He took off his slippers and put on his boots slowly. When Bertram came back he was ready, and that was the first thing that morning which looked like a decision.

Bertram held the door while he passed, and the stable yard was bright with cold light, and the horse was brought around with its reins over a groom's arm. The Duke took the reins and put one foot in the stirrup, and then he stopped and asked for the map again, and Bertram went back for it without a word.
