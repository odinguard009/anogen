# Chapter 30: The Boy's Late Mother

> "A ballad is a lie told so that a heart can say the truth." - Duke Aldous Argentine, unpublished verse

The boys left on Tuesday morning, and the house went quiet in the way a house goes quiet when it has been loud on purpose. Greta took the guest sheets off the line and folded them twice. The two dogs lay in the hall with their ears flat. There was no one at the door to come in.

Duke Aldous had written a ballad about the visit, called "The Lad Who Napped Through a Siege". He had copied it four times and kept one copy in his coat. Now he sat in the small dining room with two papers in front of him and a cup of tea that had gone cold. When he was thinking seriously, the room seemed to lose a little warmth, and Bertram had learned to put an extra log on the fire before the Duke asked for one.

The first paper had been taken from the bandit with the knife. It was a half-sheet with four lines on it, written in a hand that pressed hard into the page.

Stop the carriage on the north road. Do not hurt the boy. Search the bags for letters. Wait for the signal.

At the bottom someone had drawn a mark like a gate with one post missing. The paper was folded along one crease so often that it had begun to split. Somebody had carried it a long way and had not wanted to lose it. Aldous read the lines twice, which was not like him. He usually read a thing once and made a joke about it.

"The road was chosen," he said.

Bertram stood by the door with his hands folded. "Yes, my lord."

"Not the house. The road. They knew which road the boys would take and at what hour. And they were told not to hurt the boy." Aldous put the paper flat. "Who knows my carriage road?"

"The household, my lord. The stablemaster. The cook. And your guests from the last month. There were eleven."

"Eleven." Aldous closed his eyes briefly, as a man does when he counts people he is fond of and wishes the number were smaller. "A bandit writes his name or he does not write at all. This mark is for a reader who knows who sent it."

"Then the reader is the one who paid."

"Yes." Aldous folded the half-sheet along the crease. "And that reader also told them not to hurt the boy. It is the kindest line in the whole paper, and it is the one I like least."

"And the school," he said, touching the second paper. "That is the other trouble."

The second paper had come in the post the day before Eden arrived. It had the academy's stamp in the corner. It was a form about Eden's absences, and it was signed at the bottom by Halvard Strent, the vice-headmaster.

"Sixty-one absences," Aldous said. "A boy of eighteen who sleeps in a garden."

"Medical reasons, my lord. That is what the form says."

"Medical reasons." Aldous rubbed his thumb over the signature. Strent's hand was small and neat. "I do not know this man."

"No, my lord."

"Yet he writes to me about my son, and he knows to write to me here and not at the town house. A stranger would not know that."

He read the last line of the form again. It had been added below the signature, in the same small hand, as if it were an afterthought.

Please advise whether the boy's late mother left any papers with you.

Aldous put the paper down with his palm over it. His hand stayed there for a while, and the tea went from cold to colder, and Bertram did not move from the door.

"The Wren file says nothing about a mother," Aldous said.

"No, my lord."

"Tell me what it does say."

Bertram folded his hands behind his back. "That the boy was raised by tutors in the north. That he is eighteen. That he is valuable to the crown and is not to be spoken of. There is no mother in it."

"So the vice-headmaster knows something that is not in our file." Aldous said it twice, and the second time he said it quietly. "He writes about the boy's late mother as if it were nothing, as if he were only repeating what he had read. But he could not have read it. Nobody wrote it down."

"Perhaps the file does say it, in another office."

"Yes." Aldous stood up, and the chair went back against the wall. "That is the worst kind of answer. It is possible, and it makes nothing better."

He went down the west corridor with his boots in his hand. He was not sure why. A man in socks made less noise, and he did not want to be heard.

On the way he passed the portraits in the hall, and he did not look at the empty space beside the last one. It was the width of a woman's portrait, and the wall behind it was a shade paler than the rest. Eleven years ago he had taken the painting down himself, because he could not walk past it and also be a duke. He had promised to hang it again when he could. In the chapel off the west corridor the light was low and the stone floor had been swept that morning, and a row of candles burned in front of a painted window that showed a man in a blue cloak with his hands out.

The chapel had been built for a smaller house and then added to, so the west wall was a foot out of line with the east, and the bench under the window had been shortened to make room for a pillar. Aldous had chosen that bench when he was thirty and had never chosen another. The case stood on it now, long and plain with brass corners. He opened it, and the silver inside caught the candle light and held it. Sevar had been made for a man who was not his son, by a woman who had been dead eleven years. The plates had a feather's veins running through them, finer than any smith had ever managed before or since. Aldous did not look at the veins today. He took out the helm first, which was the part he had come for.

The armor was called Sevar. It was Argent work, kept in this chapel and not in the vault, because he liked to know where it was. He did not wear it to fight any more. He put it on when he needed to think, and when he could not bear to think without it.

The helm was smooth, with a narrow slit across the front and a crest that had been beaten flat into the silver so that it looked like a line of water. Inside, the padding had been cut and stitched by hand. A person's breath stayed in it for a moment when it was lifted out. No person had worn it for months.

"Check the fit," Aldous said, to the empty chapel.

He put the helm on. He thought of the evening before, when the whole table had laughed and the chapel bell had rung once by itself. He had not laughed like that in eleven years. He did not know whether the bell had rung for the laugh or for the boy, and he was afraid to find out. The world narrowed to the slit and the sound of his own breathing. He turned his head left, then right, and waited for the strap to settle against his jaw. Then he sat down on the bench with the case shut beside him and his hands in the gauntlets that lay there, and he began to hum.

It was not a tune with words. It was four notes, up and down and back again. He had heard them in a workshop when he was young, and in a house where a woman argued with someone across a kitchen table. He hummed them once. He hummed them again. The chapel kept the sound the way stone keeps the warmth of a fire after the fire has gone out.

Bertram waited in the corridor outside with his back to the wall and his hands folded. He could not see the Duke, but he knew what the Duke was doing. He had heard the humming through that door many times. Through the door, once, he heard a short sharp sound that might have been the helm touching the bench, and he took a half step toward it and stopped. A Duke who wanted help in a chapel would have said so. A Duke who did not want it would be insulted by an offer. Bertram had served for thirty years in the narrow space between the two, and he stayed in it now as another man might stay in a favourite chair. He had stood there before, at other houses and in other years. He knew the length of this silence better than he knew the length of a meal. A servant passed and looked at the chapel door and then at him, and Bertram shook his head once, and the servant went away. A second servant came with a tray of tea and was sent away as well. The tea went cold on the table in the hall and Greta came out and took it back without asking anyone, and she did not look at the chapel door either.

An hour went by. The only sound from inside was the humming, and after a while even that stopped, and the chapel was quiet except for the candles. Bertram moved once, from his left foot to his right, and then stood still again. He did not count the minutes, and his eyes did not leave the door.

When the door opened, the Duke came out with his beard combed and his face arranged in the expression he used for guests. He held a folded paper in his hand, which was the letter to the academy that he had written that morning and not sent.

"Bertram," he said.

"My lord."

"Tell Hammond to saddle a horse. Not the grey. The one with the bad left foot, and do not tell him why I chose it. He will put it in a song." He put the letter in his coat and buttoned the pocket, which was something he only did when he had made up his mind. "I want to go and see a man about a form."

"Where will you go, my lord?"

"To the academy. I will arrive late and unannounced, in a coat with mud on it, as a father who has come to ask about his son's attendance. A strange man does not usually mind a father." He looked at the letter in his pocket. "I want to see Strent's face when I ask about the mother. A man can sign a form and keep his face still. He cannot keep his face still when the question is a surprise."

"Will you be back for dinner tonight, my lord?"

"Cold fish again," Aldous said. "It can wait. Tell Greta I have gone away to be useful, and that she is not to put it in a ballad."

Bertram did not move yet. "My lord. The boy will hear that you came to the school."

"I know."

"He may not like to be checked on."

"No." Aldous stopped with one hand on the door. For a moment the goofy duke was gone, and the man under him looked very tired. "But he will like it less if I sit here and do nothing. I have done that for eleven years, Bertram. It has not kept him safe. It has only kept me comfortable." He pulled the door open. "Bring the horse round. And a second coat. It is going to be cold on that road."
