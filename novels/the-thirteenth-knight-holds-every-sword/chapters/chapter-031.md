# Chapter 31: The Cold Here

The Southern Wall had no grass on its battlements and no smell of school anywhere on it. The wind came over the parapet from the grey country beyond with dust in it that stuck to the teeth, and the snow that had fallen in the night had gone brown by morning. The camp lay behind the wall in rows of low stone huts Smoke from the cook-fires did not rise but lay along the ground in long grey ropes, as if the sky above had been lowered by a hand and the camp pressed flat beneath it. with no windows on the north side, and the bunks inside were two boards and a blanket, and a peg for a coat. Felix looked at his bunk for a while and said that it was less comfortable than the stable bench, which was the first honest thing anyone had said about the Rotation.

Lance Nine was posted to a stretch of wall with four other Lances on either side, and for the first time it stood as its own unit. Okoru had written the order himself and read it aloud before they climbed the stair: Nine holds the middle, Nine reports to the Wall sergeant, Nine does not attach itself to anyone else.

"Why us?" Rowan asked, on the stair.

"Because I want to see what you do when nobody is holding your hand," Okoru said.

Felix looked down at the snow on his boots. "That is a worrying answer."

"It is an honest one." Okoru's one arm was behind his back as usual, and the empty sleeve was pinned at the shoulder. "You have three Summer Postings each. That was rear work and escort roads with veterans in front of you. This is a front battlement in Choir season. Watch the sergeant and learn what is different." He waited until they were all looking at him. "In the summers the Wall was a place you visited. Now it is a place you hold. If you cannot tell the difference by the end of the week, I will tell you again."

The sergeant waited for them on the wall with a board under one arm and a rifle in his hand. He was a broad man in a grey coat with a scar through one eyebrow, and he had the particular stillness of a soldier who had stopped being surprised by the Wall.

"Nine?" he said. They identified themselves. He counted them and wrote something on his board. "You are the youngest unit on this stretch. That is not an insult. It is the reason I am going to show you one thing before I put you on watch."

He lifted the rifle. It was a long gun with a glass tube set into the stock and a brass ring at the breech, and a thin blue line ran along the tube where the mana sat.

"Mana-lock," he said. "The tube holds a small spell that pushes the ball out when you pull. It is quicker than powder and it works in rain. People think a gun with a mana-lock is the answer to anything past the parapet. It is not." He pointed to a post at the far end of the wall. "Husk comes up at night. You shoot it in the chest, and the ball goes in and stops. It keeps coming. You need something that cuts, and you need an arm behind the cut."

A conscript beside him said, "Sir, the Ward-plate stops a ball. Why does the plate not stop the Husk?"

"Different problem." The sergeant set the rifle against the wall and turned to a man in plate who had been standing at the stair mouth. "Show them."

The man in plate raised one arm, and something happened to the air around his shoulder. It was not a glow and not a sound. The snow near his boots flattened, and the conscripts nearest him stepped back without being told. That was aura: a knight's will laid over the world so that ordinary things had to go around it.

The sergeant took a pistol from his belt and fired at the plated man's shoulder. The ball struck the Ward-plate—a broad steel plate with a worn dent in its centre—and slid off into the snow with a small hiss. The man in plate did not move his arm.

"Ward-plate," the sergeant said. "Raised aura. A pistol ball slides off. A Husk does not. A Husk's grey is like wet clay and it keeps its grip." He handed the plate to Rowan, who held it with both hands and made a small sound at how heavy it was. "Feel that? That weight is why you train. The plate does not save you if you cannot move in it." He picked the ball out of the snow and put it in his pocket. "So. You shoot to stop the legs, then you close and cut with aura behind the blade. That is the whole lesson. Any questions?"

Rowan raised his hand. "Can I try the gun?"

"No."

"Can I try the plate?"

"When you can stand still for a minute without talking."

Felix turned a page. "That will be never."

Lysette was the last up the stair. She had a roll of paper under her arm and a pencil behind each ear, and she stopped at the top to look along the whole length of the wall, left and right, with the focus she gave to a form that had been filled out wrongly. "Eleven towers," she said, to nobody in particular. "Between here and the horizon. And the sixth one has no lamp."

"It has a lamp," the sergeant said.

"It has a lamp-bracket. I can see the bracket." She wrote it down. "That is an item for the stores form."

The sergeant looked at her for a long moment, then at the board in his own hand, and Eden saw him decide not to explain that the sixth tower's lamp had been taken down on purpose. Whatever the reason was, it was not one a sergeant told a girl with two pencils.

* * *

The Husk came at the second watch, when the light had gone and the wall had become a line of dark shapes with snow between them. It climbed the outer face where the stone had a ledge, moving slowly and without noise, and the two soldiers on the parapet at that end saw it before the Lantern call went.

Eden watched from Lance Nine's stretch, twenty steps away. He could see enough: a grey body with too many joints, a head that hung down, and fingers that kept finding holds where no holds should be. A soldier fired the mana-lock twice, and the balls went into the grey flesh and stopped there, and the thing kept climbing.

"Close," the sergeant said, in a low voice that carried along the wall. Two veterans went over the parapet with blades. One put a cut across the Husk's wrist and the grey hand came off and fell into the snow, still moving. The other drove a short sword into its neck with his shoulder behind the blade, and the grey body folded down against the stone and lay there with its fingers opening and closing.

The soldier with the cut wrist wiped his blade on the snow and looked at the edge, which had gone grey along one side. He said nothing about it and put the blade back in its sheath.

"See?" the sergeant said. "Lead does not finish it. Steel with an arm behind it does." He looked at the grey edge of the blade and wrote something else on his board. "And do not let the grey sit on your steel. It takes a hold."

Rowan had gone still beside Eden. "I could have done that," he said.

"Yes."

"But I would have gone over the parapet first."

"You did."

"I am thinking about next time." Rowan looked at the grey hand in the snow. "Is it always like that?"

"Every time," the sergeant said. "That is why we pay you to learn."

Clara came along the wall to look at the grey hand and put one hand near it without touching, and then took her hand away. "It is still moving," she said.

"It does that," the sergeant said. "For a minute. Then it stops."

"Can anything be done?"

"Not for the hand." He looked at her for half a second. "You will see worse by the end of the month. That is not a threat. It is a schedule."

A man came up the stair with a sack over his shoulder and a thermos in his hand. He was a groundskeeper by the look of him, a thin man in a brown coat with a cap pulled low, and he moved around the camp stores as if he had been doing it for years. He set the thermos on a bench and began sorting the cut fuses from a bundle without light enough to see their colours, and he hummed as he worked, four notes up and down, wrong by one note at the top every time.

"Tea," he said, to no one. "Hot tea. Two sugars for the tall one. I do not know his name."

"That is Tallis," a soldier said.

"Then I will learn it." The groundskeeper went on humming, wrong at the same note, and did not look up at anyone.

An old soldier sat on a crate nearby with his hands around a cup. He had a face like folded paper and a blanket over his knees, and he watched the groundskeeper with the mild attention of a man who had seen a lot of people come and go.

"The ground's cold here," the old soldier said, to Eden, as if they had been talking for some time. "Like she turned her back."

"Who is she?" Rowan asked.

The old soldier took a drink and did not answer. He looked at the parapet, where the Husk's grey hand lay in the snow with its fingers still moving, and then at the dark country beyond the wall. He held the cup in both hands, and the tea in it had gone the colour of the snow water. "Forty-one years I have been coming here," he said. "It was colder when I was young. Or I was warmer. One of those."

"Birds," he said, a moment later. "There used to be birds on this wall, even in winter." He smiled at the thought, a small private smile, and went back to his tea, and nobody asked him anything more.

* * *

At the third watch a small bird landed on the parapet beside Felix and put its head on one side to look at him. Rowan reached out a hand, and the bird did not move away. Two more landed on the next merlon, and one on the sergeant's board, and for a moment the wall had more birds on it than men.

Then every bird on the battlement went silent at once and flew up together, in one direction, over the grey country and out of sight. They did not circle. They did not call. They went as if something under the wall had drawn a breath. The snow where they had perched showed small prints in a line, and then the wind came over the parapet and filled them with dust, one after another, from the north end to the south.

Felix watched the empty stone where the bird had been. "I did not like that," he said.

"No," Eden said.

The sergeant came along the wall with his board under his arm and stopped beside them. His face in the dark was all lines, and he looked like a man who had been awake for several days and had made peace with it. He looked at the empty parapet, then at the dark country beyond it, and wrote one word on his board without showing it to anyone.

"First watch in the morning," he said. "Sleep when you can. The next watch comes early."

The cold wind went on putting grey dust on the brown snow.
