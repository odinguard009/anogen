# Chapter 31: The Southern Wall

The Southern Wall ran east and west until it was lost in the grey. And the camp sat against it in rows of low stone huts. And the wind came over the parapet with dust in it. Eden had been cold in the academy yard. And he had been cold on the roof, and this cold was different. It came through the wall and up through his boot soles. And it did not let go when he moved. Because there was no sun on the north face of the Wall. Eden stood in the muster yard at six with his coat up to his throat. And the cold went through the seams of the school uniform and found the skin at his wrists.

"Why are they looking at us?" Rowan asked.

Rowan thought about that while he pulled his gloves on. "Then we will have to be very good five. Rowan said it with a straight face. And Clara put her staff across her shoulders and looked at the wall. She did not say that five would not be enough. Eden watched the veterans instead. One of them was sharpening a blade on a stone. And he did not look up once. Another was counting tins into a crate. And he counted them twice, and the second count was different from the first.

"Why count twice?" Felix asked.

"Because men steal," Eden said.

"On the Wall?"

"Especially on the Wall."

Clara stood with her staff across her shoulders and her breath in a white cloud, and Felix had his bow case under one arm and his hands deep in his pockets, and Lysette wrote something in her black book with a pencil that kept sticking to the page in the cold.

A sergeant came along the row with a clipboard and a hard face, and he stopped in front of them and read their names off a sheet, and he did not read "Wren" twice, and he did not read Wren twice, which Eden noted. The sergeant's writing was small and neat. And the paper had a damp edge. And the pencil left marks where the cold had made it hard. Eden saw five names on each of the other sheets. And all the spears had five names. And he wondered why the man had made them look the same.

"You are the school's answer to a problem nobody asked," the sergeant said. "Your stretch is four hundred paces of parapet, and you hold it until you are told to come down. The wall does not care that you are five, and the Hushborn do not care what your rank is."

"Yes, sergeant," Lysette said, and the others said it after her, and Eden said it last. The sergeant wrote a number on his sheet and shut the clipboard. He looked at Lysette for a beat longer than he looked at the others, and then he walked away with his coat collar up.

"He knows your name," Rowan said.

"He knows everyone's name," Lysette said.

"He looked at you twice."

"That is his job." She opened her book. "Mine is to write it down."

The sergeant took them to a practice bay behind the stores. Where a row of conscripts stood shivering in a line. And behind the line a man in a weathered coat held a long gun with a glass tube along the barrel and a brass ring at the breech. He wore a broad steel plate on one shoulder with a dent in it. And he held the gun at his hip while he spoke.

"This is a mana-lock musket," he said, and the conscripts leaned in to look. The gun was longer than a hunting piece, and the glass tube held a small blue spell, and a thin mana line ran from the tube to the brass ring at the back. The sergeant said it worked in rain and did not need powder, and that the ball inside was heavy enough to knock a man down when it went straight. "It is a good tool," he said. "It is not a wall."

He raised his free arm, and the air near his shoulder went thick, and the snow on a nearby crate flattened where it fell, and two conscripts stepped back without being told. Eden watched the line of the man's shoulder and the way the cold air bent around it. And he wrote one word in his notebook: aura.

The sergeant fired the musket into a sand bank. And the gun gave a hard click and a puff of blue light. And the ball went into the sand and stuck there. Then he fired again, and the glass tube made a cracking sound, and the ball came out sideways and hit the ground three paces short.

"That is what a misfire looks like," he said. "You will see it in the dark, and you will not have time to be surprised."

A conscript asked what stopped a ball when the gun worked. And the man turned so that the dented plate on his shoulder faced them. He called it Ward-plate, and he said it was broad steel worn over the coat with a dent from an old struggle, and that when a knight raised their aura the plate threw a ball aside instead of taking it through the body. He said it was heavy and useless to a man who could not move in it. And then he let a soldier fire a pistol at his shoulder from ten paces. And the ball slid off the dent and and the ball slid off the dent and went into the snow with a small sound. A conscript asked if the plate stopped everything. And the man said it stopped what it stopped and nothing else. And that a man who believed otherwise was dead. The conscript went quiet, and the man gave him the musket to hold, and the boy held it with both hands.

The conscripts made a noise that was almost a laugh, and the man pocketed the pistol and told them to stop looking at the plate and start looking at the parapet.

"Does it always work?" a conscript asked.

"No," the man said.

"Then why wear it?"

"Because it works more often than a coat." He tapped the dent with one finger. "This came from a man who did not believe that either."

Eden wrote three words in his notebook: mana-lock, misfire, Ward-plate. He put a line under each word and then a line under all three. Because the sergeant had said a gun was not a wall. And that was a fact worth keeping.

A bell rang twice from the wall gate, and the sergeant's face changed into something shorter. "Probe," he said, and he went up the stair with four veterans ahead of him, and spear Nine followed because Okoru had told them to watch and not to help.

Eden saw the Hushborn before he understood what it was: a grey shape on the outer face of the wall with long arms and no neck. And it climbed with its hands flat on the stone as if the wall were a ladder it had used before. A veteran fired a musket into its chest. And the ball sank into grey flesh and stayed there, and the thing kept climbing.

"That is why you carry a blade," a soldier beside Eden said, and he put his own aura up and went over the parapet with a short blade, and the blade went through the grey shape at the shoulder and the thing fell back into the dark without a sound.

Two more veterans finished the probe while the conscripts watched, and it was over in a minute and a half, and the soldiers went back to their posts as if a thing climbing the wall at dawn were a matter of sweeping the step.

Lysette had written four lines in her book. "They did not call us," she said.

"They did not need us," Eden said.

"That is worse." Lysette shut her book. "Okoru told us to watch, and we watched. We did not learn how those men moved. We learned that they do not need us."

"They have done this for years."

"So will we, if we are still here in six weeks."

Rowan came back from the parapet with his hands in his pockets. "The thing had no neck," he said.

"I saw it."

"I did not know they could climb like that."

Felix, who had been quiet, said, "They climb like that because the wall is made for it. Stone has openings. Hands find them."

Eden looked at Felix, and then at the wall, and he said nothing.

On the way down to the stores, a man with a round face and a limp handed out tins from a crate, and he hummed a tune with four notes down and one that went up while he did it, and he did not seem to know that he was humming.

"Groundskeeper Hallow was left here when the school went north," the man said, and he gave Eden a tin of beans and a spoon wrapped in paper. "I run the stores, and I knew the man who ran them before me, and I can tell you which tins are lying about their date." He looked at Eden's face for a moment and then looked away. "Cold out," he said, and he went on humming while he counted the next crate.

An old soldier sat on a barrel by the store door with his hands around a cup, and he had a grey moustache and a missing finger on his left hand, and he watched Eden and the others with the calm of a man who had decided they were not interesting yet.

"You are the school ones," he said.

"Yes," Lysette said.

"Staying long?"

"Six weeks."

The old man drank his tea and looked at the ground between his boots, and then he said, "The ground's cold here. Like she turned her back."

Nobody asked who she was, and the old man did not explain, and the wind went over the yard and made the tins rattle in their crate.

"Who was she?" Rowan asked, when the old man had gone to fetch more tea.

"The ground," Eden said, and he did not say anything else.

The groundskeeper came back with a second crate and hummed the same four notes down and one up, and he stopped when he saw Eden looking at him.

"Do I know that tune?" Eden asked.

"You might," the man said, and he went back to humming, but he hummed it softer now, and he did not look at Eden while he did it.

"He knows it," Lysette said, when the man had gone.

"Yes."

"Why hum it if he knows we know?"

"Because it is the only way to say a thing without saying it."

Clara knelt and put her hand on the stone. And then she stood up and wiped her palm on her coat. And her face had changed. "The wall is cold in a way stone should not be," she said. "It is not the weather. Eden put his hand where hers had been. And he felt nothing, and that was the trouble.

At the top of the parapet, the birds that had been picking at crumbs along the stone stopped all at once, and every one of them lifted its head and looked south without making a sound. Eden wrote it down and then he waited.
