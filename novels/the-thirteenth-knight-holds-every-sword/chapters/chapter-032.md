# Chapter 32: The Silence

The mess hut had one stove and three long tables. The men at the nearest table were playing cards with frozen fingers. Lance Nine ate at the end of the second table. The beans were hot, and the bread was as hard as a board.

"Why is the bread like this?" Rowan asked.

"It was baked four days ago," Felix said.

"Why four?"

"Because the cart could not get up the road." Felix broke a piece off and threw it at the wall, and it bounced. "That is a fact, and it is also a warning."

Clara put her piece in her tea and waited. Then she ate it with a spoon. "It is better wet," she said.

Lysette wrote in her book without looking up. "There is no rule against bread in tea."

"I was not asking about rules."

"You were asking why the bread is hard, and I am telling you that there is no rule about it." She shut the book. "Eat it or do not eat it."

Rowan ate his bread dry and made a face, and the men at the next table laughed, and one of them called out a question about school bread, and Rowan shouted back that school bread was soft and had seeds on top, and the man said that anything with seeds was a luxury and should be reported.

An old soldier came past with a mug in his hand and stopped at their table. "You are the five," he said.

"Yes," Lysette said.

"Watch the wall tonight, and watch it with your hands as well as your eyes. The Choir does not always use voices." He drank his tea and went on, and he did not say which Choir he meant or where it had come from.

"Does he talk like that to everyone?" Rowan asked.

"He talks like that to people he thinks will listen," Eden said. Rowan looked at the old soldier, and then at his own bread, and he put the bread in his tea as well. Clara watched him do it and said nothing, which was worse than a comment. Felix ate his bread in four bites and asked whether anyone had counted the men on the wall, and Lysette said that the roster had been posted at six and she had copied it. 'How many?' Felix asked. 'Nine hundred and twelve,' she said, and Felix said that was a lot of men to lose in a silence, and Lysette said that was not what the word meant.

The weather turned at midnight, and the wind dropped out of the wall so suddenly that Rowan stopped mid-sentence and stood with his mouth open. His second word came out as a breath of white fog and no sound at all.

Eden put a hand on the parapet and felt the stone under his glove. The cold was there, and the dark was there, and the sounds of the camp were gone: no boots on the stair, no cough from the next post, no scrape of a shovel in the yard below. A Choir silence had come down over Lance Nine's stretch of wall. It took the voices out of the world but left everything else in place.

The watch began at one, and Lance Nine took the stretch between the third and fourth lamp posts, and the lamps burned low because the Choir season made the oil thick. Rowan checked his shield strap twice. And Eden checked it once, and Felix checked his bow string by plucking it with a fingers nail, and Clara put a fresh band on her staff. Lysette read the round sheet by the light of the lamp and then put it in her coat. Okoru came along the wall from the centre post. He did not shout, because shouting was gone, and he did not waste time on it. He held up one hand with the fingers flat. He pointed at the left parapet. Then at the right, and then at his own chest, which was the sign for hold. Eden had seen the sign in a drill book. He had never thought to need it in the dark.

Lysette stood on his right with her batons at her belt. She read the signs before Eden did. She put two fingers to her own eyes and pointed down the wall. And Okoru nodded once.

Eden showed them the signs again in the lamplight because new men forget things in the cold. Hold was a flat hand. Wait was a closed fist turned down. Fall back was two fingers toward the body. Lysette made each sign back to him. She made them faster than he did, and Felix asked how she had learned them. 'He uses them in his sleep,' she said. And Eden said that he did not, and Lysette wrote a line in her book. The first Husk came up over the outer face at the left end. It had no neck and its hands were long. It pulled itself over the stone without a sound because the sound was not in the air. Felix saw it first and put an arrow to his string. He did not shoot, because there was a second shape behind the first and a third below it, and the shots in a silence had to count.

Eden held up one hand and made the sign for wait. Rowan saw it, and Clara saw it, and Lysette saw it a beat before the others, and the whole line held.

The first Husk put both hands on the stone and pulled. Its fingers had no nails, and the stone under them made a small sound that Eden felt in his teeth rather than heard. Rowan struck it with the shield rim. The thing did not fall, and it swung its long arm at his head. Eden put his lance into the joint at its shoulder, and the arm dropped. The Husk came on with the other arm. Clara set her staff against the crack. The broken stone shifted, and the thing went down to its knees. Felix shot once. The arrow took it in the side of the head and it stopped. Three Husks came over the parapet, and then four more. Okoru moved along the wall with his stick in his hand and his one arm free. He did not make a sound. He could not have made one if he had tried. He came up behind the largest of the climbers. Whose leg was hooked over the stone. He put his hand on the joint behind the knee.

"**[Tavu]**," Eden read on his lips, and he understood the sign even without the word.

The **[Tavu]** was a thread of force. Pale and thin, coming from one hand, and it needed a touch or a thing close enough to touch. Okoru tied the climbing Husk's leg to a brass bracket on the parapet with one twist. And the thread held, and the Husk kicked against it and could not pull free. It was one knot, made with one arm, and it would not hold a second leg.

Clara moved to the left with her staff low. And she put the butt of it into a crack in the stone and held it there. The Husks at that end came on. And the staff did not glow. And the stone under their hands cracked where she had set it. And the men behind her had a wall of broken stone to work from.

A shape moved in the shadow beside Eden. And Mirelle stepped out of it without a sound. She was small and pale, and her thin blade was no wider than a needle, and she put it into the joint of a Husk's elbow as the thing reached for Rowan. The Husk's arm stopped, and Mirelle was gone back into the shadow before Rowan had turned his head. He saw the arm hang oddly and looked at Eden. And Eden pointed at the dark, and Rowan nodded and went back to his post.

Lysette had been reading Eden's hands all through it. When he signed for the right end to close. She signed it on to Felix before he had finished. And Felix moved, and the right end closed. She knew the signs better than the others because she had watched him use them in drills for two years. And she had written every one of them in her black book. And now she was using them faster than he could make them.

Okoru came back along the wall and held up two fingers. Then closed them into a fist. The sign meant hold the line and do not follow. The Husks on the parapet were down or bound. And the ones below had stopped climbing, and the silence went on.

Eden watched the dark beyond the wall. The silence did not feel empty. It had a weight to it. And the weight was under the stone. And he put his hand flat on the parapet and felt a slow movement there that was not the wind. Because the wind was gone.

He signed to Lysette: something below. She looked at the stone, and then at him, and then she wrote one word in her book, because writing still made a mark even when a pencil made no sound.

Okoru pointed at the left, and at the right, and then at his own chest, and the message passed down the line without a word. Rowan put his shield up and waited. And Clara put her hand on the broken stone. And Felix kept his arrow at his cheek. And Lysette read Eden's hands and then the wall again. Lysette put herself between Rowan and the dark. And she used her batons the way she used a pen. With short quick strokes that did not waste motion. She did not strike to hurt. She struck the joints and the hands, and the Husks fell where they stood. Okoru's thread held the largest one by the leg. And it beat the brass bracket with its free hand until the stone dust came off in a grey line. Eden kept signing. Hold. Wait. Close. Pass. The signs went down the wall and the five of them moved as one shape in the dark. The line held until the bell in the camp yard rang for the end of the watch. And the sound came back all at once: boots. Voices, a man swearing in the stairwell, the scrape of a shovel. Rowan let out a breath that was almost a shout. And Clara sat down on the step and put her hands on her knees. And Felix checked his string twice because his hands wanted something to do.

Okoru came to the middle of the wall and looked each of them over. And then he pointed at the stone under their boots. The Choir had not finished. It had moved while they fought. And it was moving still, and Eden could feel it through the soles of his boots as the camp woke up around them.

The camp came back in pieces, and the men on the next stretch called across to ask how many the five had taken, and Rowan held up four fingers and then another one, and the men cheered because five was a good number. Lysette wrote the number in her book and said that the cheer was not a rule and would not be in the report. Eden watched the dark below the wall, and he kept his hand on the stone until Okoru came past and told him to come down.

He counted the Husks on the stone and got seven, and Lysette counted them and got eight, and the eighth was under the parapet with its hands still on the wall. Okoru looked once and sent a veteran down with a blade, and the sound came back then, and the man came back up with his glove grey to the wrist. Nobody spoke about it, and Eden wrote one line in his book, and the line said: eight.
