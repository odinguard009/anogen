# Chapter 32: The Silent Stretch

"Two fingers means hold," Okoru said. He stood in the snow before the fourth bell with his stick in his hand and drew the signs in the air where everyone could see them. "A fist means down. An open hand swept left means move. Learn them now. In an hour you will not be allowed to speak them."

Rowan held up two fingers. "Like this?"

"Like that."

"A fist."

"Yes."

"And the hand."

"Not so fast. The person reading it has to know it is you."

Felix looked up from his bow. "What if I need to say something that is not one of three things?"

"Then point," Okoru said. "Pointing is a full language."

Lysette had her book out and was copying the signs on the back of a warrant with her pencil. "Can I write on my palm?"

"If your palm is where everyone can see it." Okoru turned the stick over. "No spoken spells. No calling to each other. No shouting when a Husk is on the parapet. The Choir hears voices, and it turns toward them."

"How long?" Rowan asked.

"Until it stops. It has stopped before."

"Can my light speak?" Rowan asked. Okoru looked at him. "It makes shadow. Does that count as words?"

"It counts as a light." Okoru tapped his own chest with the stick. "Use it, but do not make a show of it. I can see it from the other end. So can anything under the wall."

"Pointing is a full language," Felix said, in a voice that tried the words out.

"It is the only language left after the bell," Okoru said. "You can use it with your hands full."

Okoru turned away, then turned back. "One more thing. Hollen reads hands. If you do not know what to do, look at her."

Lysette's pencil stopped. "Sir?"

"She reads hands better than you speak them." Okoru walked toward the stair. "That is not praise. It is a tool."

"How long before that?"

"Long enough to learn." Okoru looked at each of them in turn. "You have fought with your hands for five years. Today you will do it again with your mouths shut."

The Choir's silence came at the fourth bell and arrived like a lid closing over the camp. One moment the wall had the ordinary noises of men stamping boots and the sergeant shouting names. The next there were no voices anywhere on the battlements. The wind itself seemed to have gone quiet behind the stone.

They knew the plan. Lance Nine held the third stretch, forty paces of parapet with a stair at each end and a low wall on the inside. No one spoke. No one cast a spell that needed words, and no one whistled either. Eden had drawn three signs in the snow with his boot the night before. Two fingers meant hold. A fist meant down. An open hand swept left meant move. Lysette had watched him draw them. She had copied them with her pencil on the back of a warrant without asking why.

Rowan stood at the left stair with his spear across his body and his mouth shut, which was the hardest part for him. Clara crouched behind the merlon with a roll of linen and her hands ready. Felix was on the right stair with an arrow on the string and his bow low. Lysette stood beside Eden at the centre with her batons in her hands and her eyes on his right hand, not on the wall.

It was a strange way to fight: every habit that belonged to the mouth had to be moved into the hands. Eden had always fought quietly, and it still felt like learning to walk in a house where he had lived all his life. A sign took the time of a breath, and a breath was too long. He found himself cutting the signs shorter each time, until Lysette was answering a flick of the thumb instead of a whole open hand, and he realised that she had been waiting for him to simplify them.

The first Husk came up over the outer face at the left end, and Rowan saw it. The cold had made everything small and clear: the grey of its skin against the white, the single frozen drop on its chin, the way its shadow fell the wrong way along the stone. He made no sound. He held up two fingers, then pointed down, and brought the spear tip round. Rowan's light came into his palms without a word, soft and gold, and the shadows behind the merlons moved back.

Eden signed move. Lysette's hand went up and down once, which was her way of saying she had seen, and she ran left along the parapet with her batons close.

Three more Husks appeared on the wall between them and the stair. They climbed with the slow patience of things that did not get tired, and their grey fingers found the snow-hidden holds. A conscript at the next stretch fired a mana-lock and the ball went into a grey chest and stopped there, and the Husk kept coming. The conscript did not shout. His face did the shouting for him.

Okoru was already over the parapet at the far end with his one arm out. **[Tavu]** came from that hand as threads of force, thin and pale. Okoru flicked his fingers and bound the nearest climbing Husk's leg to a brass bracket on the parapet. The Husk hung there with its body still trying to climb and its foot held fast. **[Tavu]** needed touch or a near thing. Okoru had one arm, and he used it like a man tying a knot he had tied a thousand times.

Eden signed down to Rowan, who brought the spear head down into the bound Husk's neck with his shoulder behind it. The grey body went still.

A fourth Husk came over the merlon three paces from Eden, close enough that he could smell the cold dust on it. He put the shield up and took its weight. Something struck the Husk from the side at the same moment. It was a long thin blade from the shadow of the stair door, almost invisible in the dark, aimed at the joint under the arm. Mirelle did not speak. She struck and was gone back into the shadow before the Husk had finished falling. The only sign she had been there was a cut in the grey that did not bleed.

Eden signed hold to Lysette and swept his open hand left. She read it perfectly and changed direction without looking at his face. Rowan came up behind her with the spear and put two Husks down in the time it took Clara to reach the stair. Felix did not shoot. He held an arrow on the string for four breaths and then let it down. Rowan's gold light had filled the space and there was no clean line through it. Eden signed to him: wait. Felix nodded once and kept the bow steady.

The silence had a shape to it. Eden could hear the Husks because their fingers made small scraping sounds on the stone, and he could hear Clara's breath through her nose, and the ring of Rowan's spear when it struck brass. Nothing else. A spoken spell would have gone through the silence like a crack in glass, and the Choir would have heard it and turned toward the sound.

A fifth Husk came up where the parapet had a crack, and Lysette met it with both batons. She put the left stick under its wrist and the right into the joint at its elbow, and the arm went loose, but the body kept coming. Rowan's spear went past her shoulder and into the grey under the jaw, and the Husk folded onto the merlon with its fingers still searching for a hold. Clara pulled Lysette back by the sleeve before the grey could touch her, and put a hand on her forearm for one breath, and let go.

The sixth Husk did not climb. It stood on the outer ledge with its head hanging down and its hands flat on the stone, and it did not move for three breaths. Eden held up two fingers, then made a fist. Rowan shifted his feet. The thing's head came up, and its mouth opened without a sound, and Mirelle's blade went through the gap at its collar from the stair shadow. The body slid down the outer face and was gone into the snow below.

By the sixth Husk the stretch was quiet again. The bodies lay over the outer face where they had fallen, and the threads of Okoru's knot still held one of them by the ankle. The conscript at the next stretch lowered his rifle with a face full of relief, and nobody said thank you because nobody could. Clara went along the line instead, from face to face, putting two fingers on a wrist here and a shoulder there, checking for the grey. Each man she touched looked at her as if she had spoken. One of them, a young soldier with a bandaged ear, put his hand over hers for a moment and then took it away, embarrassed, and she smiled at him and moved on. It was the loudest thing anyone did all day.

Okoru came along the parapet and checked the three signs in the snow with his stick. He pointed at the open hand Eden had drawn, then at Lysette, and made a short sound in his throat that was not quite a word. Lysette's pencil appeared and she wrote on her palm. She showed Eden what she had written: ONE HAND IS A WHOLE SENTENCE.

Eden wiped the signs away with his boot so that the snow was smooth again. It was habit, and it was also a small superstition. A man who wiped his trail did not leave a story behind him. He glanced up and found Lysette watching, her palm still marked with ink. She did not write down the wiping. She closed her hand over the words instead, so that the sentence stayed on her skin and nowhere else.

The silence did not lift at the next bell. It went on through the morning and into the afternoon. The light changed twice while the wall stayed as quiet as a room with someone sleeping in it. Men ate without talking. A soldier dropped a spoon on the stone and every head on the stretch turned toward the sound, and then turned back when nothing followed it. The groundskeeper went along the line with a bucket of hot water and a rag, and he wiped the brass brackets that Okoru had bound the Husk to, humming the wrong top note under his breath as he worked. Nobody stopped him. Nobody asked why a groundskeeper was cleaning a bracket on a front battlement. The sergeant wrote orders on his board and held them up. A prefect passed a note along the line, and the note said the Choir would finish when it finished, and that nobody was to strike a bell or sing.

At dusk the silence grew deeper instead of lighter. Eden stood at the parapet with his hand on the cold stone and looked down at the wall's inner face, where the snow had melted in a line along the stone. The line ran the length of the battlement and ended at a drain in the floor. Under the drain something moved with a slow, even pull, like a great throat breathing in its sleep. Eden put his glove flat on the stone above the drain and felt the pull go through the wall into his hand. It came in and went out at the same pace as the silence, and when it went out the snow at the edge of the drain jumped a grain at a time.

He took his hand away and wiped the wet stone off the glove on his coat. The pull kept its rhythm while he watched. It did not hurry and it did not stop, and it was not the wind, because the wind had gone quiet behind the stone hours ago.

The Choir was not dead. It was moving under the wall, and it had been moving there all day.
