# Chapter 33: The Scarf

The bellwire above the camp gate clattered at seven. It did not stop when the clerk put his hand on it. Because the king's party had come up the road in four carriages and the wire had a message to deliver. Eden stood at the end of the stores hut with a crate in his hands and watched the gate. He did not move, because a student who ran to see a king was a student who had forgotten his post.

Rowan came up behind him with his coat half buttoned. "Is that the king?"

"Yes."

"Do we salute?"

"Okoru will tell us."

Okoru did tell them, and he told them in a low voice while he walked past with his stick under his arm. "Stand where you are and hold what you are holding. A king who sees a man drop a crate remembers the crate."

"He remembers the man," Lysette said, and she was already holding her own crate with both hands and had stepped back into the shadow of the door.

By the time the party came into the yard. spear Nine was a line of five people doing ordinary work in the cold. That was the correct way to be invisible. Eden lifted a crate onto a stack and took the empty place at the end. He kept his face still and his eyes on the middle distance. The king's carriage passed within six steps of him.

King Theodric was in an open carriage with a blanket over his knees. His face was thinner than Eden remembered. And Ysolde sat in the carriage behind with a folder on her lap and her cards in her hand. The horses were grey and their breath made clouds in the cold air, and a footman held the door of the second carriage with both hands. Ysolde's folder was open on her knee, and she had a pencil in one hand and a card in the other, and she did not look up at the men along the wall. The king waved at the men at the gate and then looked at the wall. Then at the huts, and then at the ground, and he did not look at the line of students by the stores.

The party went into the map hut. The door shut, and the yard went back to its ordinary noise. Eden set down the crate and flexed his fingers. Because the cold had made them stiff. Rowan came over from the coal pile with a lump of coal on his glove. He put it back because Clara told him to. Felix counted the carriages and said there were four. That the fourth had curtains in the windows. That people who put curtains in carriage windows did not want to be seen. Clara asked who would put curtains on a carriage in the middle of a war. And Felix said that was the point of it. Lysette wrote a line in her book. And Eden saw her write a second line and then cross it out.

The sergeant went into the map hut with his clipboard under his arm. Came out twenty minutes later with his face unchanged. Which Rowan said was a skill in itself. Okoru came along the wall and told them all to keep working. He did not tell them to stand straight. An hour later he came past the hut with a sack of coal on his shoulder. The door was open a hand's width. He heard the king's voice through the gap.

"Then give me the second envelope," the king said, and there was a paper sound, and then Ysolde's voice, low and flat.

"There is only one envelope in the file, Your Majesty."

"Then somebody has moved the second one."

There was a chair scraping, and then the king said something that Eden could not hear, and then the door moved and he walked on with the coal, and he did not slow down. He counted the steps to the corner. Which was eleven, and he wrote the word envelope in his mind and left it there.

Lysette found him at the coal pile at noon. She was holding her gloves in one hand and her book in the other. Her lips were pale, and there was a red mark on her wrist where her cuff had rubbed.

"You are cold," Eden said, and he said it as a fact and not as a question.

"I am the same temperature as everyone else on this wall." She held out the gloves. "Take these."

"I have gloves."

"Your gloves are thin, and mine are thick, and I am not giving them to you because I like you." She put them in his hand. "I am giving them to you so I am not stuck with your paperwork when you get frostbite and Okoru writes a form about it."

"My hands are not cold."

"That is not the point of a scarf."

Eden looked at her, and then at the gloves in his hand, and she had not said the word scarf, so he waited.

Lysette pulled a grey wool scarf from her coat and held it out without looking at him. "There. Wrap it around your neck and do not say anything about it. Her chin was up, and her face was set, and the red mark on her wrist showed above the cuff. "You are the only person I know who can stand still in this wind and claim to be warm."

"Thank you," Eden said. He took off one of her gloves and put it back, and then he put it on again, and Lysette watched him do it with her mouth shut in a straight line. "Are you laughing at the gloves?" she asked. "No." "You are smiling." "It is the cold." "It is not the cold." She shut her book. "Do not thank me. It is a loan, and I will want it back in the spring."

"Do not thank me. It is a loan, and I will want it back in the spring." She wrote a line in her book and shut it. "And if Rowan asks, you found it in the stores."

" Rowan does not ask useful questions."

"He will ask this one."

Eden wrapped the scarf around his neck under his coat. And the wool was warm from her skin. And the smell of it was soap and mint. And he did not say anything about that either. He put her gloves on and found they were too big at the fingers. Lysette watched him do it and then put her own hands in her coat pockets. "They are too big," he said. "I know. "Then why give them to me? "Because you never complain about anything, and I wanted to see you do it once. She turned away. "Do not make a speech about it. "I was not going to. "Good. And he kept them on.

The king came out of the map hut in the afternoon and walked across the yard with the secretary beside him. And the men at the gates stood straight, and the horses stamped in the cold. Eden was at the far end of the coal stack with a shovel in his hand. And he had no reason to move, and he kept his eyes on the shovel.

The king stopped four paces away. He did not call the boy's name. And he did not look surprised. And he looked at the coal and the shovel and the grey wool at Eden's throat.

"Cadet Wren," he said, and his voice was tired and kind. "Your mother would have hated the cold."

Eden kept his hands on the shovel. The king waited for a moment. And then he nodded once, as if he had expected no answer and had come to accept it, and he walked on toward the gate with his secretary a step behind.

Lysette had been standing by the stores with her book open. And she saw the king stop. And she saw him speak, and she wrote nothing in her book. When the carriages had gone down the road she came over and looked at the scarf at Eden's throat. And then she looked at his face.

"He spoke to you."

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

Eden told her, and he said it exactly, and Lysette closed her book and held it with both hands. She stood very still for a while, and the wind moved her hair across her face, and she did not push it back.

"Is that how he greets all the cadets?" she asked.

"I do not know."

"He has spoken to you twice now, and both times he has said something he should not say to a boy with your papers."

"That is his business."

"It is my business as well." She shut the book tighter. "He knew your mother, and he spoke about her where any one of nine hundred men could hear him, and he did not lower his voice."

"He is a king."

"Kings are listened to more carefully than other people, not less." She looked at him and then put the book under her arm. "I am not asking you what he meant, and I am not asking why he said it, because you will give me a true answer that says nothing. I am telling you that I heard it and that I wrote it down."

"Wrote what?"

"That he knew you before this year."

Eden said nothing, and Lysette walked back to the hut, and she did not look at him again.

"Your mother," she said. "He said your mother."

"Yes."

"Not your guardian, or your papers, or the name on the form. Your mother." She looked toward the road where the carriages had gone, and then back at him, and she did not ask the next question, and that was the strangest part of it. "Wear the scarf," she said. "And do not lose it."

"I will not lose it."

She walked away, and Eden put his hand on the wool at his throat, and he kept it there until the yard bell rang for the evening watch. Rowan came past with two tins of beans and asked why he was holding his neck. And Eden said it was the scarf. And Rowan said it was a nice scarf and looked expensive. And Eden said it was a loan. Rowan asked who had loaned it. And Eden said he had found it in the stores. And Rowan looked at the scarf and then at Lysette. Who was writing at the door of the hut. And said that the stores had good taste. Eden did not answer, and Rowan went away with his two tins, and the bell rang again, and the yard went quiet in layers as the men went to their posts.

The evening watch began at six, and Eden took the last stretch with the scarf under his coat and the gloves tight on his hands, and he counted the lamps along the wall until the count had a shape. The third lamp was low and the fourth was high, and the space between them was where the Choir had moved under the stone, and he kept his feet on the cold parapet and waited for the sound to come back.

Felix came up the stair with a cup of tea and said that the king's horses had better coats than the cadets, which Clara said was a fact and not a complaint, and Lysette wrote a line in her book and said that there was no form for horses. Rowan asked whether there was a form for kings, and Lysette said there was, and that she had filled in three of them that week, and none of them had asked about a mother. Eden held the scarf under his coat and said nothing. He slept that night with the scarf folded under his pillow.
