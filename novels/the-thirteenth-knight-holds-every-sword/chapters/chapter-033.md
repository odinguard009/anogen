# Chapter 33: The Second Envelope

The day after the silence the wall woke in pieces. Men stretched their hands in the cold air and slapped their arms against their chests. The ground under the parapet still had a dull feel to it, as if the stone were tired. A man struck the bellwire outside the mess hut. The wire carried the sound to the next bell, which struck after it, and then the next. The news travelled faster than a runner. Eden counted four bells before the message reached the far end. Each bell was a little quieter than the one before, and the last one gave a dry click instead of a note.

"The king is coming," a conscript said. "That is the inspection pattern. Four strikes and a pause."

"How long?" Rowan asked.

"Two hours. He brings secretaries and a painter." The conscript put on his cap. "Nobody is to look at him unless he looks at you first."

Felix read his book upside down for a moment. "That is a difficult rule for Rowan."

"I can do it," Rowan said.

"You cannot," Clara said, without looking up from her bandages.

A clerk with cold hands had explained the bellwire in the morning. It was a wire strung along the wall on iron posts, with a hammer at each end, so that a bell could be rung from a distance and the next bell would answer. It was faster than shouting and quieter than a rider. The Wall used it for alarms as well as visits. Eden had noted the posts on his first day and had not touched them.

Lance Nine was given the stretch nearest the inspection stair, which meant they would be seen. Okoru came along the line while they waited and checked their belts one by one. He stopped at Lysette and looked at her hands, which were bare because she had taken her gloves off to write.

"Lysette," he said. "You are cold."

"No, sir."

"Your hand is white. Put your gloves on."

"I write better without them."

"You will write nothing today if you cannot hold the pencil." Okoru moved on to Rowan, who was holding his spear at the wrong angle, and corrected it with the stick.

Clara waited until he had gone. Then she took a pair of thin wool mittens out of her coat and pressed them into Lysette's hands without a word. Lysette looked at them, and at Clara, and put them on over her bare fingers. She did not say thank you. She wrote one short line in her book, and Eden guessed that it said thank you in a form she could bear.

Eden looked at Lysette's hands once and then at the sky over the parapet. Her fingers were the colour of candle wax, and the knuckles stood out as white as the snow. She wrote anyway, in small stiff letters, and he thought that it must hurt to hold the pencil. He noted it as a fact about a hand, and noted that he had noted it twice. The sky was the colour of dishwater and the wind had not stopped since the fourth bell the day before. He did not say anything about her hands, because she had said she was not cold and he had no reason to disbelieve her. He noted that her pencil had slipped twice while she wrote, which was a thing he could count, and he put it in the box of facts.

* * *

The king arrived with six people and a man carrying a sketchbook. A motor-car had brought them up the hill and stopped where the road ended at the gate. The engine kept running while the party climbed the stair. The driver had been told not to kill it. Two officers brought up the rear with their hands full of papers, and a servant followed with a folded rug over one arm in case the king wanted to sit. Theodric wore a plain grey coat with a fur collar and no crown, and he walked the wall with his hands behind his back, looking at the parapet rather than at the soldiers. The first thing he did was stop at a merlon. A Husk's fingers had chewed the snow there into grey slush. He asked the sergeant how long it would take to replace the stone.

"Three days, sir."

"Then do it in two and send me the bill." Nobody laughed, because nobody was sure the king had been joking, and the sergeant wrote it down on his board with a face that said he was not going to find out. The king's voice was warm and rough at the edges, and he said the next words to the man with the sketchbook. "Draw that. The marks, not the stone. I want to remember what it does to a place."

Ysolde walked half a step behind him with a folder against her coat and a pen in her hand, and she looked at everything as if she were checking a list. Eden stood four paces from the stair with his spear grounded and his eyes on the king's boots, which was the rule and also useful, because boots did not look back.

"Your men look tired," the king said to Okoru.

"They have had a Choir silence, sir."

"How long?"

"Twenty-six hours."

"Too long." The king looked at the line of soldiers. "Give them a day of nothing when I have gone. That is an order."

"Yes, sir."

The king moved along the stretch with his party in a small tight cluster. The secretaries stopped to write, the painter stopped to draw, and the servant with the rug stopped to find a place to put it and failed. Only Ysolde kept pace, one step behind his shoulder, her eyes going from the king to the parapet to the soldiers and back. She was counting something that Eden could not see.

The king came to Rowan, whose gold light had made the day before easier and whose face was open enough that people told him things. "You are the one with the hands," the king said.

"Yes, sir."

"Do they ever get cold?"

"Always, sir." Rowan held them out because the king had asked. "They get warm when I use the light. Then cold again."

"Ah." The king touched two fingers to his own coat pocket and then took them away. "I was the same at your age. Cold hands. My father said it meant I had no fire in me." He moved on before Rowan could answer, and the painter drew Rowan's hands with a stick of charcoal in three quick strokes.

Eden kept his eyes down. The king passed him without stopping, which was the safest thing that could happen, and he was beginning to let his breath out when Ysolde came the other way along the line with her folder and stopped beside him.

"Wren," she said, in the flat voice she used for forms. "The grey office wants your report on the silence by the end of the week."

"Yes, ma'am."

She wrote something in her folder and went on. At the lee of the stair she stopped beside the king and spoke quietly enough that a soldier would have ignored it. Eden had been listening to the king's boots, and the wind changed while she spoke, so he heard one sentence.

"My lord, the second envelope is still in the safe," Ysolde said. "You asked me at the palace not to bring it."

"I asked you not to open it," the king said. "Bring it when we are back." Then he saw Eden looking and changed his face into a small polite smile, and said something about the cold.

Eden looked back at the boots.

* * *

By the time the inspection ended, the snow along the parapet had been trampled into a brown line and the painter had filled four pages. He drew the king's boots twice, the sergeant's board once, and Rowan's hands in the margin of the third page with a line underneath that Eden could not read. The king signed nothing and thanked no one by name, but he had stopped at every stretch and asked at least one question, and the soldiers on the line stood a little straighter when he had gone.

Lysette was waiting at the bottom of the stair when the inspection ended, with her book under her arm and her red band pulled straight. She had been writing while the king walked the wall, and the top line of the page had gone crooked at the end.

"I have to give you something," she said.

"You do not."

"I do, and it is not a gift. It is a piece of equipment." She took a scarf from her bag, long and grey-green with a narrow red thread at each end, and held it out without looking at him. The wool had been folded and folded again until it was no wider than a belt, and one corner had a small stitch where a thread had come loose and been tied back by hand. "You have no winter kit. The permit says you are delicate, and the uniform they gave you is for autumn."

"I am not delicate."

"The form says I must ensure you are warm. I have written 'cold hands' four times and they keep returning the form." She put the scarf against his chest and he took it because she had put it there. "Wear it so I am not stuck with your paperwork."

"It is very long."

"It goes around twice. Rowan showed me on a piece of rope, not on himself. Do not tell him I said that." She put her gloves back on, one finger at a time, and this time she did not say they were unnecessary. "I have written that you accepted it."

"Did you write that I was cold?"

"No." She looked at the wall over his shoulder. "I wrote that the weather was cold." She put the pencil in her book without adding anything to the page. She pulled her coat straight and stood a moment longer than she needed to, as if waiting for him to say something. He did not know what to say, so he said, "Thank you," and watched her decide whether that was acceptable.

"It is acceptable," she said. "Do not do it again."

The camp went on with its evening below, and Eden wore the scarf around his neck and then around his mouth when the wind came up from the grey country. He wore it the next day and the day after that. He wore it for the rest of the winter. It was a piece of equipment and not a gift. When steam from a cup got into it, it smelled faintly of thyme and soap.

On the morning the king left, the cars waited at the gate with their engines running. The servants loaded a basket of papers into the last car and tied the painter's sketchbook shut with a string, and one of the king's officers walked the wall once more to check that nothing had been left behind. He found a glove on a merlon, held it up, and someone below said it belonged to the painter, who came up the stair with his hands full and took it without thanking anyone. Theodric came down the line of soldiers and spoke to Okoru once, then walked past the Lances with a hand raised, not quite a salute and not quite a wave. When he came to Eden he stopped, and for one moment the tired eyes looked straight at him.

"Cadet Wren," the king said, in a voice low enough for the three nearest soldiers to hear and high enough for the wind to carry it along the wall. "Your mother would have hated the cold."

Then he got into the car, and the cars went down the hill one after another, and the wall had no sound in it except the bellwire answering itself in the distance, three strikes and a pause, over and over, until a clerk went out and stopped the hammer with his hand.
