# Chapter 34: The Cistern

Under the middle of the Wall lay a cistern with a stair from the inner passage. A channel ran out under the stone to the ditch. Water stood in it all year in the dark. In winter it came to a man's chest at the shallow end and over his head near the far wall.

"Everything in this place is a problem," Felix said, on the stair. His voice went down the stair and came back thin. He stopped talking at once, as if the stone had warned him.

"The Choir is in there," the sergeant said. "We know because the silence comes out of the drain at noon and goes back in at dusk. Something under the water is singing without a mouth. One more silence like the last one and half this stretch will be too tired to hold. Find it and end it before the next bell."

"How do we find a thing in a dark tank?" Rowan asked.

"You listen." The sergeant pointed at the water. "When the song is quiet you will hear it in your teeth. When it is loud you will not hear anything at all."

"Why not just drain it?" Felix asked.

"There is a sluice, but the sluice opens into the ditch and the ditch is watched. If the Choir knows we are coming it will move." The sergeant looked at each of them. "You are going in quietly and coming out quietly. That is the whole job."

"Can we speak down there?" Lysette asked.

"Keep it short. The thing works through sound, and a voice gives it a place to look." He handed Eden a coil of rope. "Tie them together. If one of you goes under, the others will feel it."

Rowan looked at the water. "How deep?"

"Deep enough." The sergeant turned toward the stair. "Nine minutes from now the Choir will start its noon song. Be finished before then, and do not make me come down after you."

Okoru had written the order, so Lance Nine went down first. The cistern was narrow enough that soldiers could not help them. Eden took the lead with a rope in one hand and a covered lantern in the other. The Choir worked through sound, and light gave its shadow a shape. Lysette came behind with her batons. Rowan followed with the spear, Clara with linen around her forearm. Felix last with his bow dry under his coat.

The water was cold enough that it went through the boots and into the knees in ten steps. It did not feel like water. It felt like a hand, a large careful one, closing around each leg in turn. Clara's teeth began to click, and she clamped her jaw to stop them. The sound of her not clicking was nearly as loud. Rowan counted under his breath, his lips moving. Felix put a hand on his shoulder and Rowan stopped, because the rule was no sounds and counting was a sound even when it was only breath. A ledge at the bottom of the stair led to the deep part. Curved stone walls rose around it with old lime in the joints. Eden saw the Choir's shadow before he saw the Choir: a great swollen shape on the wall with a slow pull to it, like something breathing under water. Its edge was clean where the lantern light touched it.

"Stop," Eden said, quietly, and the others stopped.

He tried the shadow first with the lantern half open. A thin bar of light went across the water and up the curved wall. The swollen shape moved away from it like a thing that did not like being looked at. He shut the lantern again. The shape came back to the same place, but an inch lower. The water under it went on pulling toward the outlet.

Lysette put a hand on his arm and pointed at her own ear, then at the wall. She had heard the rhythm change. It had gone from four beats to three. The third beat was late, as if something large were turning over in its sleep. Eden counted three more rounds and then held up one finger, which was the sign for wait, and she took her hand off his arm.

In the cistern the silence had a weight it did not have on the wall. It pressed on the ears from every side at once. The water moved against their legs in time with a rhythm no one was making with their feet. Eden put his hand on the wall and felt the pull go through the stone. It was the same pull he had felt at the drain two days ago, only closer.

Eden needed the thing that made the song, not its shadow on the wall. Felix crouched in the shallow water beside him. His ability was **[Trail]**: it showed him where people and things had moved. Here it showed a thin line in the air, the path of the current running from the far wall toward the outlet. The line stopped at a stone slab half under water near the deep end.

"Under that," Felix whispered. His eyes went wide. "It is not an animal. Someone put it there."

The rope passed from hand to hand and came taut when the deep part pulled at them. Lysette tied hers with a knot Eden had not seen before, a fat loop with a tail that pulled free with one tug. When he looked at her she touched the corner of her mouth with a gloved finger, which meant she had learned it from a book and was proud of it. He nodded. It was a good knot. In a place where a rope might be the only thing between a person and the dark, a good knot was a way of saying that she planned to come out. Eden tied his end around his forearm and waited while the others closed the gaps. He listened to the water against their legs because it was the only sound the Choir had not taken. The slab lay six steps away with the current going under it. The surface above it had a smooth place where nothing floated, as if the water went down instead of across.

Eden moved along the wall to the slab with his hand on the stone. The shadow moved with him but not at the same speed. That was the problem. A Door needed an edge that stayed still while he cut it. Every time he found the edge the breathing moved it an inch along the wall.

Rowan came up beside him with his spear held across his chest. "It is faster than the water," he said.

"No. The water is moving it."

"Then stop the water."

"I cannot." Eden looked at the channel, then at the slab, then at the swollen shadow on the wall. "I do not need to stop it. I need it to show me where the edge is for one breath."

Rowan held out both hands. Gold light came into his palms, small at first and then everywhere. **[Dawnbreak]** filled the cistern from the floor up in one breath, bright as noon under water, with no warmth in it and no sound. It was a thing Eden had seen painted on a chapel wall and never in a room: a light that did not come from a place but from the whole air at once, so that nothing in the cistern could stand behind anything else. Every shadow snapped into a hard line. The Choir's swollen shape stood out with its edge sharp and still. Under the slab, the water showed a seam of black where the current went in.

Eden knocked twice on the air beside the seam.

The Door opened in the Choir's own shadow: a black rectangle cut out of the swollen shape, with a bright edge from Rowan's light along one side. The slab went into it as the current pushed. Deep water closed over the space where it had been. Eden shut the Door on the shadow itself. The thing that had been singing went down into the dark with a sound like a hand closing over a bell.

The light went out. For one breath after it, Eden could still see the seam under the slab and the hard line of the shadow on the wall, the way a bright thing leaves a mark behind when you close your eyes. Then even that went, and the cistern was dark water and cold stone with five people standing in it. Rowan's hands dropped to his sides and he took one step backward. The cold came into his face the way it did after every use. "That is the last of it," he said.

The silence left all at once. Sound came back into the cistern in a rush. Water dripped from the vault. A boot scraped stone. Clara breathed through her nose, and Lysette's pencil moved on a page she had not remembered taking out. Felix put a hand on the wall. The sudden noise made the place feel wider than it was. Somewhere above them a bell rang once, and then another bell answered, and the wall had its voice back.

Rowan stood with his hands held out and looked at them. "It is warm when I do that," he said. "Then it is cold. I never know which part is me."

"Do not practice it," Okoru's voice said from the stair, which was not in the cistern and yet came down clearly now that sound had returned. "Not yet."

"Why not?" Rowan asked.

"Because the first time you use a thing is the only time nobody is waiting for it." Okoru came down two steps and stopped where the water touched his boots. "You will have reason to use it again. When that happens, I will tell you."

"Yes, sir."

Clara put a hand on Rowan's sleeve. "Your hands are shaking."

"Cold," he said.

"I know." She did not heal anything, and took her hand away.

Eden put his hand on the wall where the Door had been. The stone was wet and ordinary, and the seam under the slab had gone quiet. Gold light had made the shadow hold still for one breath and had made his doors come easier. He did not say that out loud. Okoru had just told Rowan not to practice, and a thing that made doors easier was worth keeping to himself.

They climbed out with the rope in the last light of the day. The Wall above them had the noise of men talking again. The conscripts on the parapet were laughing into the wind because the silence had been long and the return of voices felt like a gift. The sergeant came down to meet them and wrote on his board without asking what had happened below. When he had finished he showed them the line: ONE CLEAN CUT, NO DAMAGE TO THE STONE. "That is the report the office wants," he said. "The rest is yours, and I will not write it down."

Felix stood beside him with his bow across his shoulders and his hands in his pockets. "I do not like that," he said, without being asked. "It is bigger than anything on the maps."

"There is nothing on the maps past the Wall," Rowan said.

On the southern horizon, where the grey country met the sky, a shape taller than the clouds moved once and was still. Eden watched it from the cistern stair until the light went out of the sky, and it did not move again. He counted its height in towers and stopped at eleven because there was no twelfth tower to compare it to. Lysette came up behind him with her book closed and followed his eyes. She wrote one line without asking what he was looking at, and he did not read it. The shape stayed where it was. The bellwire along the wall gave one quiet click as the wind got under it. Nobody went to see which bell had answered.
