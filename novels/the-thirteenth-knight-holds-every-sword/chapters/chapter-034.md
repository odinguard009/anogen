# Chapter 34: The Cistern

Okoru gave them the plan in the stores hut at dawn. He wrote four lines on a round sheet and held it up where all five could read.

"The node is under the fourth parapet, in the cistern," he said. "It makes the silence, and the silence ends when it ends."

"How do we find it?" Rowan asked.

"You will hear it with your teeth before you see it." Okoru turned the paper around. "Line one: go down in a line and do not separate. Line two: do not speak once you are under the stone. Line three: the water is deep and the ledge is narrow, so keep your boots on the ledge."

"What is line four?" Lysette asked.

Okoru looked at Eden. "Do not listen to it."

"Does it speak?" Clara asked.

"It does not have a voice," Okoru said. "That is why you must not listen."

Felix said that was the strangest rule he had ever been given. Okoru said most good rules sounded strange at first, and he put the sheet in his coat so it would not get wet.

The cistern lay under the fourth parapet. Its stair went down in a turn so tight that the lantern on the step above lit only the next step below. Eden went first with **[Exa]** folded across his back. And Lance Nine came after him in a line with Felix at the end.

"How deep is it?" Rowan asked, and his voice came back thin from the stone.

"Deep enough," the sergeant had said, and that was the whole answer he had given them.

The Choir had moved under the wall the night before. The silence had come back at the third bell and stayed for forty minutes. Which was longer than any silence in the rotation. Okoru had sent them down with a plan. The plan had four lines, and the last line said: do not listen to it.

The stair turned eleven times, and each turn took more of the light. The air got colder and wetter as they went down. Rowan counted the turns out loud until the eighth. Then he stopped, because his voice came back wrong from the stone and he did not like it. Clara asked him what was wrong with it. He said it sounded like somebody else's voice following him one step behind. Felix said that was an echo. And Rowan said that an echo would not know his name. Nobody laughed because the stair had gone quiet around them.

Lysette stopped on the ninth turn and put a hand on the wall. "The stone is moving," she said.

"It is the water," Eden said.

"No," she said, and she took her hand away. "The stone is moving under the water."

The water at the bottom was black and still. It went down past the edge of the lantern light. The stone steps ended on a narrow ledge above the drop. A node hung in the middle of the cistern. It was bloated and pale. With a face made of too many folds, and it did not have a voice. Eden could feel it in his teeth. He could feel it in the water through the soles of his boots.

Clara stood on the ledge beside him and put her staff across her body. "It is listening," she said.

"I know."

"It is not one of the ones on the wall."

"No," Eden said. "This one is under the wall."

Lysette stood on his right with her batons out. She had written nothing since they came down, because a pencil on the page made a sound, and she did not want to give the node a direction.

A bubble rose in the water and broke. The sound of it went up the stair in a small echo. Then the silence came back heavier. Felix put an arrow on his string and looked along it. Then he lowered the bow. Because the node had no throat and no heart that he could see. An arrow would only make a hole in a thing that was already wrong. Clara put her staff flat across her chest and breathed twice. The second breath came out as fog. The fog went sideways because there was a wind under the cistern and no wind on the parapet above.

Rowan lifted his spear and his light came up in his palms. Apple-sized and soft, and the pale face of the node turned toward it. The light went out of his hands as if a mouth had closed over it. His face went blank with surprise. And Eden put a hand on his sleeve to stop him from striking.

"It does not like light," Rowan said.

"It does not like anything," Eden said. "It is not a person."

The node moved one fold of its face, and Eden saw its shadow on the water: a wide dark shape with the pale thing at its centre.

He knocked twice on the hard edge where the ledge shadow met the wall. A black opening appeared in the dark under the node. The node's own shadow went into it. And the pale body followed the shadow because the body and the shadow were the same thing in that light. There was no splash. The water closed over the place where it had been. And the dark shape on the water went flat and quiet. And Eden shut the opening with one hand on the stone.

Eden kept his hand on the stone and counted ten beats. And the water stayed flat, and the pale shape did not come back. Lysette put a boot on the edge of the ledge and looked down. And she said there was nothing there but deep water. And she wrote one word in her book with the pencil held tight. And the scratch of the pencil was the first small sound they heard.

Sound came back in a rush. The water slapped the steps, and Rowan breathed out hard, and Felix's boots shifted. Somewhere up the stair a bird began to call as if it had been holding its breath.

Clara reached him first and put a hand on his shoulder. He was still upright and his spear was still in his hand, but the light had gone out of his palms and left them grey. "I am all right," he said, and his teeth were chattering, and he said it again because the first time had not come out right.

Felix came up beside him and said, "You are not all right. Sit down." Rowan sat, and Clara put her staff across his knees and he held it with both hands. His colour came back slowly, as light comes back to a room when a lamp is turned up.

That was when Rowan saw it. He put his hand on Eden's arm and pointed up. And his mouth was open, and he was not looking at the stair.

Light filled the cistern in one breath. Rowan's **[Dawnbreak]** came up his arms and out through his spear head. And the gold filled the space from the water to the roof. And the shadows on the stone went hard and sharp. And Eden saw the shape of every crack in the cistern wall. It was bright as noon for one breath. And then it was gone, and Rowan's hands dropped, and he staggered back into Clara, who caught him by the coat.

"Doors," Rowan said, and his voice was thin. "They come easier."

Eden felt it too. The **[Dawnbreak]** light had left hard edges everywhere. And every edge held a dark line, and the lines were places a Door could open. He did not say that aloud. Because Okoru's fourth line had said do not listen. And a Door that came easier was a thing to notice and not a thing to explain.

Clara kept her hand on Rowan's arm until his colour came back, then let go and put her staff down. She looked at the black water and said a short word that Lysette wrote down later.

Lysette read the four lines on Okoru's sheet to herself. And she checked them off against what had happened. And she wrote a fifth line at the bottom which said that the relief had come too fast and that Rowan had gone grey. Okoru read the fifth line when they came up and said it was the best line on the page. Because a report that only said what went well was a lie told to a man with a clipboard.

They climbed out in a line, and the stair gave them back their voices step by step. Felix came last, and he turned at the top and looked down into the dark.

At the surface the camp was loud and the lamps were lit. And the men at the next stretch waved across to ask if the silence had ended. And Rowan waved back with both hands because he could not think of a shorter signal. Lysette wrote four lines in her book and gave the book to Okoru to read. And Okoru read it with his stick under his arm and said that it was the clearest report he had had all winter.

Dusk came early under the cloud. And Eden stood at the parapet with the scarf at his throat and the gloves in his coat. And he looked south over the dark land beyond the Wall. A silhouette stood on the horizon where the land met the sky. And it was taller than the clouds behind it. And it moved once, slowly, as if something very old had turned its head, and then it was still.

Eden watched it for eleven seconds, and then went down the stair to the camp. He told nobody what he had seen until the evening bell rang.

The camp ate in silence that night because the men on the next stretch had heard the rush of sound come up the stair, and they had seen Rowan sitting with his spear across his knees. A veteran brought a cup of tea and set it in front of him. He said light always took it out of a man the first time, and it got easier only if the man was careful. Rowan asked what happened if a man was not careful, and the veteran said there was a bed in the infirmary with his name on it. He walked away before Rowan could ask how he knew.

Lysette sat at the end of the table and copied her report a second time. She left out the part about the Door because Okoru had not asked for it, and put in the part about the light because he had. Eden watched her write and saw the word doors appear in her lines, get crossed out, and then appear again in a smaller hand. She caught him looking and put her hand over the page. "You may read it when it is filed," she said. "That is the rule." "I know the rule," Eden said. "Then you know why I am writing it twice," she said, and she went back to her page.

Felix ate four tins of beans and said the cistern smelled of old pennies. Clara said it smelled of still water, and Rowan said both were wrong because it had smelled of the inside of his mouth. Nobody asked him what that meant, and he ate his bread wet and did not say anything else about it.

Eden lay on his bed an hour later. The scarf was under his pillow and the gloves were folded on the crate. The wall was quiet above him, and the camp was settling into its night sounds. He kept seeing the hard edges Rowan's light had made, and each edge had a dark line, and each line was a place a Door could open. He counted the edges until he lost the count, and then he slept, and the dream did not come.

The next morning the sergeant asked for the number of Husks. Lysette said nine, Felix said ten, and Eden said eight because he had counted eight. The sergeant wrote down nine because he had to write something. He said a disagreement of two was normal after a silence, and that the line holding was the important thing. Rowan asked whether a line that held with eight was better than one that held with ten. The sergeant looked at him and said it was better not to be counted at all. Clara wrote that down in her own book, and Lysette copied it with the date, and Felix asked if it was a rule or a joke. "It is a saying," Lysette said, and she underlined it twice.
