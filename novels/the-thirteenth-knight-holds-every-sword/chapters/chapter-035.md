# Chapter 35: The Lodge

Rations week began with bread that had frozen hard enough to ring when it was set on the table, and a lottery to decide who got the two loaves that were not frozen. The lottery was run by a prefect with a tin cup and forty slips of paper, and he shook the cup for a full minute because Rowan had accused him of stacking it the week before. Frost had got into the camp stores and turned the sacks of flour grey at the edges, and the tea at breakfast had a skin of ice on it before anyone could drink it. Felix drew a number and read it upside down.

He held the paper up to the lamp to check it, and the number was the same either way, which Felix said was the only luck he had had all winter.

"Fourteen," he said. "Is that good?"

"There are forty of us," Rowan said. "So no."

"Fourteen is closer to forty than one is." Felix put the ticket in his pocket. "I am keeping it." He took it out again and looked at it, as if it might have changed. "In a year I will tell people I drew fourteen at the Wall in the worst winter in memory, and they will be impressed, and I will not mention the bread."

A courier from Ysolde came down the camp path with a folder held flat against his coat and a pencil behind his ear. He had come all the way from the palace in the cold and would not sit down, because Ysolde's couriers were paid by the standing.

"Form 13-B for every Husk killed," he said. "The grey office has changed the rule. A Husk is not a person, but it was once, so it gets a form."

"Who fills it in?" Rowan asked.

"You do." The courier looked at Eden. "You, actually. The office says you already have the form."

Eden took a blank 13-B from his coat and looked at the boxes. "There is no box for Husk."

"There is now." The courier turned the paper over and showed him a new line printed on the back, which said HOSTILE, FORMERLY PERSON. "I have four hundred of these. I will be here all week." He lined them up on the bench with the care of a man laying out a dinner set, and Rowan, who could not leave a neat row alone, picked one up and read it with moving lips. "Hostile," he said. "Formerly person." He put it down and was quiet for a moment. "That is a sad thing to print on a form."

"It is a form," the courier said. "They are all sad. That is how you tell they are important."

In his head, without opening his mouth, Gorrath said, "I am going to complain about this all night." His voice had the weight of a large man sitting down on a step, and it came from the direction of the stores rather than from Eden's own chest, which was something Eden had stopped trying to explain.

Eden did not answer him out loud. Gorrath's voice was low and tired and came from somewhere behind his left ear, and he had learned to let it finish.

"Nobody asked you," the Dragon said, from a door Eden did not look at. "He is the one who always has to be right about forms."

"I am not right about them," Gorrath said. "I am just here."

Seraphine arrived with a card in her hand and a scarf over her hair, which she had tied under her chin against the cold. She had been on the wall for two days with Lance One and had come down to trade posts with someone, which was something she did often enough that the prefects no longer asked her why.

"Seventy-six," she said to Felix.

"I have not laughed at anything."

"Seventy-six is a thing I said, not a thing you did." She wrote on the card. "You did not laugh at the bread. That is one."

"That was not a joke. The bread is a physical fact."

"Seventy-seven is a physical fact as well." She snapped her fingers and winked at him, and she was suddenly standing two steps to his left with her back to the wall and Felix sitting where she had been. **[Switcheroo]** had moved them within ten steps without either of them walking, and the snow under Felix's boots had not been disturbed. It worked when nobody was looking straight at the light; in the honest glare of a lamp it failed, and Seraphine had once swapped herself with a post and been stuck beside it for a minute. A candle on the table flickered backward once as she landed, and then burned normally.

Felix looked at the card in his own hand, then at Seraphine's empty seat, then at Seraphine. "I am going to be sick."

"No, you are not. You are going to say something funny now." She sat down in his place and put her boots on the bench opposite. "Say it."

"I hate you."

"Seventy-eight." She wrote it down and looked across the camp at Eden, who was sitting by the stores with Form 13-B on his knee. "I am going to trade posts again. I want the one beside Wren."

"You cannot just choose a post."

"I can snap my fingers." She did it again, and this time she came down beside Eden on the bench with her card in her hand and the cold off the wall in her hair. The candle on the stores table flickered backward once more. Felix, two benches away, kept his face in his book.

Eden moved along the bench to make room and put Form 13-B flat under his glove so the wind would not take it. "You are in my light."

"There is no light." Seraphine sat down with her legs tucked under her and looked at the wall above them, where the snow had a blue edge. She had taken off one glove to hold the card and her fingers were white at the tips, but she did not put the glove back on. The candle between them burned low and straight, and the smoke went up in a thin line that did not move when she spoke. "It was cold like this. At the lodge."

Eden kept his pencil on the form. "Which lodge?"

"The one in the north. The winter one." She turned the card over and drew a line on the back with her thumb. "Felix, come here. I am telling a story and I need you to hear it properly."

Felix came and stood in front of her with his book shut. Rowan drifted over because Seraphine had said the word lodge and he had heard it before in the hall, and Lysette, at the next table with her book open, put her pencil down without looking up.

"It was the winter after the court went away," Seraphine said. "There was snow up to the second step and the road was closed. My brother had gone ahead with the older children and I was with the ones who could not walk that far." She had stopped smiling, but her voice was still light, as if she were telling a joke and had forgotten the end of it. "The fires went out in the night. Nine of us came into the hall and sat under one table with a lamp that had almost no oil. It was cold like this. Colder, because there was no wall to stand behind."

Rowan crouched beside the bench. "What happened?"

"Someone found the door." Seraphine's thumb stopped moving on the card. "It opened from the outside. There was a boy standing there with a sword. He was no bigger than me. He said we had to come out because the roof was going to fall, and I said it was warmer under the table, and he said the roof was going to fall in four minutes."

Felix looked at her. "Four minutes?"

"Four." Eden said it before he had decided to, with his pencil still on the form.

Seraphine's eyes came to him and went back to Felix. "Yes. Four." She went on. "He counted us as we went out and he made us go in a line with the smallest first. I was not the smallest. I was fourth from the end, and I remember because I kept turning round to see if the last one was still there."

"Was he?" Rowan asked.

"Yes." She let out a breath that went into the cold air and disappeared. "The roof fell after we were out. It took the table and the lamp and the place where I had been sitting. I have never liked being under a table since." She looked up at the wall again. "He walked us to the main house. He did not carry anyone and he did not say he was a hero. He asked me if I was warm and I said yes, and he gave me his glove because I was lying."

Lysette's pencil moved at the next table. She wrote one word and then crossed it out.

"How many were there?" Eden asked.

"Nine," Seraphine said.

"Eleven."

"No." She turned to him, and her smile came back for one second and was gone. "Nine came into the hall. I counted them."

"Nine came in," Eden said. The number arrived with a picture of a hall under a table and a line of children moving toward a door. He said the next number before he could put the picture in the box of things that were none of his business. "Eleven went out."

Seraphine looked at him without moving, with the card in her hand. Then she said, "Yes. Eleven went out. I had forgotten the two by the door." She put the card in her glove and stood up. "That is the whole story. Tell me I did not tell it badly."

"You told it badly," Felix said. "You left out two people."

"Seventy-nine." She wrote it down and walked away toward the stair with her scarf over her hair, and Felix went after her with his book under his arm.

The camp went back to its noise. Gorrath said, in Eden's head, "You should not have corrected her number." The Dragon said, "He was never good at leaving a thing alone." Eden filled in the first box on Form 13-B and did not answer either of them.

At the table beside the stores, Lysette finished writing on a fresh form. Her hand shook once at the wrist, and she held it still with the other until the ink dried. The form was headed DISCIPLINE COMMITTEE: IMPROPER POST SWAP, and the name on the line was Seraphine Aurenmark.

Rowan went back to the bread table and broke his frozen loaf against the bench, and it split with a crack like a small shot, and half of it flew across the stores and hit the groundskeeper, who was sorting fuses by the wall. He looked at the bread, looked at Rowan, and said, "That is a good arm, young man. Tea for the tall one, two sugars." He hummed his four wrong notes and went on sorting, and Rowan, delighted, took the piece of bread to him with an apology.

Felix stood where Seraphine had left him with her card still in his hand and did not put it down. Lysette watched neither of them. She had turned the form so that the headings faced the wall, and the pencil lay across the corner where she had written the name twice.

She did not look up when Eden came to put his own form in the folder. "I have to write her up again," she said. "I do not know why my hand is doing that."

"Yes."

"Do not be pleasant about it." She held the form at the corner and the paper shook in the cold wind coming off the wall. "There were eleven of them. I have written nine in three places and I cannot change it now."
