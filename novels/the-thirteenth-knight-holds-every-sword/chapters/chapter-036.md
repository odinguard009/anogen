# Chapter 36: The Second Reading

"One of them is wearing a dead man," Vesk said. "I smelled it in the garrison this morning."

Eden was on the step outside the stores with the scarf pulled up over his mouth. The smell Vesk meant was the dry cold one from the exchange and the dye-works, and it did not come through the snow. It came through Vesk's report, which was enough.

"Which one?"

"Storems. The sergeant who runs the night roll. They like him. He has been here nine years and he knows everyone's name." Vesk said it with a kind of reluctant respect. "He remembers the name of every soldier's dog. That is how they take a place, you know. They do not break the door. They learn the dog."

"How many in the room?"

"Twenty at the change of watch, then twelve. He calls the roll at the long table with the lamps on." Vesk put one hand on the wall. "If you go in with the black plate they will remember it for a year."

"I will not wear it."

"Then how will you make the grey show?" Vesk's voice went thin. "You need light."

"Not my light."

Vesk looked at him for a moment and then looked away, because he could not follow Eden into a lit room and did not like being reminded of it. "There is a signal mirror in there," he said. "A soldier told me about it. It hangs on the west wall." Vesk's yellow eyes went to the garrison door. "He cannot be taken in front of soldiers. They would put blades through you before you reached him."

"Then I do not take him in front of soldiers."

Vesk looked at the sky. "It will be dark soon. I cannot go past the second lamp."

"I know."

Eden went into the garrison at the change of watch, when the men coming off duty crowded the lower hall and the ones going on duty pushed past them in the opposite direction. He kept his hands in his pockets and his eyes on the floor, which was the way a quiet student moved through a crowded place, and by the time he reached the stairs half the hall had forgotten he was there.

The night roll was called in an upper room with a long table and a board of names. Three lamps hung from chains over the table, and the light on the polished boards made a second set of edges under every object. A rack of roll books stood by the door with the current one open flat, and a box of pens had been left where anyone could take one, which told Eden that the soldiers who came here trusted each other about small things. Storems stood at the head of it with a pen in his hand, a big man with a red face and a laugh that had made him popular for nine years. He was reading out names in a slow voice and marking the answers. When the laugh came it went around the room like a mug passed from hand to hand.

"Wren," Storems said, when he came to the last line of a different list. "You are not on this roll."

"I am attached to Lance Nine." Eden had practised the answer on the stair. It had to sound like an answer a bored boy gave to a bored man, and he had put the stress on the wrong word on purpose.

"Then I will make a line for you." Storems dipped the pen and wrote EDEN WREN in a space that was too narrow for the second name. "Do you know why we do this at night, boy?"

"So we know who is here."

"So the officers know who is here." Storems looked round the room with the smile still on his face. "I like a full roll. It is a kind of company."

"So nobody is missing in the morning."

"That is the reason they give you." Storems' smile came a beat late, and the room laughed because they had heard the joke before and liked it again. His shadow stayed where it was for half a breath before it followed his shoulders.

Eden saw the shadow move late, and he saw the two things at once: It was a small lag, a finger of dark that stayed behind when the man turned, the sort of thing nobody would see unless they had spent three winters watching shadows for a living. The men at the table were looking at Storems's face, where the warmth was. Eden looked at the floor. the man's smile arriving after his eyes, and the grey under the neck where the collar stood away. He needed light to make the grey honest, and he could not draw **[Inead]** in a room full of soldiers who would remember a gold sword in the hands of a cadet.

He did not look at Storems. He looked past him at the window, where the last light of the day lay across a square of polished metal hanging on the wall: a signal mirror, used to flash messages to the far watch in clear weather. Its edge was a clean line and its face held what was behind him.

Eden knocked twice on the air beside the table leg where Storems' shadow fell across it.

The Door opened black behind the sergeant, and the cables of the room's ward-line leaned into it and went quiet. Two soldiers at the table looked up because the lamp flickered, not because they had seen a door. Eden drew **[Inead]** low at his hip where the table hid it, and turned the gold so that it struck the signal mirror and came back over Storems' left shoulder.

The light was honest when it bounced. The stolen face went to glass under it and the grey body showed: long, thin, folded, with too many joints and a mouth that ran from ear to ear. A soldier dropped his pen. Another said something that was not a word. A third one stood up so fast that his chair went over backward on the boards, and the sound of it filled the room and went on after everything else had stopped. Storems' laugh began and did not finish, because the mouth that made it was no longer shaped for laughing.

Storems moved fast. He took the lamp from the table and swung it at the mirror, and the mirror cracked across its face, and the gold light in the room broke into a dozen pieces. Eden had expected that. He had put himself where the broken pieces would fall on the Door rather than on the soldiers, and he stepped into the black rectangle as the lamp went past his shoulder.

Inside the Door there was no distance, only the cold and the edge. He came out behind the sergeant with **[Inead]** still low and his other hand on the iron ring of **[Exa]**. The reading in his head changed when he drew on the Door. It had been **[Knight]** and **[Black Lancer]** on the board in December; now a third line stood under them where there had been nothing before.

**[Void Heir]**.

His breath stopped for one beat while the line was there. He did not say it out loud. There were rules about names, and a name spoken in a room of soldiers was a thing that could not be taken back. He let the breath go and cut once from the shoulder down.

The grey body came apart and fell to the floor in a coat that no longer had a man inside it. The voices that had been layered in the dead sergeant's laugh stopped one by one: the old joke, the young voice, the woman who had not been there. The last one said nothing at all. Grey dust lay on the boards under the table and stuck to the cracks.

Eden let **[Inead]** go and folded **[Exa]** away into his shadow. The soldiers were still standing with their pens in their hands. One of them had written EDEN WREN on his own roll and underlined it, and he looked at the name and then at the dust on the floor and did not cross it out. A prefect reached for a bell and then stopped, because there was nothing to call anyone to and no one he could name as the reason.

"What was that?" someone said.

"Lamp broke," Eden said. "I will pay for the mirror." Nobody laughed. A corporal, white to the lips, said, "It was only a coat," and then said it again, as if the second time might help. Eden picked up the pen that had rolled from the roll and set it neatly beside the box where it had come from.

He wiped the dust where it touched the table legs, took Storems' roll book from the floor, and put it back on the table with the last line blank. When he left the room the soldiers were looking at the coat on the floor, and nobody had thought to stop him.

Vesk was waiting at the bottom of the stair in the dark beyond the second lamp. "Done?"

"Yes."

"Any noise?"

"One chair and a mirror." Eden held out his hand and looked at the soot on the cuff.

Vesk's nose worked once. "You smell of it. It stays on you after it dies. I told you it would."

"How long?"

"An hour in open air. Longer in a coat." Vesk stepped back from the stair. "Do not go near Hollen until it is gone."

Eden thought about that for the length of a breath. It was a strange instruction to receive from a man who could not enter a lit room: a rule about distance, from someone who had spent his life keeping to the edges of things. He said, "I will take the long way round the stores," and Vesk, who was already turning away, nodded as if that were the only sensible thing anyone had said all night.

Eden pulled the scarf up over his mouth and went past him into the cold. The smell was in his coat and in the sleeve that had held the sword, and he could not wash either before morning. He thought of the scarf, which was a piece of equipment and not a gift, and moved it a finger's width away from his collar.

The path down from the garrison had ice in the ruts and the snow at its edges was dirty from a week of boots. Eden kept to the middle where the ice was thinnest, and Vesk walked in the dark under the wall without making a sound. Eden, who had not yet said thank you for the warning, said it now, in four words, at the end of the path. "Thank you for the smell." Vesk looked at him as if he had been handed a coin of an unfamiliar country, turned it over, and decided to keep it. After that neither of them spoke until they were past the second lamp. A bugle went somewhere in the camp and was answered by another, and a soldier on the parapet above them began to sing because the silence had been lifted and singing was allowed again.

A veteran stood at the garrison door with a cup in his hand, watching the two of them go down the path. He had a face like a boot and a grey moustache, and he spat into the snow at the side of the step.

"Thirteenth Vigil work," he said, loud enough for the men behind him to hear. "Like the Hollen boy."

Lysette came round the corner of the stores with her book under her arm and her red band pinned straight. She had heard every word. She did not call after the veteran and she did not ask him what he meant, because a question asked in front of a man like that was an answer given to him. Eden kept walking. The distance between them stayed the same for eleven steps, and then she was no longer behind him. He counted the steps without deciding to. At the hut he wrote the number on the back of his hand with a pencil, then rubbed it off with his thumb.
