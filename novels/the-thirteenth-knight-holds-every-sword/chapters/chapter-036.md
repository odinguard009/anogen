# Chapter 36: The Mirror

Okoru gave the order at the camp gate before the sun was up, and he gave it without looking at Eden, because the gate was full of men going to their posts. "There is a job in the yard," he said. "Take it and do not come back until it is finished."

"What job?" Eden asked.

"The kind you do alone."

Rowan came up behind them with his belt half fastened and asked what kind of job a man did alone. And Okoru said it was the kind where a friend was a witness and a witness was a problem. He walked away with his stick under his arm. Rowan looked at Eden and then at the yard. He said that he had heard the first part and not the second. And Eden said that was the point of it.

Vesk found the Veil in the garrison yard at dawn. He did not come out of the shadow to say it. His voice came from behind a stack of crates. It was flat and quiet, and it said one word: sergeant.

Eden had been mending a lamp on the yard wall with a screwdriver in his hand. He put the screwdriver in his pocket and looked across the yard. The sergeant who trained the conscripts stood by a signal mirror on a post. He was showing a private how to turn the glass toward the sun. His smile came a beat after the private said something. Stayed after he stopped smiling.

Eden had seen that smile before, and the sight of it put a cold line down his back that had nothing to do with the weather.

"Which sergeant?" he asked, and he did not move his lips much.

"The one they like," Vesk said. "The one who gives out the extra socks."

"How long?"

"Three days, perhaps four." Vesk stepped back into the shadow under the crates. "The soldiers will defend him, and they will do it before they ask a question."

Umra closed over Eden without a sound. The edges of the yard grew sharp: the rim of the mirror. The line of the sun on the stone, the rail of the stair. He drew **[Exa]** and folded it into the shield. He went along the wall with the shield low and the sun behind him.

Lysette was on the other side of the yard with a round sheet in her hand. She saw him come out of the shadow of the doorway and started toward him, then stopped, because Okoru had told her to stay where she was.

The sergeant saw Eden and waved him over. "Wren! Come and look at this glass, and tell me if it is bright enough to blind a man on the next wall."

Eden went, and the mirror was a round piece of polished metal on a swivel, and it threw a hard white spot onto the stone at their feet. He put the shield between his body and the yard so that the soldiers saw the drill posture and not the black plate. He knocked once on the iron ring so that the shield sat square on his arm.

"It is bright," Eden said.

"Bright enough for a signal," the sergeant said, and his smile arrived late again. "Hold the glass for me, and I will show the private the angle."

He handed Eden the mirror and Eden took it, and the sun caught the polished face. The white spot went across the yard and into the sergeant's eyes. Eden tilted the mirror one degree. The light went into the man's eyes. The paper skin showed through. His shadow on the stone lagged half a step behind him as he moved.

The private with the mirror dropped it. The metal rang on the stone. The white spot of sun went up into the sky and vanished. A corporal put himself in front of the sergeant with his arms out. Two more soldiers came round the crates with their hands on their blades. And they were not looking at the paper face or the lagging shadow. Because they had known the man for three days and they had not known the thing under him at all.

"Back," Eden said. He put the shield between the soldiers and the Veil and kept his voice flat. "I need him alive long enough to be sure."

"He is our sergeant," the corporal said, and his jaw was set.

"I know."

The soldiers around them went quiet. A private said the word Veil. And another soldier put a hand on his sword hilt. And then stopped, because the sword was pointed at a man they had eaten with for three days.

"No," Eden said, and his voice was flat and carried across the yard. "Stand back."

"That is our sergeant," a soldier said.

"I know."

The Veil came at him with its hands open. Eden drew **[Inead]** from under the shield. And the gold sword made one clear note in the yard. And the light from it laid the truth over the man's face: the skin turned thin as paper. The eyes became two dark seams, and the grey body showed under the uniform. Eden put the mirror up again and bounced Inead's light into the Veil's face. And the grey inside it went pale and honest for one beat, and he cut once.

Eden felt the register before he saw it. The way a man feels a step missing on a stair. In his mind the line under his name held **[Knight]** and **[Black Lancer]**. And while the gold was in his hand a third line stood under them. And it read **[Void Heir]**. He did not say the words. And he did not look at the soldiers. And he held his breath for one beat because the third line had never stood there in daylight before. Then the line went away and the breath came back. And the sword's light was only light again.

The body came apart into grey dust and fell in a soft line onto the stone, and the uniform fell with it. They landed at different times, and the soldiers did not move.

A **[Door]** opened in the shadow under the crates, and Eden put the shield and the sword into the dark and they were gone, and Umra folded into his own shadow, and the yard held only a student with a screwdriver in his pocket and a crowd of soldiers looking at a grey stain on the stone.

A veteran spat on the ground beside the stain. "Thirteenth Vigil work," he said, and he said it loudly enough for the whole yard to hear. "Like the Hollen boy."

The corporal went down on one knee beside the grey dust and put his hand out. And then took it back before he touched it. A soldier behind him began to say a prayer and stopped halfway through. And another soldier turned away and was sick against the wall. The sun came up over the gate and the yard got brighter. And the grey dust on the stone did not look like a man at all.

Lysette Hollen stood ten paces away with the round sheet in her hand. And her face changed in a way Eden could not read. And she did not write anything down. He had counted the facts in the space of one breath: PR 110 was six years ago. And he had been ten at the time. And the file called it a confirmed kill. And he had never marked a confirm without seeing the grey. He had seen the grey. He could not recall the face. And he had never been able to recall the face. And he had written that down once in a notebook that he had burned.

The veteran said the name again. One soldier said the Hollen boy had been a traitor and the Vigil had been right to end him, and another said that nobody had seen the black knights up close.

Eden put the screwdriver back in his pocket and walked toward the gate with his hands at his sides. He did not look at Lysette, because she had not asked the question yet.

Vesk came out of the shadow beside the gate and walked with him, and his voice came low from the edge of the wall. "The smell stays after it dies," he said. "The Veil smell. It stays in the coat and in the stone, and no amount of rain takes it out."

"I know," Eden said.

"You smelled it today and you did not react."

"I have smelled it before." Eden looked at the yard behind them, where the soldiers were putting the grey dust into a tin. "That is the part I cannot explain."

The veteran was still in the yard when Eden passed the gate. And he said the words again for the men who had come late: Thirteenth Vigil work. Like the Hollen boy. Eden kept his face still and counted the steps down the stair. And he heard Lysette behind him say the veteran's name in a voice that was quiet and careful. And then he heard the veteran say that he had only said what everybody knew. And that a boy with a dead relation was a boy people talked about.

Vesk did not answer, and Eden went through the gate and down the stair, and he wrote one line in his notebook before the bell rang: "PR 110. Only him. Six years." He looked at the line for a while and then wrote "I did not see the face" underneath it. Then he crossed it out, because it was not a fact he could prove.

The mess hut was full of the job by midday, and the men at the tables spoke about the mirror and the grey dust as if they had been closer. Rowan asked whether the light had shown the thing before it moved. Eden said the light showed what was already there, and Rowan said that was not what he had meant. Clara put a cup of tea in front of him and told him to drink it while it was hot. He drank it and said nothing about the third line in the register.

Felix asked whether the Veil had been the sock sergeant, and Eden said it had. Felix said he had thought so and that it was a poor trick that made people slow to draw. Lysette wrote that down without looking up, and then wrote a second line saying the soldiers had defended the thing before they knew what it was. She said that this was not stupidity and that she would not have it called that.

The veteran came into the hut at the end of the meal and stood by the door, and he told Lysette he had meant no harm by the words. He said the Hollen name had been in the paper that morning as well. Lysette stood up and asked him to say the name again, and he did. She wrote it on a fresh page, showed it to him, then tore the page out and folded it into her pocket. "That is for my mother," she said, and she walked out of the hut with her head up.

Eden walked the wall alone after supper, and the mirror had been taken down from its post and locked in the stores, and the place where the dust had been was swept clean. He stopped at the stair and looked at the stone, and he tried to remember a face behind the paper skin, and there was only a shape with a hook in it, and the hook turned out to be the corner of a uniform. He counted his pulse and got seventy-two, and he wrote the number down and wrote nothing else. He put the notebook away and went down. The bell rang.
