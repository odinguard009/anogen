# Chapter 38: The Fire

Rowan came to the hut door and asked Eden whether he was coming to the fire. And Eden said that he was. And Rowan said that was the fastest answer he had had all week. He stood in the door with his hands on his hips and waited. And Eden put his book on the bed and went out with him. The yard was cold and the stars were sharp above the wall.

"Did you eat? Rowan asked.

"Yes."

"Felix says you never eat."

"Felix says a lot of things."

"He says them in a quiet voice, and he is usually right. Rowan walked a step ahead and then stopped. "Lysette is already down there."

"I know."

"She has been sitting there since the bell."

The fire was built in a circle of stones behind the stores hut. The men from the other stretches came with their own tins and sat where there was room. The flames threw their shadows up the wall. Eden came down with his hands in his pockets and sat at the edge of the circle where the light was thinnest. He did not say anything when Rowan moved closer to make room.

The bread was thawed this time. And Felix toasted a piece on a stick and burned it. He said that a burned piece of bread was still bread. And Clara said that was not a rule anyone needed. He gave her the burned piece anyway. She ate it and said nothing about the taste, which was its own kind of kindness.

Rowan asked Lysette to write down a new joke for the ledger. Because Felix had said something funny at the coal pile that morning and there had been no witness with a book. And Lysette said that a joke without a witness was not a joke. It was a noise, and Rowan said that was the funniest thing she had said all week. She wrote a line in her book and underlined it. And Felix looked at the line and then away. His ears went red in the firelight.

A soldier from the next stretch asked Rowan whether the school still had fires like this. And Rowan said that the school had a fire in the great hall on feast days and that it was bigger than this one. The soldier asked whether there were speeches. And Rowan said there were always speeches. The soldier said that was the difference between a school fire and an army fire. That an army fire was better for it. Felix said nothing, but he moved his tin closer to the warm side of the circle, and Clara moved with him without being asked.

More men came and the circle grew wider. They talked about the summer postings and the price of boots and the man who had stolen a crate of socks from the quartermaster in NovemberThen it went quiet in the way a fire circle goes quiet when a person decides to say something.

A man at the back asked whether the Wall had always been this cold. The old soldier beside him said it had been colder in his father's time. That not one man had been alive to enjoy it, and the men laughed. Rowan passed the tin of bread around a second time. And Lysette took a piece and put it on the stone beside her. She did not eat it.

Lysette put her book on her knee and shut it. She looked into the fire for a while.

"My brother was executed six years ago," she said, and her voice was plain and steady. "His name was Corentin, and he was in the Justiciar Seal, and they called it treason, and the paper said one line about it, and my mother has not said his name since."

Nobody in the circle moved, and a man at the far side put his tin down slowly, because the tin made a sound on the stone and he did not want to interrupt.

"He laughed all the time," Lysette said. "That is the part people leave out. He would laugh at a broken chair or a spelling mistake. My mother said he would laugh at his own funeral. He said that if he did, she should not be embarrassed. He came home from court in his last year and he could not dance. He stood by the wall at every party. He turned the button on his cuff with his thumb. When my mother asked what was wrong he said it was the heat. It was December."

She stopped and put a stick into the fire. The stick caught and went orange. She watched it burn down to the middle before she went on.

"The treason was never explained," she said. "Not to me, and not to my mother, and not in the paper. A file was closed, and a name was left off a list, and that is how it was done. I was twelve, and I wrote him three letters, and I put them in a box because there was no one to send them to, and I still have the box."

Felix put a hand on the ground between them and did not touch her. The hand stayed there. Eden thought about a boy turning a button by a wall. He kept his face still and looked into the fire.

"A veteran near the door took off his hat and held it in his lap. Another man who had been laughing a moment before stopped. The fire made a soft sound as a piece of wood broke in the middle and fell into the coals. No one told her to go on. No one told her to stop. She sat with her hands flat on her book. As if it were a paper she had to read from.

He was older than me by four years," Lysette said. "and he was the only person in that house who did not care if I won an argument. He would let me win and then tell my mother that she had won as well. Which made us both angry, and he would laugh. She shut her eyes for a beat and then opened them. "I do not have a picture of him that looks like him. The portraits are all from his court years. He is standing straight in every one of them. He never stood straight in a room in his life."

A veteran at the back said something very low that Eden did not catch. The man beside him told him to be quiet. The fire went on making a small sound, like a page turning. Eden kept his eyes on the fire and counted the sticks. There were nine, and one burned at both ends, and that was a thing he did not need to write down. He heard Lysette turn a page even though she had shut the book. He knew she had opened it again because her hands wanted work. He kept looking at the fire because it was the only place where he did not have to see her face.

The fire went on burning with a small sound like paper being turned.

Lysette looked across the circle then. Her eyes went past Rowan and Clara and Felix and stopped somewhere near Eden. He could not tell whether she had seen him.

"I have wanted to know for six years," she said, and her voice did not rise. "If I ever find the one who did it, I will not kill him. I will make him tell me why."

The circle stayed quiet, and the fire settled, and a spark went up and died before it reached the wall. Rowan put a hand on Lysette's sleeve and then took it back. Because he did not know if he was allowed. And Clara put another stick on the fire. And Felix sat with his hands closed around his knees and looked at the ground.

Eden said nothing. He had told himself in the yard that he always confirmed and that a confirm was a thing with grey in it. That rule had been true every time he had used it. He could not prove it to a room full of soldiers and he could not prove it to the girl across the fire. He could not prove it to himself. Because the face on the page had gone and the shape in his mind kept changing. He was the only person in the circle who knew that the question had an answer. He was the only person who could not give it.

After Lysette walked away, Rowan stood up and said he would go and find her, and Clara told him to sit down, and he sat. He asked her why, and she said he would know in a minute. He did not know in a minute, but he stayed. Felix looked at the place where Lysette had sat and then at Eden. He opened his mouth, and Eden shook his head once, and Felix shut it again, and he was the only one who had seen the small movement.

The men went to bed in twos and threes. The fire burned down, and Lysette stood up and took her book. She waited a moment as if someone might speak. Then walked away with her head up and her shoulders straight.

Eden stayed until the coals went grey. Then he put his boot on the edge of the stone circle and pressed it down. He sat with his hands in his lap and the night air coming over the wall and did not go to bed until the last bell.

The next morning Lysette was at the coal table with her book open and a fresh line in it. And she did not look up when Eden came in. And she did look up when Rowan came in behind him. She asked Rowan whether the east rail had been reported. And he said that it had. And she said that a reported rail was a safe rail. And Rowan said that a safe rail was a boring thing to write about. And she said that the report was not a story and did not need to be interesting, and he said that was exactly what made it interesting. Felix came in with four tins and put one in front of Eden. And he asked whether he had eaten. And Eden said that he had. And Felix said that was a good answer and sat down.

Clara took the tin that Lysette had left on the stone the night before and put it on the table. And it was still full of bread. And she set it beside Lysette's cup without saying anything. Lysette saw it and shut the book. And then she opened it again and wrote one line. And the line was a number and nothing more. And Eden wondered what the number counted, and he decided not to ask.

The wall was quiet all day. And the men went about their work in the usual way. And the only strange thing was that nobody made a joke about the fire. And that lasted for a week.

"Is it true? Rowan asked the next morning.

"Is what true? Felix asked. He stopped cutting his bread and looked at Rowan.

"About her brother."

"I do not know. I was not there. He said it into his cup and did not look up.

"Do you think it was the black knights?"

Rowan looked around the table, and his voice went lower.

"I do not know that either."

"Clara, do you know?"

"I know that she did not eat her bread."

"Why not?"

"Because she was thinking about something else."

"Can a person do both?"

"Eat and think? Yes."

"Can a person think and not eat?"

Clara put her spoon down and looked at the cup across the table.

"Yes. Lysette does it every day."

Rowan looked at his bread and then at Clara. And then he ate his bread. And he did not ask another question. Which was a thing he did about once a year. Felix watched him do it and said nothing. And his face was calm, and Eden knew that he had decided something about Rowan that morning and would not say what it was.

"Where were you last night? Lysette asked Eden at the coal table.

"Here."

"At the fire?"

"Yes."

"You did not say anything."

"I do not say much at fires."

"You said nothing all week. She shut her book. "That is also a thing a person can write down."

Eden looked at the book and then at the fire and then at the wall. And he said nothing, and Lysette wrote a line and shut the book, and she put it in her coat and walked away with her cup, and the cup was empty, which was a fact he had not noticed until she went.

Felix came to stand beside him. "She is not asking you to speak."

"I know."

"She is telling you that she noticed."

"I know."

"Do you want me to say the rest?"

Felix looked at the wall and then at the fire.

"No."

"Good. Felix put a bun on the coal table. "Eat it. I made it."

"It is burned."

"Yes."

"Then why give it to me?"

"Because you will eat it anyway. Felix walked away, and Eden ate the bun, and it was burned, and it was warm in the middle, and he did not say anything about either of those facts.

The wall went on with its ordinary work. And the men checked the parapet and counted the tins and cleaned the lamps. And the silence did not come back that week. And every morning the birds on the warment called at dawn as if nothing had happened. And Eden counted them because they were a thing with a number.

"Did you sleep? Felix asked Eden at the end of the week.

"Yes."

"All night?"

"Most of it."

"Do you dream?"

"Sometimes."

Felix put his cup down and looked at him for a while. And then he said that he had asked because he had a dream once that he could not shake. And that he had never told anyone what it was. And that it was not a funny dream and he was not going to turn it into a joke. Eden waited, and Felix looked at his hands, and then he said that the dream had a road in it and a cart that would not move, and that he had been the only one pushing, and that he had woken up still pushing. Eden said that sounded like a cart with a bad wheel. And Felix stared at him for a moment and then laughed. And the laugh was short and surprised. And he said that was joke seventy-nine whether Eden liked it or not.

Clara came past with her staff and asked what was funny. And Felix said that Eden had made a joke. And Clara looked at Eden, and Eden said that he had described a wheel, and that a wheel was not a joke, and Clara said that was exactly why it was one. She wrote nothing in a book. Because she did not keep one. And she went on down the yard with her staff on her shoulder. And Felix watched her go with his cup in his hand. And then he drank the tea and made a face because it was cold.
