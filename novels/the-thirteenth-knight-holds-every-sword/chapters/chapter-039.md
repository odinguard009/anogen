# Chapter 39: The Oil Line

Rowan asked the question at the coal table in the morning. He asked it in and he asked everything, with no warning and no softening. "Why does Lysette hate you? he said, and he put his cup down while he waited for the answer.

Eden put his spoon in his cup. "I do not know."

"She writes you up, and she follows you, and she looks at you like you are a broken tool she has to carry."

"She has a job to do."

"A job is not a reason to look at someone like that. Rowan sat back. "I have asked her, and she says you are a mystery, and then she writes something down, and then she will not say anything else."

"Then leave it alone."

"I cannot. Rowan looked at the table, and then at Felix, and then at Eden again. "You are my friend, and she is my friend, and you two stand three feet apart every day and pretend that nothing is happening. That is a thing a person notices."

Felix said nothing, and he took a piece of bread from the plate and broke it into two pieces, and he gave one to Rowan, which was not what Rowan had asked for and was probably an answer of its own.

Eden thought about it for a while. The kettle on the stove went quiet as it boiled away. The cold came in under the hut door and went around their boots.

"She does not hate me," he said.

Rowan waited, and he scraped his spoon against his cup, and the sound went around the table until he stopped.

"That is all I can say."

"You are shutting the door, and you know which door it is."

"It is a true door."

Rowan looked at him for a while. His face went still with the effort of waiting. Then he said, "I do not understand it, and I do not think you are trying to make me understand it," and he picked up his bread and ate it, and he did not ask again.

Clara had been sitting at the end of the table with her staff across her knees. She said, "He told you as much as he can," and Rowan said that he had noticed that as well, and Clara said that noticing it was not the same as understanding it, and Rowan said that he was beginning to hate the difference between those two things. Clara said that he was not. She put a hand on his sleeve for one beat and took it away. The subject ended there because Okoru came into the hut with a length of oiled cord in his hand and told them all to come outside. He did not say what the cord was for. And Felix asked, and Okoru said that a cord was a cord, and that a man who needed to know before he walked had no business on a Wall in winter. Felix said that was a fair answer and put his cup down. Rowan asked whether the story was about the cord. And Okoru said that the story was about a man with one arm and a great deal of patience. That both things were true in their own way.

The wall was grey and the sky was lower than it had been. The men were working along the parapet in pairs. And Okoru took them to a quiet stretch behind the third lamp where the stone was dry. He sat down on a step with his back against the wall and his one arm across his knee. He told them a story about a man with one arm who had held the cistern in the first winter of the Wall.

"They rang every bell on the wall that night," he said. "All at once, and no hand on a rope. The man was down in the water with his threads out. He tied the gate shut with one arm and a knot. When he came up the bells stopped. He said that he had not rung them. That the cistern had rung them itself because it was glad to be closed."

Rowan asked who had tied the knots if the man had only one arm. And Okoru said that a one-armed man could tie a knot with his teeth and his knee. That he had seen it done. Clara asked whether the man had still had his teeth. And Okoru said that he had. That this was the part of the story people always asked about first. Felix asked how long the man had held the gate. And Okoru said that the story said all night. That stories made time longer. That six hours in a cistern in winter was a night by anyone's count.

Rowan asked whether that was true. And Okoru said that it was a Wall story and that a Wall story was true in and a map was true: it showed you where to stand. Not what had happened. Eden wrote one line in his notebook about bells with no hands on the ropes. He put a mark beside it, and he did not say what the mark meant.

After the story, Okoru gave Eden a tin of oil and a brush and told him to lay a line under the camp stores where the lamp men would see it. "For the lamps," he said.

"For the lamps," Eden said, and he took the tin and went to the stores with the brush in his hand.

Lysette was in the stores counting tins when he came in. She did not ask what he was doing. He did not explain, and he got down on his knees behind the crates and painted a line of oil on the underside of a beam where the lamp men would pass with their lights. The oil was thick and dark. It smelled of pine, and it took a long time to dry in the cold, and he was still on his knees when she came past with her book.

"You missed a spot," she said. She pointed at the beam with the end of her pencil. Then she walked on with her tins.

The stores smelled of oil and tins and old grey dust. The crates went up in two rows to the beam. The beam had a scar along one edge where a lamp had been hung for years. Lysette put her tins on the counter and took out her pencil. She read the numbers on the tins in a low voice. She wrote them down, and after a while she stopped reading the numbers and listened to the brush instead. Eden heard her stop, and he kept painting, and he did not look up, and the oil went on the beam in a dark line that did not wobble.

Eden painted the spot, and he wondered whether she had seen the shape of the line, because it was not a line for lamps, and the lamp men did not need oil on the underside of a beam behind crates. It was a mark for a opening: a clean dark edge between the oil and the stone. It held a shadow in the morning light. He painted the last inch and put the brush in the tin. He sat back on his heels and looked at the line. It was straight, and it was his.

Vesk came out of the shadow under the crates while he was still kneeling. And the little man's eyes were lamp-yellow in the dark.

"There is a gate under this camp," Vesk said.

"I know."

"No, you do not. Vesk looked up at the beam and then at the dark line of oil. "The maps say the Mawgate is three days south of the Wall. And the maps are wrong. It is closer than that, and it is under the stone you have been sleeping on for six weeks."

Eden looked at the line of oil and then at the crates and then at the floor. And he put the brush across the tin so that it would not drip. He had seen a door in the shadow of a chandelier and a door in the shadow of a Choir node. And both times the dark had been thin and clean. And here the dark under the beam was neither thin nor clean. And it was warm in a way stone should not be. He put his glove flat on the floor and waited. And the cold came up through the leather and then went away. And came back again, and that was not how stone behaved.

Eden kept his hand on the tin. "How close?"

"Close enough that the ground is cold in a way stone should not be. Vesk stepped back into the shadow. "And close enough that something under here has started to listen when you open a door."

Rowan came to find Eden at the stores an hour later. And he stood in the doorway with his hands in his pockets and looked at the oil on the beam.

"Is that for the lamps? he asked.

"Yes."

"The lamp men will be pleased."

"They have not complained."

"They complain about everything. Rowan came in and put his hand on the counter and then took it away because there was oil on it. "Clara says I should stop asking you questions."

"She is right."

"She says you answer them, and that you never give anything away, and that a person can only listen to that for so long before they start to feel stupid. He looked at the line of oil and then at Eden. "I do not feel stupid. I feel like there is a room in you with the door shut. And I keep knocking because I can hear you moving around in there."

Eden wiped the brush on a cloth and put it in the tin. And he thought about the room, and he said, "There is a room."

Rowan's face changed, and he stepped back a half pace, and then he smiled, and the smile was slow and not happy. "That is the first true thing you have said to me in a month."

"I do not have the key."

"You would say that even if you did. Rowan turned to the door and then stopped. "I will keep knocking anyway. You should know that."

"Yes," Eden said. "I know."

Clara came past with her staff and stopped between them. And she looked at the oil and at Rowan's face. And she said that the wall needed both of them and that they should come up before the bell. And she went on up the stair without checking whether they followed. Rowan went after her, and Eden shut the tin and put it on the shelf, and he was the last to leave the stores, and he shut the door behind him.

The oil line dried in the cold and went black. And the lamp men came through at dusk with their lights and did not look at the beam twice. And in the night Eden heard the sound of a door opening under the camp and closing again. And he lay on his bed with his eyes open and counted the seconds between the two sounds, and there were nine.

In the morning there was a thin line of ash under the beam where the oil had burned in the night, and the lamp men swept it away before anyone else came, and Eden watched them from the stair and did not go down to tell them what it was. He wrote one word in his notebook and put the book away: nine. He ate his bread that morning and drank the tea, and he went to his post and stood there with the scarf at his throat. The wall was quiet, and the birds called at dawn, and he counted them all.
