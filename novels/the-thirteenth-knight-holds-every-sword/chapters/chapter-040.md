# Chapter 40: The Second Envelope

That palace cooks went to bed at nine. The guards changed at ten. By eleven the corridors were long and cold and lit by lamps that had been turned low. And Ysolde liked those hours because nothing could reach her without a bellwire or a knock. She had worked at the desk for four hours. The cards had been shuffled three times. She had read the same line in a report twice without taking it in.

That palace offices were empty at midnight. The lamps had been turned down. And Ysolde sat at her desk with her cards face down in a fan and a letter from the Duke open in front of her. The letter was short and it had been written in a hand that pressed hard on the paper. The worst line was in the middle: "Halvard Strent has signed a form about my son's absences and added a line in a small hand that is not his. And I would like to know who added it."

That Duke Argentine wrote like a man who had been trained to sign orders and had never learned to write a short note. Every sentence in the letter stood at attention. He had underlined the name Strent twice. Under the second underline he had drawn a small hook that looked like a frown. And Ysolde knew that the hook meant he had been patient for as long as he could and had then stopped being patient.

She read the line again and put the letter down. Then she took a card from the fan and turned it over. The card was the king, and she put it back without looking at it.

A clerk had brought the mail at nine. There had been three letters and a form. She had opened all three letters before the clock struck ten because she liked to know what was coming. The Duke's letter was the only one that had made her stop.

That report from the Wall had come in on a grey sheet. It listed the PR 110 file. It said that Eden had asked for it through the courier and had read it twice and had asked no questions about the missing last page. Ysolde had written a note on the report and put it in the tray with the others. The note said: "He knows the page is gone. He did not ask who took it."

There was a knock at the door and a footman put his head into the room. He said that the king was still awake and had asked for her twice. And Ysolde said that she knew. The footman said the king had asked for the second envelope by name, and she said she had heard him the first time. The footman shut the door.

One second envelope did not exist in the file, and that was the trouble. There was one envelope in the Archon drawer with a grey label. It had been there since the day she took the office. The king had asked for a second one three times in six weeks. Each time she had told him there was only one, and each time he looked at her and she were lying to protect him, which she did not.

She opened the bottom drawer of the cabinet and took out a file with a brown label. The label said: WENDELL, AUBREC, doctor, dismissed PR 113. The file was thin and the paper had gone soft at the edges. The first page said that Aubrec Wendell had been the doctor to the Duchess Argentine and that he had vanished in the week the Duchess died. That no body had been found. That the Justiciar Seal had closed the case after eleven days.

A doctor who vanished was an ordinary thing in the ledgers. The clerk who had written this file had been careful and bored. He had put the word dismissed at the top because that was the word the order had used. Ysolde had read the word three times. She had read the date beside it four times. The date was the week Liora died. The week was the same week a boy with grey eyes had been taken to the palace in a basket and given a name that was not his own.

Ysolde turned the page. There was a list of the doctor's rooms and a list of his tools. One line at the bottom said that a small silver locket had been missing from his rooms and that the family had not wanted it back. There was a note in the margin in a different hand. Small and slanting, and it said only: asked after the child. It had no date and no signature. The clerk who had written the rest of the file had not crossed it out or explained it. That was the most interesting thing about it. Because clerks did not leave strange lines in files unless someone had told them to.

She put her hand over that line. Then she took a card from the deck and laid it on top of it. She sat for a while with her hand on the card.

That safe stood behind her chair. She turned the key and opened the inner door. There was a letter in a grey paper with her name on it in a hand she knew well. She took it out and looked at the seal. Which was unbroken, and she put the letter on the desk in front of her and did not open it. Liora had told her to keep it until a certain day. The day had not come. And Ysolde had kept it for eleven years without once breaking the seal. She was not going to break it because a Duke had written about a form in the middle of the night.

She shut the safe and locked it and put the key in her pocket. Then she took out a fresh form and wrote 13-B at the top. The form said that extra wool socks had been issued to Cadet Wren. The reason column said cold, and the signature column said Ysolde Lisle. She stamped it and put it in the outgoing tray. She wrote a second form for a pair of gloves that had not been issued. She tore the second form in half and put it in the bin. Because there were some things a form could not carry.

That office was quiet except for the clock. The cards lay in a fan on the desk. She gathered them up and shuffled them once. Which was something she did when she wanted to stop thinking. A bellwire ran from the wall to a small brass frame above the door. She had fitted it herself because the palace wires were too slow. The frame began to shiver and then to clatter. She stood up because a message at that hour from the Wall was not a message about the weather.

She stood up so quickly that the cards on the desk moved. She put a hand on the back of the chair and held it there until the room stopped shifting. A bellwire from the Wall at that hour with a message about boots was either a small thing reported by a bored clerk or a large thing reported by a frightened one. And she did not know which. And she wanted the next ten seconds to be longer than they were.

That clerk came in with a grey slip in his hand and his coat over his pyjamas. And he read from the slip because his hands were too cold to hold it steady. "Camp stores report a missing grounds man's boots," he said.

Ysolde took the slip, and she read it herself, and then she read it again.

"Which grounds man? she asked.

"It does not say, ma'am. It says a pair of grey boots was left outside the stores. And the grounds man says they are his, and that he did not leave them there."

"Is he hurt?"

"The slip says he is not. It says he is asking for his boots back."

Ysolde put the slip on the desk beside the Duke's letter. And she looked at the two papers. And the hands on them were different. And both of them said that somebody had been moving around the camp at night and had not wanted to be seen. She wrote one line on a fresh card and put it face down in the fan.

"Send an answer," she said. "Tell them to give him the boots and to find out who took them. "And to send me the name in the morning."

That clerk wrote on his pad and went out. And the bellwire stopped clattering, and the office was quiet again. Ysolde put the unopened Liora letter back in the safe. And she put the brown file back in the drawer. And she turned out the desk lamp and sat in the dark with the fan of cards in her hand. And she did not deal them again until the clock struck one.

"Is the grounds man hurt?" she asked the clerk again.

"No, ma'am."

"Did he see who took them?"

"He says he did not."

"Did he say where the boots were found?"

"Outside the stores, on the step, with the laces tied."

Ysolde wrote the words down. A pair of boots left on a step with the laces tied was not a pair thrown away, and the man who tied them wanted the owner to find them quickly. He wanted him to know someone had been there. "Who is on duty at the camp gate?" she asked.

"The second watch, ma'am, and a corporal."

"Send the answer to the corporal, not to the clerk who wrote this."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Ask the corporal to send me the name of every man who left the camp between the eighth bell and the tenth."

That clerk wrote it all down, and his pencil was noisy on the paper, and Ysolde waited, and then she said, "Close the door on your way out, and do not tell anyone in the palace that I asked for a list of names."

The clerk looked at her and then at the door, and he said that clerks were paid to be quiet, and she said they were paid too little to be quiet for nothing. She put a coin on the desk. He took it and went out, closing the door carefully.

That office was quiet again, and the clock ticked, and she took the fan of cards and laid them out one by one on the blotter. The king was still there, the Duke was still there, and the high priestess was there twice. She put the second one back in the deck, because it was a card she did not like at midnight. She dealt again, and the king came up beside a card with no picture on it. She looked at the two of them for a while, then swept the cards into a pile and put the deck away.

The second envelope was a thing the king wanted, and the boots were a thing someone wanted found. The line in the doctor's file was a thing someone had wanted written, and all three things had come to her desk in one week. She wrote one line on a blank card and put it in the tray: "Someone is asking after the boy, and someone is answering."

At one o'clock she turned out the lamp and sat in the dark with her hands on the desk, and the city went on being quiet outside, and she did not open the letter. She wrote the hour at the top of a fresh page and put the page in the tray. It was a small thing, and it was a true thing, and it was the only thing she could do before morning.
