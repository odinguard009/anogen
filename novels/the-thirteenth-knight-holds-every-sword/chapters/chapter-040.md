# Chapter 40: The Box for the Dead

> "Every form has a box for the dead. I have never once been allowed to leave it empty." - Ysolde Lisle, office note

The palace offices at midnight had one lamp lit and a clock that clicked too loudly. Ysolde had worked there for twelve years and had never once turned the clock around. A clock that faced the wall was a clock nobody could trust.

A clerk put his head round the outer door at nine. "Can I put the lamp out, ma'am?"

"Not yet."

"There is only the one file left."

"It is my file." Ysolde kept her pen on the page. "I have not finished reading it."

"Is it bad, ma'am?"

"It is old." She turned a sheet. "Those are different things."

He went away and came back with a cup of tea that was too hot, and she let it stand on the corner of the desk while she read.

"Ma'am," he said, from the door, "there is a form from the Wall about a broken mirror."

"Put it in the middle stack."

"It says a cadet will pay. The name is Wren."

"Then it will say that." Ysolde did not look up. "I have paid it already. Write paid on the line and put the coin in the drawer, and do not write the boy's name on the coin."

"Yes, ma'am." He went away again, and she heard him move the clock an inch along the shelf and put it back where it had been.

Eden's PR 110 file lay on the desk with the paper clip removed and the pages in order. She had read it on the way back from the Wall and again in her office. The second reading told her what the first had not. The last sheet had been taken out before the copy was made. The index at the end had been left behind so that a clerk would think nothing was missing.

She drew a line under the index and wrote one word in the margin: EARLY.

The courier's envelope held the report from the garrison, two pages of clean handwriting and one line that had been crossed out and rewritten. It said that a Veil wearing a sergeant had been ended in the roll room. A mirror had been broken. No soldier had seen how. The space marked WITNESSES held a long dash, and the space marked DAMAGE held the price of a mirror. Ysolde wrote the price into the accounts book in the column for school damage. She put the coin in the petty cash drawer. The boy would try to pay for the mirror himself if she did not.

There was a form for the Veil, because everything that had once been a person got a form. There was a form for the mirror. There was a form for the Wall's silence. It had lasted twenty-six hours and been entered as thirty-one touches, because the office preferred the bigger number. Ysolde filled in the boxes in her small flat hand. When she came to the box marked DECEASED she stopped, as she always did, and read the line again.

Every form had a box for the dead. She had never once been allowed to leave it empty. In her first year she had left one blank by mistake and the office had returned it twice and then sent a clerk to stand at her desk until she filled it. She had learned the lesson and had never forgotten it, which was how a person learned most things in that office.

She wrote Storems, sergeant, garrison, and put the form in the middle stack. Then she wrote a second form for a man whose name had been on a different file for eleven years. That one went in the bottom drawer with the seal turned face down. The dead were easier to file when they were in separate drawers.

* * *

The king's second envelope was in the safe, in a slot behind a stack of old school papers. Ysolde had put it there in November. She had not opened it. She had taken it out twice to check that the seal was whole. It was grey with a ring pressed into the wax, and the ring had a flaw in one point of its crown. She had asked the king once what it was. "Something I should have read before I was king," he had said, and then asked her to put it away.

He had asked for it again on the Wall. Bring it when we are back.

She took it out and held it for a while with her thumb on the flaw. Then she put it back in the slot and shut the safe. She turned the key twice. Her mother had told her to turn a key twice when she was ten.

"No," she said, to the empty office, and then, because the word had been too small, "Not tonight." She put the key under the blotter where her hand would find it without looking.

A letter lay on the corner of the desk with a drop of wax on the back, sent by a father who wrote once a month and apologised in every sentence. Ysolde had read it twice. The Duke wrote that a vice-headmaster had written to him at Silverfell about a boy's absences. The man's name was Strent. The signature looked like an apology made by someone who had never apologised in his life. He had underlined the word odd four times and asked, in a sentence that changed direction halfway through, whether Ysolde had ever met the man.

She had. She had shaken his hand at a school function in the spring. He had kept his hands in the light and flinched when a lamp was moved. She had looked him up afterward, because looking people up was what she did. Strent, vice-headmaster, History of the Breach, twenty years at Hallowcrest, no complaints, no periods of absence, no record before the age of twenty-two. A man with no early life was not strange in her work. A man who apologised to a paper before he wrote on it was.

The letter had a smear of honey on the second page and a sentence about fish crossed out and written again. She read the crossed sentence twice because the Duke crossed things out when he was being careful, and he was never careful about ordinary matters.

She wrote back: Strent is a good teacher. Keep the letter. Ask nothing yet.

Then she wrote a second line, which she did not send: I do not like the way he writes to you about the boy.

She crossed it out and used the paper for the accounts instead.

* * *

The old file of Wendell was in the third drawer under a bundle of sealed school reports. The paper had gone soft at the edges and the ink had browned. The first line said that Wendell had been physician to the Duchess. He had vanished in the week Liora died. There were nine sheets. Eight were inventory and dates: a house on a street that had been pulled down, a list of instruments, three payments made in advance and never collected. The ninth was a single line: PHYSICIAN DISMISSED, NO FURTHER RECORD. The word dismissed had been written over another word that had been scratched so hard the paper had gone thin.

Ysolde read the line twice and put the file back exactly where it had been, with the sealed reports on top. She had pulled it out four times in eleven years and had never found a tenth sheet. There were no tenth sheets in any file in her office, which was a fact she had stopped trying to explain.

The safe held one more thing behind the grey envelope, wrapped in cloth and folded into a square. Liora's letter had her name on the outside in a hand that pressed hard into the paper. Ysolde lifted the cloth and looked at the writing for three breaths. Then she folded the cloth back over it and shut the safe. She did not open letters that had been left for someone else. That rule had kept her useful for twelve years, and she had no wish to become the kind of secretary who could not be trusted with a seal. The king had once asked her to open a sealed packet from a foreign court in front of him, and she had put it on the table and asked for a witness, and he had laughed and told her to put it away. He had not been angry. He had trusted her more after that, in the way a man trusts a locked door.

At the end of the desk lay a pair of wool socks left by a quarter-master that afternoon. They were thick and grey with the toe mended. She wrote a Form 13-B to go with them. The reason box said cold. The description box said extra warmth, two, not a gift. She folded the form into three and put it in the outgoing tray with the socks under it. The night courier came in quiet as always and waited with his cap in his hands.

"For the Wall," she said. "Say the line out loud at the gate. He forgets things that are said only on paper."

He took the tray and read the line from the back of the form: "He will say they are equipment. Write that down as equipment."

"Yes."

"Socks, ma'am? In the winter issue?"

"Equipment." She did not look up. "If he asks why, say the form is cold."

"Yes, ma'am." He went out with the tray held flat, and the door shut behind him without a sound at all.

The office clock clicked and the lamp burned low. The ink on the last form had dried into a small brown line where she had pressed too hard. She put the covers back on the stacks and turned the key in the bottom drawer, and she was reaching for the lamp when the bellwire on the wall gave a sound like a finger tapping a cup. Ysolde had worked there long enough to know that the Wall did not tap for nothing. The night clerk appeared in the door with his coat over his arm. "Shall I fetch the duty officer, ma'am?"

"Not yet." She took down the message pad. "Write what I say."

He came to the desk with a pencil and stood waiting while the clicks came across the wire, one word for each pause.

She did not know whether to send the socks first. The clicks went on until the pad held one line. She read it back to the clerk and he wrote it again in his own hand, and both copies said the same thing.

CAMP STORES REPORT A MISSING GROUNDSKEEPER'S BOOTS.
