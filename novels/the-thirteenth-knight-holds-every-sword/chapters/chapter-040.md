# Chapter 40: The Box for the Dead

> "Every form has a box for the dead. I have never once been allowed to leave it empty." - Ysolde Lisle, office note

At midnight the palace office had one lamp lit and a clock that clicked too loudly. Ysolde had worked there for twelve years. She was the king's private secretary, and she kept the files that nobody else was allowed to read. Tonight she stayed because there was a pile of paper on her desk, and she wanted each sheet to have a place before she slept.

A clerk put his head round the door at half past eleven. "May I put out the second lamp, ma'am?"

"Not yet."

"Only one file is left."

"It is the one I am reading." She kept her pen on the page. "Go home. I will lock the door."

He went. The clock went on clicking.

The file on her desk was Eden's PR 110 file. She had read it twice. The first time she looked for what it said. The second time she looked for what it did not say. That was the better way to read a file, and it told her something the first reading had not.

The last page was missing. The list of pages was still there at the end, and it named a witness statement, a note about the dead man's family, and a note about the result. Someone had taken those pages out before the file was copied. They had left the list behind on purpose, so that a clerk would think the file was complete. Ysolde put a finger on the list. The paper there was newer than the rest. The numbers on the pages ran from one to forty, then jumped to forty-two. Page forty-one was gone.

The stamp on the last page was a day older than the stamp on the first. That meant the file had been closed before the events in it were finished. Ysolde laid a ruler under the stamp and saw that it sat a little too low. Someone had stamped it by hand, in a hurry, with the seal turned the wrong way.

"Ma'am," the clerk said from the door. He had come back with a cup of tea that was too hot. "There is a form from the Wall about a broken mirror. It says a cadet will pay. The name is Wren."

"Then it says what is true." She did not look up. "Put it in the middle stack. I will pay it myself."

"Ma'am, the form says the cadet will pay."

"I know what it says. Write paid on the line. Do not write his name on the coin." She waited until he put the cup on the corner of the desk and went away.

Then, in the margin, she wrote one word in pencil in the margin: EARLY. A pencil mark could be rubbed out. A pen mark would become a fact, and she did not yet know whose fact it would be.

Next came the report from the Wall, two pages of clean handwriting from the garrison. It said a Veil had been wearing a sergeant, and it had been ended in the roll room. One mirror had been broken. No soldier had seen how. The line for witnesses was a long dash. The line for damage held the price of a mirror.

She entered the price in the school damage book. She put the coin in the drawer. Eden would try to pay for the mirror out of his own pocket, and she did not want him to.

There was a form for the Veil, because everything that had once been a person got a form. There was a form for the mirror. When she reached the box marked DECEASED, she stopped, as she always did.

Every form had a box for the dead. She had never been allowed to leave it empty. In her first year she had left one blank by mistake. The office had returned it twice and then sent a clerk to stand beside her desk until she filled it in. She had learned the lesson. She wrote *Storems, sergeant, garrison* in the box, and put the form in the middle stack.

* * *

At the back of the safe lay a grey envelope with a ring pressed into the wax. It was the king's second envelope. He had given it to her in November and said, "Keep it. I should have read it before I was king." She had not opened it. The ring had a small flaw in one point of its crown, and she knew the flaw as well as she knew her own handwriting.

On the Wall, the king had said another thing. *Bring it when we are back.*

Out it came, and she held it for a while with her thumb on the flaw. Then she put it back in the slot and shut the safe. She turned the key twice, as her mother had taught her.

"No," she said to the empty office. Then, because the word was too small, "Not tonight."

The honest reason was this: Ysolde was afraid of what he would do with it, and what it would do to him. She was not afraid for herself. That was the odd part. She put the key under the blotter, where her hand would find it without looking.

Next to the envelope lay another one, wrapped in cloth and folded into a square. It was Liora's letter. Ysolde had kept it since the week Liora died. The name on the outside was in a hand that pressed hard into the paper. It was not addressed to Ysolde. She lifted the cloth, looked at the writing for three breaths, and folded the cloth back over it.

Letters meant for someone else stayed shut. That rule had kept her useful for twelve years. Once the king had asked her to open a sealed packet from a foreign court in front of him. She had put it on the table and asked for a witness. He had laughed, and had trusted her more afterward, the way a man trusts a locked door.

The safe door clicked shut.

* * *

A third letter lay on the corner of the desk. It was from the Duke, who wrote once a month and apologised in every sentence. This one was longer. The Duke wrote that the vice-headmaster of the school had sent him a letter about a boy's absences. The man's name was Halvard Strent. The Duke had underlined one word, *odd*, four times. He wanted to know whether Ysolde had ever met the man.

She had. She had shaken Strent's hand at a school event in the spring. He had kept his hands in the light, and he had flinched when someone moved a lamp. Later she had looked him up, because looking people up was what she did. His record said: Strent, vice-headmaster, History of the Breach, twenty years at the school, no complaints, no absences. There was nothing about his life before the age of twenty-two.

She wanted to know why a man like that would write to a duke about a boy's absences. A vice-headmaster had no business in the Duke's letter box. The boy was Eden, and Eden's absences were already on a school form. The only reason to send the Duke a second copy was to make him look. She wrote that thought down in her head and did not give it a name.

A man with no early life was not strange in her work. A man who flinched at a moving lamp was.

Her answer was short: *Strent teaches well. Keep his letter. Ask nothing yet.*

On a second line she wrote: *I do not like how he writes to you about the boy.* She did not send that line. She crossed it out and used the paper for her accounts.

* * *

The third drawer held the old file of Wendell. He had been the physician to the late Duchess. He had left the palace in the week that Liora died, and nobody had seen him since. The file had nine sheets. Eight were lists: the street where he lived, a list of his instruments, three payments made in advance that he never collected. The ninth sheet had one line: *PHYSICIAN DISMISSED, NO FURTHER RECORD.* The word *dismissed* had been written over another word that someone had scratched out so hard that the paper had gone thin.

In the morning she would send for the stamp clerk and ask, as if she were only curious, who had held the key to the closing seal in the week of the file. She would not write the question down.

Ysolde held the sheet to the lamp. She could not read what was under it. She had taken the file out four times in eleven years and never found a tenth sheet. Not here and not in any other file in this office. She no longer tried to explain that.

She was not sure it was an accident. A missing page in Eden's file, a scratched word in Wendell's file. Someone had been cutting things out of the king's records for a long time. She wrote the two cases on one card and put the card in her pocket, not in the files.

She made herself a list for the morning on the back of the card. First, the stamp clerk and the key to the closing seal. Second, the registry in the lower city, to ask who had signed Strent's first paper at the age of twenty-two. Third, a quiet word with the Duke, which she would write as if it were about fish. The list was short. Each line had a name and a place, and none of the lines had a reason written beside it. A reason on paper was a thing that could be read by the wrong eyes.

Back went Wendell under the sealed school reports, exactly where he had been.

* * *

At the end of the desk a pair of thick grey wool socks lay under a lamp. A quartermaster had left them that afternoon. The toes had been mended. Ysolde filled in a Form 13-B to go with them.

The reason box said *cold*. The description box said *extra warmth, two pairs, not a gift*. She folded the form in three and put it in the outgoing tray. The night courier came in without a sound and waited with his cap in his hands.

"For the Wall," she said. "Say it aloud at the gate. He forgets things that are only on paper."

The courier read the form. "Socks, ma'am?"

"Equipment." She did not look up. "If he asks why, tell him the form says it is cold."

"May I ask something, ma'am?"

"You may ask."

"Why does he get socks and the other cadets do not?"

"Because the others have mothers who send them." She capped the ink. "He has the school stores and this office. If I do not send them, nobody will, and in a week he will be cold and say nothing about it."

The courier turned his cap in his hands. "He does not complain, ma'am."

"No. That is why someone has to count for him."


"Yes, ma'am." He took the tray and went out with it held flat.

She sat for a moment and listened to the clock. Then the wire on the wall gave a small sound, like a finger tapping a cup. It was the bellwire from the Wall. It did not tap for nothing.

The night clerk came to the door with his coat over his arm. "Shall I fetch the duty officer?"

"Not yet." She took down the message pad. "Write what I say."

He stood by the desk with his pencil. The clicks came across the wire, one word for each pause, and she spoke them aloud for him. When it stopped, the pad held one line. She read it back, and he wrote it again in his own hand. Both copies said the same.
