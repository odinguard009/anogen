# Chapter 42: Present

The roll call bell rang at the fifth bell and found Eden asleep in a tent that was not his. He had been in his own bunk at the fourth bell, and after that the memory had a gap in it the size of an hour, which was Gorrath's doing.

"Wren," the prefect called, at the tent with the green stripe.

"Present," said a voice from the back of the tent.

"Wren," the prefect called again, because he had been awake long enough to notice that the tent held two sleeping soldiers and no Eden.

"Present," said a voice from inside a boot.

"Wren," the prefect called a third time, from the end of the line.

"Present," said a voice from inside the empty kettle.

The prefect put his pencil behind his ear and looked into the kettle, which was empty and had a dent in its side. He was a first-year with a new clipboard and a face that wanted very much to be trusted. "Is that a trick?"

"It is a kettle," Rowan said, from the next tent.

"Yes, but is it a trick?"

"Only if someone is hiding in it." Rowan went back into his tent, and the first-year wrote something down with a great deal of pressure.

The prefect wrote something else down and moved on. By the time he reached the sixth tent he had heard "present" from thirteen different directions, and the camp had begun to make the sound men make when they are trying not to laugh at an officer.

Gorrath was good at the voices but he was a terrible liar. Eden had told him three times to keep the answers short, and Gorrath had said "Yes, sir" in a deep voice every time, which was the kind of answer that got a giant in trouble. His real shape sat somewhere in the dark under the wall with a tower shield across his knees, and his voice came out of thirteen tents with a different tired note in each one. His Dragon complained in Eden's head that the whole trick was childish. "Thirteen voices to say one word. It is the sort of thing a god does when it has nothing to do."

"It is a roll call," Eden said, in his head.

"I have called rolls that ended empires. You do it with a boy hiding in a kettle."

Gorrath heard none of this. He was busy being a log, and being a log was harder than it looked when a girl with a pencil stood three feet away and did not blink. The Dragon was ignored.

Lysette came up the path with her book open and her red band pinned straight, and the prefect stopped writing.

"Roll," she said.

"Nine present, miss," the prefect said. "One asleep in a boot."

"I can see the boot." Lysette walked down the line of tents with her pencil moving. She stopped at the third tent and listened. "That voice is too calm."

"It is a calm camp, miss."

"It is a cold camp. Calm is different." She went into the tent and came out two seconds later holding a pillow. Under the blanket on the bed lay a soldier with his boots on and his face turned to the wall. "That is not Wren."

"Present," said a voice from the woodpile.

She stood at the woodpile and listened to the "present" coming from a split log. "That one sounds like a giant."

"It is a cold morning, miss. Voices carry."

"Voices do not carry out of wood." She put two fingers on the log and waited. Gorrath's voice came from the far side of the camp, answering a roll call in a tent where nobody had asked one. Lysette's pencil stopped. "There are thirteen of you," she said. "I have counted. You have answered from thirteen places, and this log is the twelfth."

There was a silence in which a man dropped a bucket. Then a deep voice said, "Present," from inside the log, which was the wrong thing to say and Gorrath knew it as soon as he had said it.

Lysette shut her book. "I am too tired for this," she said, and walked away with the pillow still in her hand.

The log sat still for a long minute after she had gone. Then the bark on one side peeled away like a loose door and Gorrath unfolded out of the wood with twigs in his beard and his shield still on his arm. He looked at the pillow Lysette had dropped on the path, picked it up, and brushed the snow off it as if it were a wounded bird.

"She counted me," he said. "Twelve. I was the twelfth."

"You were the thirteenth," Eden said. "You were still wrong about which one."

"I will practise." Gorrath put the pillow on the woodpile where it could be found. "I am sorry about the boot."

Eden woke in the supply tent with the scarf over his face and snow on his coat, and he had no memory of how he had got there. Felix sat beside him with a bun in his hand.

"You were in the roll call," Felix said.

"No, I was not."

"You were. Thirteen of you were." Felix broke the bun in two. "I heard you say present from the laundry line. You do not have a laundry line."

"Gorrath, and he cannot lie."

"I know it was Gorrath. I am telling you what the form will say." Felix gave him the larger half. "Eat. Lysette has written you down as 'present, location uncertain' which is worse than absent and better than dead." He looked at the snow on Eden's coat. "Where did you sleep?"

"Here."

"That is not true either." Felix went back to his book. "I am not writing it down. Do not ask me why."

He turned a page and did not read it. A little later he said, to the book, "If a man tells you the same lie every night, it stops being a lie and starts being his address." Then he held out the other half of the bun again, because Eden had not yet eaten the first.

By the second bell the camp had turned the roll call into a game. Seraphine declared a snow-fort war on Lance One and built a wall of packed snow around the north table with her own hands, and Valerius's men built a taller one with a bank of ice and a flag made from a towel. Felix was pulled in because he was standing near the north table with a roll in his hand, and he was given the job of counting ammunition, which in this war meant snowballs. He counted them into a sack and counted them out again and wrote the number on his book, and then Seraphine took six for a surprise attack and he had to begin again. The wall on the north side grew to the height of a man's waist and then to his chest, and two soldiers from Lance Three joined because they had nothing better to do on a day off, and Valerius sent an officer over to say that a snow fort built on the council path was a matter for the council, which made the war twice as good.

"One hundred," he said, after a while.

"That is not how many we have," Seraphine said.

"It is how many I have counted. I am being accurate."

"Count again."

Felix counted again and got a different number because Rowan had eaten two snowballs by mistake, thinking they were rolls. Rowan said the snow tasted like nothing and he had been hungry. Clara told him not to eat fortifications, and he said he had not known they were fortifications, and she said that was the trouble with him.

Seraphine came past Eden with a card and wrote on it. "One hundred and one."

"That was not a joke," Felix said, from behind the snow wall.

"You coughed."

"I coughed because Rowan threw snow at my face."

"One hundred and one is for the cough." She wrote it down with a pencil that had no point and pressed so hard the card bent. "I will tell you the joke at the end of term."

"I have heard that before."

"Then you will hear it twice."

By the third bell Rowan and Clara had stopped fighting the snow war and were sitting on the wall with a single blanket over both their knees because the wind had come round the north side of the camp. Rowan had his spear between his legs and was talking without stopping, and Clara had her hands tucked under the blanket and was not looking at anyone. Three soldiers walked past and stared, and Rowan looked down at the blanket and then up at the soldiers.

"What?" he said. "It is cold."

"It is very cold," Clara said, in a flat voice that suggested he should stop talking.

"That is what I said."

"That is not what you said. You said 'what'."

"Because I did not understand the looking."

"They were looking at the blanket."

"I know they were looking at the blanket." Rowan pulled it higher over both their knees. "I do not understand why that is interesting. It is a blanket. It is cold."

Clara put her hand on the blanket and left it there. "It is a blanket," she said. Then she looked at the wall and did not say anything else for a while, and Rowan, who could not bear a silence when he was sitting next to someone, filled it with a story about a snow fort he had built when he was nine that had melted in an hour.

The fort in the story had had a door, a chimney made of a rolled-up sock, and a sign that said KEEP OUT. The neighbour's dog had come in anyway, through the wall, and the whole thing had fallen on both of them. Clara listened to every word of it and did not move her hand off the blanket.

Rowan thought about the looking for another minute and then went back to talking, and the three soldiers went away with the expressions of people who had seen something they would tell their friends about at dinner. Eden sat on the step of the stores with the scarf over his mouth and watched Okoru walk past with his stick, and Okoru did not stop and did not chew anything, which meant he had noticed the blanket as well.

At the fourth bell the snow war ended because a prefect ordered everyone inside. Felix came out from behind the north wall with snow up to his elbows and a card in his hand that now had a second number written on it, and he gave the card to Seraphine without looking at her. She read the card and said, "One hundred and one," and he said, "That was the snow falling off the wall," and she said that falling snow was not a joke but that the cough had been one and the count would stay where it was. Felix went into the hut with his book over his head and did not answer.

The camp went quiet in the way it did when the cold finally got into everything. Lysette came down the path from the wall with her book held shut and stopped in front of Eden. She did not look at him, which was how she began the questions she had been saving.

"Where do you actually go at night, every night?"

"The roof," Eden said.

There was no roof at the Wall and she knew it as well as he did. There was a battlement, a parapet and a drop of eighty feet into the grey country, and no garden and no apple tree and no stone bench with a bird nest in the telescope. Lysette stood with the book under her arm for three breaths, and then she wrote something on the page and walked away without saying whether she believed him.
