# Chapter 42: Present

At the fifth bell, the camp held roll call. Every soldier stood outside his tent, and a prefect with a clipboard walked down the line and called each name. When a name was called, the person said "Present."

Eden was not there. He had come up from the tunnel at dawn with grey mud to the elbows, and he was asleep in the stores hut with his coat over his face. He had asked Gorrath, the third member of the Thirteenth Vigil, to cover for him. Gorrath was a huge man in black plate who could fill a street with his voice, so that it answered from many places at once. This morning his job was easy. He only had to say "Present" when the prefect said "Wren."

The prefect was a first-year with a new clipboard and a face that very much wanted to be trusted. He stopped at the tent with the green stripe.

"Wren," he called.

"Present," said a voice from the back of the tent.

The prefect moved down the line. He called "Wren" again at the next tent, because the roll had said Wren was in two places, and he wanted to be sure.

"Present," said a voice from inside a boot.

The prefect looked at the boot. It was empty. "Wren," he said, in a smaller voice, at the third tent.

"Present," said a voice from the kettle.

He looked in the kettle. It was empty too, and it had a dent in its side. "Is this a trick?"

"It is a kettle," Rowan said from the next tent.

"Yes, but is it a trick?"

"Only if someone is hiding in it." Rowan went back into his tent, and the prefect wrote something down with great force.

Gorrath was too good at voices and too bad at lying. Eden had told him to answer once. Gorrath had answered nine times, from nine places, in nine tired voices. By the time the prefect reached the sixth tent, the whole camp was making the sound people make when they try not to laugh at an officer.

* * *

Lysette came up the path with her book open and her red band pinned straight. She was the vice-chair of the Discipline Committee, and the roll call was hers to check.

She had one rule for roll call, and she said it aloud to the prefect so that he would remember it. "A name is only present if you can see the person. A voice is not enough. Next time, look."

"Yes, miss. I am sorry, miss."

"Report," she said to the prefect.

"Nine present, miss," the prefect said. "One of them asleep in a boot."

"I can see the boot." She walked along the tents with her pencil moving. At the third tent she stopped. "That voice is too calm."

"It is a calm camp, miss."

"It is a cold camp. Calm is different." She went into the tent and came out holding a pillow. "There is no Wren in there. There is a pillow under a blanket."

"Present," said a voice from the woodpile.

Lysette turned. The voice had come from a stack of split logs. She walked over and listened. "That one sounds big."

"It is a cold morning, miss. Voices carry."

"Voices do not carry out of wood." She put two fingers on a log and waited. From the far side of the camp another voice answered a roll call in a tent where nobody had called one. Her pencil stopped. "I have counted," she said. "Wren has answered from nine places. One of them is a log. There are only two ways that can happen. Someone is playing a trick on the prefect, or I am more tired than I think."

She stood very still for a moment. She had slept four hours. She had written eleven reports before dawn. She looked at the pillow in her hand, then at the log, then at the grey sky.

"I am too tired for this," she said. She laid the pillow on the woodpile and walked away without writing a line.

The log stayed still a while. Then its bark peeled open like a door, and Gorrath unfolded out of it, with twigs in his beard and the shield still on his arm. He looked at the pillow and brushed the snow off it as if it were a hurt bird.

"She counted nine," he said. "I wanted thirteen. It is a more lucky number."

"It was nine, and you were the log," Eden said. He had come out of the stores with the scarf over his face and sleep in his eyes. "Thank you."

"I am sorry about the boot."

"It was a good boot."

Gorrath looked pleased. He set the pillow on the woodpile where someone would find it.

"Next time, answer once," Eden said. "One voice, one place. A tired girl counts better than a fresh one."

"I know. I know." Gorrath rubbed the back of his neck, and a twig fell out. "I get nervous. When a person stands near me and looks for me, my voice wants to go everywhere. It is like sneezing."

"Then do not stand near the woodpile."

"There is nowhere else to stand," Gorrath said sadly. "Everything in this camp is a pile of something."


* * *

By the second bell the roll call had turned into a game. Seraphine, the princess, was spending her winter at the Wall like everyone else, and she was bored. She had declared a snow-fort war against Valerius's Lance. She had built a wall of packed snow around the north table with her own hands, and Valerius's soldiers were building a taller one with a flag made from a towel.

Felix was pulled in, because he was standing near the north table with a roll in his hand. He was given the job of counting snowballs. He counted them into a sack, and Seraphine took six for a surprise attack, and he had to begin again. Two soldiers from another Lance joined them for no better reason than that they had a free morning.

"One hundred," Felix said at last.

"That is not how many we have," Seraphine said.

"It is how many I counted. I am being accurate."

"Count again."

He counted again and got a different number, because Rowan had eaten two snowballs by mistake, thinking they were rolls. Rowan said the snow tasted of nothing and he had been hungry. Clara told him not to eat the walls. He said he had not known they were walls, and she said that was the trouble with him.

Seraphine came past Eden with a card and a pencil. On the card she kept a number, and for four years that number had been the same kind of count. It was the number of jokes she had told Felix to make him laugh. He had never laughed. This morning she wrote *101* and showed Eden.

"Did he laugh?"

"He coughed," Seraphine said. "I said something about a goose and a bridge. He coughed. That is almost a laugh."

"It is a cough," Felix said, from behind the wall.

"It was nearly a laugh. It counts." She pressed so hard on the card that the pencil bent. "I will tell you the whole joke at the end of term."

"You said that last year."

"Then you will hear it twice."

Felix turned a page in his book and did not read it. The corner of his mouth moved once. Eden noticed. He did not say so.

Seraphine stopped by the stores step on her way back to the fort. She was Eden's twin, and she was the only person at the Wall who spoke to him like a sister. She sat beside him, brushed snow off her knee, and spoke without looking.

"You look terrible."

"I slept."

"You slept in a hut with lamp oil. Your hair smells of it." She picked a flake of dry mud off his sleeve. "Felix asked me to watch you. He will not say why. He only said, 'See if he eats.'"

"I eat."

"You eat when someone puts it in your hand." She flicked the mud away. "I told him I would. I am telling you so that you do not think I am spying."

"You are spying."

"Yes, but I told you first. That makes it a kindness." She stood and shook out her coat. "If you are going to be hurt, tell someone before. I would prefer it."

He did not answer. She did not seem to expect it. She went back to her fort, and he watched her walk away with her chin high, as she always did when she had said more than she meant.

* * *

By the third bell the wind had come round the north side. Rowan and Clara sat on the low wall with one blanket over both their knees. Rowan had his spear between his legs and was talking without stopping. Clara had her hands under the blanket and was not looking at anyone.

Three soldiers walked past and stared. Rowan looked down at the blanket and then up at them.

"What?" he said. "It is cold."

"It is very cold," Clara said in a flat voice that meant he should stop talking.

"That is what I said."

"You said, 'What.' That is not the same." She did not look up. "They are looking at the blanket."

"I know they are looking at the blanket." Rowan pulled it higher over both their knees. "I do not understand why that is interesting. It is a blanket. It is cold."

Clara put her hand on the blanket and left it there. She was smiling, and she hid it by looking at the wall. Rowan, who could not stand a silence when he sat beside someone, filled it with a story about a snow fort he had built at nine, with a door and a sock for a chimney and a sign that said KEEP OUT. A neighbour's dog had come through the wall anyway, and the whole fort had fallen on both of them.

Clara listened to every word. She did not move her hand.

The three soldiers walked off with the faces of people who would tell their friends at dinner. Eden sat on the stores step with the scarf over his mouth and watched them go. Rowan, he thought, did not know what he had done. Clara knew exactly.

* * *

At the fourth bell the snow war ended, because a prefect ordered everyone inside. The camp went quiet in the way it did when the cold got into everything.

Lysette came down the path from the wall, with her book shut and her pencil tucked behind her ear. She stopped in front of Eden. She did not look at him, which was how she began the questions she had been saving.

"I have a real question," she said. "I know you were not in the tent this morning. I do not know where you were. You are gone every night, and you come back grey. Where do you actually go?"

Eden looked at her. The true answer was a tunnel, a crack in a floor, and a warm stone. He had told nobody except Vesk.

"The roof," he said.

There was no roof at the Wall. There was a battlement, a parapet, and a drop of eighty feet into the grey country. There was no garden, no apple tree, and no stone bench with a bird's nest in a broken telescope. Lysette knew it as well as he did.

He did not know why he had chosen that word. The roof was the one place he had told her, in the autumn, where he went to sleep. Saying it now was the same as saying nothing, and she knew that too. It told her he would not lie well, and that he would not tell the truth.

She stood with the book under her arm for three breaths. Then she wrote one line on the page, and walked away without saying whether she believed him.
