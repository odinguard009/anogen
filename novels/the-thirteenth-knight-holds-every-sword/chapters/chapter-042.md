# Chapter 42: Thirteen Voices

The roll call bell rang at six. And Gorrath stood in the shadow of the tents with his shield on his arm and his one hand at his side. His voice came out of the dark beside a tent that held eleven sleeping men.

"Present," said the voice.

A older student at the table wrote a mark and looked up. Because the voice had come from a tent where nobody had moved. He called the next name. Another voice answered from a different shadow. Tired and calm, and then a third voice answered high and quick from behind the stores, and a fourth from the water barrel, and a fifth from the knot of men at the gate.

Lysette stood at the end of the table with her round sheet in her hand. She counted the voices, and she wrote the number thirteen in the corner of the sheet, and then she wrote a second number under it, which was the number of students who had actually answered in her own voice.

"Who is that? the older student asked.

"Nobody," Lysette said.

"That is not nobody. That is thirteen somebodies."

"It is one somebody with a talent. She shut her book. "Write it down as a disturbance and move on."

Gorrath answered for the next eleven names in eleven different voices. Not one of them was the same. Two of them argued with each other about who was present. The older student wrote both marks because he did not know what else to do. A voice from the lamp post said that it had been present since the fifth bell and had the cramp in its leg to prove itA voice from the coal heap said that the cramp was its own and had been there for years. The men in the tents began to wake up and listen because a roll call that argued with itself was better than a roll call in the cold.

The older student put his pen down and asked the next name. The voice that answered came from the water barrel. It had a different accent from the voice before it. The older student said the name again because he thought he had misheard. The second voice answered again, lower this time, and the men in the tents began to put their boots on in the dark, because a roll call that went on too long meant someone was in trouble, and not one man wanted to be the one who was not dressed.

Lysette walked along the row of tents with her pencil out. She stopped at the tent where the tired voice had come from and lifted the flap. Four men slept inside, and none of them had spoken, and the voice came again from behind her, tired and calm, answering a name she had not called yet.

"Do not answer that one," she said, and the voice stopped.

The voice that stopped belonged to a shadow on the ground. The shadow moved when Gorrath moved. And Lysette looked at the shadow and then at the ground where it lay. She understood enough to put her pencil in her pocket and walk away. She had written three pages of rules about attendance in her life. None of them had a line for a man who could answer from thirteen places at once. She decided at the foot of the stair that she was too tired to write a fourth page before breakfast.

Gorrath came out of the shadow by the stores and walked past her in the grey light. He said in his own voice. Which was low and tired and honest, "You did not see me."

"I saw thirteen of you," Lysette said. "I am going to write down that I saw none."

"That is a kind thing to do."

"It is an accurate thing to do. I am too tired to be kind."

At breakfast Rowan asked Felix whether he had heard the voices. And Felix said that he had heard thirteen, and Rowan asked how he had counted them. Felix said that Gorrath had once told him his voices had a rhythm. That the high one always came third. Clara asked whether Gorrath had been in the tent. And Felix said that he had not. That this was the point of it. And Rowan said he would count them next time. And Felix said there would be a next time.

Seraphine built the snow fort at noon. By midday it had walls as tall as a man and a gate that faced the east. She put Felix on the wall with a snowball in his hand and told him he was the captain of the guard. And Felix said that he was not. She said that he was now. He said that nobody had appointed him. She said that she had. That she was a princess and could appoint a captain of anything.

Rowan and Clara joined the war on the west side. And Rowan threw a snowball that went over the wall and hit a mule. The mule bit him on the sleeve. And Clara laughed for the first time that week. And Rowan looked at her laughing and then at his sleeve and said that he had always wanted to be bitten by a mule. That it was a thing a person could only do once with dignity. She laughed again, and the men working at the parapet stopped to watch because a snow-fort war in January was a rare thing and a mule bite was rarer.

Felix stayed on the wall of the fort with his snowball and his serious face. Joke number one hundred and one was thrown at him from the east gate by Seraphine. Who shouted that he had finally laughed. And Felix said that he had not. Then he coughed because he had breathed in snow. She wrote the joke down as a success. He said that a cough was not a laugh. She said that a cough from a man who never laughed was better than nothing. That she would take it.

Clara and Rowan sat together on the step of the fort after the war ended. He put a blanket over both of them because the wind had come up. She did not move away. He did not notice that the men at the parapet were looking. When Felix came past with his bow case he stopped and stared. Then he went on, because staring at a friend who did not know he was being stared at was a thing a person did only once.

Okoru came past the fort and told them to keep the snow out of the gate road. And Seraphine said that the fort was a military exercise. Okoru said that a fort which blocked the road was a fort that would be moved by a mule. She moved it. Rowan helped her, and Clara helped Rowan, and Felix stood on the wall and said that a captain did not move a fort, and that a captain gave orders from the wall, and nobody listened to him.

Eden sat at the edge of the fort with his back against a snow wall and his hands in his pockets. And Lysette came around the wall with her book and stood in front of him. She did not sit down.

"Where do you actually go at night? she asked.

Eden looked at the snow wall. Then at the sky, and then at her, and he said, "The roof."

Lysette shut her book and looked at him for a while. Then she looked up at the grey sky above the camp. Then back at him.

"There is no roof," she said.

"I know."

She wrote a line in her book. The pencil made a hard mark on the page. Then she walked away with her head down. And Eden stayed where he was with his back against the snow wall. He did not try to explain it. Because there was no explanation that was both true and short.

That night Lysette sat at the fire with her book shut and her hands around a cup. And Rowan sat down beside her and asked whether the voices had been real. She said that they had been real and that she had written them down as a disturbance. He asked what a disturbance was. She said it was a thing that happened when a person did not want to write the truth. Rowan thought about that and then asked whether it was about EdenShe said that she had not said that. He said that she did not have to. She told him to go and sit with ClaraHe went, and he took his blanket with him, and she watched him go and did not write anything down.

Felix came to sit where Rowan had been. And he put a bun on the ground between them and pushed it toward Eden with one finger.

"Eaten today?"

"Yes."

"Lying again."

"I ate half a bun."

"That is not a day. Felix looked at the fire and then at the snow fort. Which had a wall missing on the north side where the mule had gone through it. "She asked you where you go at night."

"Yes."

"What did you say?"

"The roof."

"There is no roof here. Felix said it plainly, and he did not wait for an answer, and then he said, "I know there is no roof, and I know you know there is no roof, and I am not going to ask you again tonight, because you will give me another true answer that tells me nothing, and I am tired of those. He picked up the bun and handed it to Eden. "Eat the other half in the morning. I will ask again then."

Eden took the bun and put it in his coat. And he sat with his hands on his knees while the fire went down. And the camp made its ordinary night sounds around him. And somewhere behind the stores a voice said "present" in a tired and calm way. And no one at the fire turned their head. Because one more voice at a camp roll call was not worth getting up for.

In the morning Felix came to the fort with a cup of tea and a question. And he stood on the wall and looked down at Eden. And he said that he had asked in the night and that he would ask again. And that this was the last time he would ask in that way.

"Where do you go? he asked.

"Under the Wall," Eden said.

Felix held the cup with both hands and looked at the snow wall and then at the camp. And he said that he had thought as much. And that a man who slept in a camp under the eyes of a vice-chair and went missing at night had only one direction left to go. And that the under was the part that worried him. Eden said nothing, and Felix drank the tea and made a face because it was cold, and he said that this was the first honest answer he had had in a month and he had not liked it.

Lysette came past with her book. And she stopped when she saw the two of them. And Felix looked at her and said that he had asked the question again and that he had got an answer this time. And she said that she was glad. And that she did not want to hear it, and she walked on. Felix watched her go and then looked at Eden. And his face had the calm on it that Eden had learned to read. Which meant he had decided something and would not say what it was until it was done.

By noon the snow fort had a tower on the east side and a hole in the north wall where the mule had come through. And Seraphine stood on the tower and said that the fort was a perfect defence and that the hole was a gate. And Felix said that a fort with a gate in the wrong wall was a fort with two enemies. And she said that was why she had appointed him captain. And he said that a captain who was never listened to was a captain in name only, and she wrote that down and said it was joke one hundred and two.
