# Chapter 43: The Shadow and the Breath

The storm came up from the grey country in the afternoon and turned the snow on the battlement into a sheet that moved sideways. The watch was doubled, and Lance Nine drew the stretch by the inner stair where the wall made a corner and the wind struck from two sides at once. The sergeant gave them an extra bucket of coal for the brazier and a rule about keeping their hoods up, and he wrote the rule on his board where everyone could see it because the wind would take a spoken one. Rowan stood at the corner with his spear planted and his hood up, and he had to turn his whole body to look at anything, which he did often, because corner-watch made him feel as if he were missing the rest of the war.

Clara arrived with her hands wrapped in cloth and a face that had gone still with cold. A conscript from the third stretch had come off the wall with two fingers white and hard, and she had worked on them for twenty minutes in the warm hut while the man watched her hands and did not ask whether they would stay.

"They will stay," she said, when Eden came to replace the bandage roll on the stores shelf with the storm already pushing snow against the hut door. "They will hurt for a week and they will stay."

"You are shaking."

"I am warm." Clara put the roll back without looking at it. "He had been on the parapet with no gloves because he thought it was a short watch. It is never a short watch."

"How much?"

"An hour of feeling in my own hands. Sometimes a day." Clara flexed her fingers once and stopped. "It is not a great price. I am telling you so you will not make a form about it."

"Can you do that without cost?"

"Everything has a cost." She held out her hands and looked at them, and the ends of her fingers were pale where the heat had gone out of them into the conscript's. "Mine comes back by morning. It did not come back by morning last time, and I did not tell anyone."

Eden wrote the hour on the stores form and said nothing about the last time. He had learned that Clara gave facts away like rolls and that asking for more made her put them back in the bag.

Rowan came up the stair with his spear over his shoulder and his scarf frozen to his chin. He had been to see the conscript twice in ten minutes and each time had asked the same three questions, which was how he worried when he did not know what else to do.

"Is he warm? Will they stay? Does he need anything?"

"He has said no twice," Clara said.

"I will ask a third time."

"He will say no a third time."

"Then I will know." Rowan set his spear against the wall and rubbed his hands together. "I hate not knowing whether someone needs something."

"You cannot fix that by asking."

"I know." He turned back to the merlon. "I will ask anyway."

"Then ask it quietly." She pulled her sleeves down. "He is embarrassed."

"I know." Rowan looked at the wall, then at the snow, then at the hut door. "I was embarrassed once. I fell over in front of all of you in the dining hall and Brandmeier asked if I was quite finished."

"You stood up and bowed to the floor," Clara said. "That was funny."

"I know it was funny." Rowan went to the parapet and began to clear snow from a merlon with his glove, which was a job nobody had given him.

Lysette came up the wall during the watch with her book under her arm. She spoke to Rowan first, about the snow and the conscript's fingers, and asked him a question about the drill on Tuesday, and then she went along the line and stopped beside Eden. She had been watching Rowan less since the fire and the conversation about the blanket, and she had been watching Eden more, and both things were written in the tilt of her head.

"Your scarf," she said. "It is frozen all the way through, and you are still wearing it."

"I am warm."

"You said that when I told you your hands were white." She touched the scarf with two fingers and took them away. "That is the same sentence. You use it when you do not want a question."

"Then I will use another one."

"Please do." She waited, but he had no other one, and she wrote something on the page. "I am not writing that down. I am writing that it is cold."

The storm grew during the night. The snow stopped falling and began to move in long flat sheets that struck the parapet and went over it. The men on the wall kept their heads down and their hands on the stone. The brazier at the corner burned with a short blue flame and gave no warmth past a man's elbow, and the coal the sergeant had brought was wet at the bottom of the bucket. Somewhere along the stretch a soldier sang two lines of a song and stopped because the wind took the words away, and nobody asked him to finish. Eden took the middle hour of the watch and counted the sounds: boots, wind, a bell from the lower camp, the scratch of Lysette's pencil two paces away.

Clara came to stand beside him at the corner with her back to the wind. She had healed the conscript's fingers and the cost had come back into her own hands, and she held them tucked under her arms.

"He will never see me," she said, without any preface.

"Yes."

"I am fine." She said it before he could answer, as if she had been keeping the sentence ready for a long time and had decided where to put it. "I know what it looks like. I know what people think when we sit together. I know he would be kind if he knew, and that would be worse than this, because then I would have to be the person who told him." She looked out at the grey country. "I am fine with it." She rubbed her thumb along the seam of her glove where the linen had worn through. "I have had months to be fine with it. I had it before you came to the Wall, and I will have it after you go back to school, and it will not change because a soldier stared at a blanket."

"That is not fine," Eden said.

It was the first thing he had said to her all week that was not a fact about the weather or a number. Clara turned her head and looked at him for a moment, and then she moved a step closer so that her shoulder touched his sleeve, and she did not say anything about it. The warmth came through the wool of the scarf where it lay against her arm, and neither of them moved for the length of six breaths.

"I know," she said, at last. "Thank you for saying it." She let her shoulder stay where it was for a moment longer, then took it away and pulled her gloves up. "Do not say it again. Once is enough to know that someone heard me."

Felix came along the wall in the small hours with a lantern held low and a pencil in his hand. He had been down to the ditch to check the oil line Eden had laid, and he had followed it from the stores to the buttress and back, and he had found three stone markers where Eden had left three stone markers and no fourth where there should not be one.

"I checked your lamps," he said.

"Yes."

"They are not lamps yet. They are a line in a ditch."

"I know."

Felix put the lantern down and looked along the wall at the snow going over the parapet. "I did not tell anyone what I found, and I did not write it down." He picked the lantern up again. "That is the whole report."

"Why not?"

"Because you laid it for lamps and it is for lamps." Felix looked at the ditch over the wall. "If it were for something else you would have told me, and then I would have to decide whether to write that down." He moved off a step and stopped. "I do not like deciding that."

"I know."

"No, you do not. You never have to decide it." Felix went down the stair with the lantern in front of him. "I am telling you so that you know I have chosen."

He went back down the stair, and Eden watched the little circle of its light move across the snow and disappear under the wall.

Lysette came along the wall twice more during the storm and stopped both times at the corner. On the first pass she asked whether the merlon needed clearing, and on the second she held out a strip of linen for Eden's hands without saying whose hands had wrapped it. He took it because she held it out, and she wrote something down and went away with her hood up against the wind.

At the change of watch the groundskeeper came up with a bucket of hot water for the brackets. He had come out in the storm without a hat, and his brown coat was wet to the shoulders, and he was humming the four notes with the wrong note at the top as usual. Eden stood four paces away with his hand on the parapet and watched the man set the bucket down.

The groundskeeper breathed out, and the breath went up in the cold air and broke apart. His shadow lay on the snow behind him with its edge sharp from the lamp. It did not move when his chest moved. Eden watched two breaths, then a third, and on the third the shadow held its shape while the man's shoulders rose and fell above it.

Eden looked at the bucket. It had a rim of ice around the water and a towel folded over the handle, and the towel was dry even though the man's coat was wet to the shoulders. He had come out in the storm without a hat. His hair was flat and dry, as if he had stepped from a doorway into the yard rather than walked across it in the wind.

The groundskeeper straightened and went on humming, and the shadow stayed exactly where it was.
