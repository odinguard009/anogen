# Chapter 43: The Shadow and the Breath

A storm came up from the grey country in the afternoon. The snow on the battlement lifted into a sheet and moved sideways. The sergeant doubled the watch. Lance Nine drew the stretch by the inner stair, where the wall turned a corner and the wind hit from two sides at once. The sergeant gave them an extra bucket of coal for the brazier and one rule: keep your hoods up. He wrote the rule on his board, because the wind would carry away a spoken one.

Rowan stood at the corner with his spear planted. He had to turn his whole body to look at anything, and he hated it. Corner watch made him feel that the real war was happening somewhere else.

Clara came up from the warm hut with her hands wrapped in cloth. A conscript from the third stretch had come off the wall with two fingers white and hard. He had stood on the parapet without gloves, because he thought it would be a short watch. Clara had worked on his hand for twenty minutes, and the fingers had turned pink again.

"They will stay on," she told Eden, when he came by the stores hut for a roll of bandage. "They will hurt for a week."

"You are shaking."

"I am fine." Clara put the roll back on the shelf without looking at it. "When I heal someone, the cold goes out of him and into me. It is not a great price. An hour with no feeling in my hands. Sometimes a day."

"Is that all?"

"Yes." She held out her hands. The tips of her fingers were pale. "I am telling you so you will not write a form about it."

Eden wrote the hour on the stores list and nothing more. He had learned something about Clara. She gave facts away like bread rolls, but if he asked for a second one, she put the first back in the bag. Once, she said, it had taken more than a day. She had not told anyone. He did not ask.

Rowan came up the stair with his scarf frozen to his chin. He had been to see the conscript twice in ten minutes. Each time he had asked the same three questions: Is he warm? Will the fingers stay? Does he need anything?

"He has said no twice," Clara said.

"I will ask a third time."

"He will say no a third time."

"Then I will know." Rowan rubbed his hands together. "I hate not knowing if someone needs something."

"Asking will not fix that."

"I know." He turned back to the parapet. "I will ask anyway."

He went to Lance Three's stretch and came back a minute later with an empty cup. The conscript had said yes to a fourth question, whether he wanted soup. Rowan had stayed until the cup was empty. "That is all I wanted to know," he said, to nobody, and carried the cup down the stair as if it might spill.

Rowan stopped beside Eden on the way back. He lowered his voice, which for him meant the whole stretch could still hear.

"Is Clara cold? She will not say."

"Her hands are cold."

"Then I should give her my gloves." Rowan began to pull at one.

"Ask her first," Eden said. "She does not like to be given things."

Rowan frowned. "That does not make sense. Everyone likes gloves."

"She likes to say yes herself."

Rowan looked at his glove, then at the dark where Clara stood, and then he put it back on slowly. "I will ask later. When there is no wind." He went to his corner, and Eden saw that he was thinking, which for Rowan looked like a man carrying something heavy across a floor.

Clara watched him go. She did not smile. Her face went very still, in the way it did when she was holding something carefully.

* * *

Late in the afternoon a sentry on the next stretch slipped on the ice and slid six feet along the walkway. Nobody saw it except Felix, who ran along the wall and pulled him up by his belt. The sentry said he was fine. His knee was bleeding through the cloth. Felix walked him to the hut with one arm under his shoulder and came back with a roll he had not eaten. "He needed it more," he said, and put it in Eden's pocket without looking.

Lysette came along the wall during the watch with her book under her arm. She spoke to Rowan first, about the snow and the conscript's fingers. Then she stopped beside Eden.

"Your scarf is frozen all the way through," she said. "You are still wearing it."

"I am warm."

"You said that when I told you your hands were white. It is the same sentence. You use it when you do not want a question."

"Then I will use another one."

"Please do." She waited. He had no other one. She wrote on the page, and then she showed it to him, which she never did. *Cold.* One word. "I am not writing down what you said. I am writing down the weather."

That was something she had not done before. She had watched Rowan less since the night of the fire, and Eden more. He felt it, the way a man feels a lamp turned toward him.

* * *

The storm grew after dark. The snow stopped falling and began to travel across the wall in long flat sheets. The men kept their heads down and their hands on the stone. The brazier at the corner burned with a short blue flame that gave no heat beyond a man's elbow. Somewhere along the stretch a soldier sang two lines of a song, then stopped, because the wind took the words away.

Eden took the middle watch. He counted the sounds he could hear: boots, wind, a bell from the lower camp, the scratch of Lysette's pencil two paces off.

Once the wind dropped for a few breaths. In the gap, he heard something small and regular, from the direction of the stores. It was a shovel. Someone was digging in frozen ground at an hour when nobody dug. The wind returned and covered it. He did not leave his post. He marked the direction in his head: the stores, the ditch, the buttress.

Clara came and stood beside him with her back to the wind. Her hands were tucked under her arms. The cost of the healing had come back into them, and she was holding them there so that nobody would see.

"He will never see me," she said.

She said it without any preface. Eden knew who "he" was.

"I am fine," Clara went on, before he could answer. She sounded like someone who had kept the sentence ready for a long time and knew exactly where to put it. "I know how it looks when we sit together. I know people stare at the blanket. Rowan thinks it is a blanket. I do not think he will ever think otherwise, because that is how he is. He looks at everything in the world, and he does not look at the girl sitting next to him." She looked out at the grey. "I have had months to be fine with it. I was fine with it before I came to the Wall, and I will be fine after."

"That is not fine," Eden said.

It was the first thing he had said to her all week that was not a fact about weather or a number. Clara turned her head and looked at him. Her eyes were bright with cold, and perhaps with something else.

Then she moved a step closer, so that her shoulder touched his sleeve. She did not say anything about it. Warmth came through the wool where she leaned. Neither of them moved for six breaths.

"I know," she said at last. "Thank you for saying it." She took her shoulder away and pulled her gloves up. "Do not say it again. Once is enough, to know that someone heard."

Eden took off his outer gloves. They were the only dry pair he had. He held them out. Clara looked at the gloves, then at his bare knuckles.

"You will lose a finger," she said. "Then I will have to mend it, and I have no hands left tonight."

She took the gloves anyway. They were too big, and she flexed the empty ends of the fingers and let them hang. After that she stood beside him and did not speak. The wind pushed at both of them from two sides.

* * *

Lysette came by once more before Felix. She held out a strip of clean linen for his hands. She did not say whose hands had folded it. Eden took it because she held it out, and she wrote one short line in her book and went away with her hood up against the wind.

When she was gone he wrote on the back of a stores slip, in pencil: *Shovel, small hours, toward the ditch. Who digs in a storm?* He put the slip in his coat. A man who digs in a storm is a man who does not want to be seen. The storm was the best cover the camp would have all winter, and Eden wrote the hour beside the question.

Felix came along the wall in the small hours with a lantern held low and a pencil in his other hand. He had been down to the ditch. Eden had laid an oil line there for "lamps". Felix had followed it from the stores to the buttress and back.

"I checked your lamps," he said.

"Yes."

"They are not lamps yet. They are a line in a ditch."

"I know."

Felix put the lantern on the stone. He looked along the wall, where the snow was going over the parapet. "I did not write down what I found. I did not tell anyone either. That is the whole report."

"Why?"

"Because you said it was for lamps. If it were for something else, you would tell me. Then I would have to decide whether to write that down." He picked up the lantern. "I do not like deciding that."

"I know."

"No, you do not. You never have to decide it. I am telling you so you know I have chosen." Felix went down the stair with the light in front of him.

Eden watched the small circle of lantern glow cross the snow and disappear under the wall. Felix had told him something he had not asked for: that a friend can keep quiet on purpose, and that it costs him. Eden did not know how to pay that back. He put it on the list of things he owed, which was getting long.

* * *

At the change of watch the groundskeeper came up with a bucket of hot water for the brackets. He had walked across the yard in the storm with no hat. His brown coat was wet to the shoulders. He hummed the same four notes, wrong at the top. Eden stood four paces away with a hand on the parapet and watched him set the bucket down.

The groundskeeper breathed out. The breath went up in the cold air and broke apart. A lamp on the wall threw his shadow across the snow behind him, sharp at the edges.

The shadow did not move when his chest moved.

Eden watched one breath, then two. On the third the shadow held its shape while the man's shoulders rose and fell above it. A shadow follows the person who makes it. This one only stayed where it was.

He looked at the bucket. It had a rim of ice around the water. A towel was folded over the handle, and the towel was dry, though the man's coat was soaked. His hair was flat and dry, as if he had stepped out of a doorway, not walked across a yard in a gale.

The groundskeeper straightened and went on humming. The shadow stayed exactly where it was.
