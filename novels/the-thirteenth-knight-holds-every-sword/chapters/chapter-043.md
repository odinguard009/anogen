# Chapter 43: The Quiet Watch

The storm held off all day. The sky went the colour of old iron. The wind stayed low behind the wall as if it were waiting for something. spear Nine took the last watch from the eighth bell to dawn. And Eden stood at the left parapet with the scarf under his coat and his gloves sealed at the wrists.

The wall was quiet in the way a wall is quiet before snow. The men on the next stretch had stopped talking an hour earlier. The lamps burned low because the oil had thickened in the cold. Eden counted the turns of the parapet and then stopped counting. Because a thing with a number was a thing he could hold. He wanted to hold something while the dark beyond the wall stayed so still.

A soldier from the next stretch came along the wall at the tenth bell with his hands wrapped in cloth and his face drawn tight. He asked Clara whether she could look at his fingers. She put down her staff and took his hands. The cloth came away with a sound like paper stuck to a wound. Three fingers on his left hand were black at the tips. The skin around the nails had gone hard and pale. He kept his hands still while she looked at them. Which was a thing a man did when he was more frightened than he wanted to show.

Clara put both hands around the injured fingers and closed her eyes. She did not move for a while. The colour came back into the fingertips in a thin line that ran from the joint to the nail. The soldier opened and closed his hand. He said that he could feel it again. Then he thanked her and went back along the wall with his hands bare.

Eden watched Clara put her staff on the stone and sit down on the step. Her face had lost its colour. There was a line of sweat on her upper lip that froze before he could see it properly. He took a step toward her. She put up a hand without looking at him. He stopped, because she had known he was coming before he moved.

"I am all right," she said.

"You are not."

"That is a thing people say when they want to argue. And I do not want to argue. She opened her eyes and looked at the wall. "He would have lost two fingers. That is inside what I can do, and I did it."

"Inside what you can do is not the same as free."

"No," Clara said, and she got up and picked up her staff, and she stood with her back straight and her hands on the wood. "It is not free. I know that."

Eden watched the soldier walk away. The man's hands were bare and his step was light. He did not look back at the parapet where Clara had been. He knew the soldier would not remember who had warmed his fingers. That was right, and it was a thing Clara had known before she sat down, and it made no difference to what she had done.

Rowan had seen the soldier come and had not seen the colour go out of Clara's face. Because he had been watching the dark beyond the parapet. When he turned he saw her standing too straight. He came over and asked what had happened. And Clara said that a man had cold fingers and that she had warmed them. And Rowan said that she looked like she had been the one in the cold. She said that she was fine. He said that she always said that when she was not. She said nothing to that because it was true.

He stood beside her with his shield in his hand and his shoulders hunched. He wanted to help and did not know how. After a while he put his shield down and took off his own gloves and held them out. She looked at them and then at his red hands. She said that he would freeze. He said that he had two hands and could manage. She took one glove and put it on her left hand. She left him with the other. He stood there with one glove and a very surprised face. And Felix, who had been watching from the lamp post, said that this was the most sensible thing Rowan had done all winter, and Rowan said that he had not known he was doing it.

Lysette was on the right parapet with her round sheet in her hand. She had spent the first hour of the watch watching Rowan. As she had spent most of the winter. Then she found that she was watching Eden instead. She could not say when the change had happened. He was standing at the left with his hand on the stone and his head slightly down. There was nothing remarkable about him that she could see. She shut her book and opened it again and wrote a line about a loose rail. Because a loose rail was a thing she could write down without writing his name.

Clara came past her on the way back from RowanShe stopped and looked at the dark beyond the wall. Then she said, without turning, "I know he will never see me."

Lysette put her pencil down, because there was no form for a sentence like that.

"I have known it since October," Clara said, and her voice was plain. "He is kind and he is loud and he sees everything in the world except the person standing next to him. I am fine with it."

"That's not fine."

"I am," Clara said. "I am fine with it, and I am not going to ask you to say anything about it, and if you write it in your book I will know."

Lysette shut the book, and she said, "I am not writing it. She looked across the wall at EdenThen back at ClaraShe said, "Are you truly fine?"

Clara looked at her hands and then at the dark. She said, "No," and then she said, "But I will be," and she walked on with her staff, and Lysette stood with her book shut and did not follow.

Felix checked the oil lines in the dark while the others stood at their posts. He went down the beam behind the stores and ran one gloved hand along the underside. The oil was dry and dark. The line held a shadow when he put his lamp near it. He said nothing about it to anyone. He came back up the stair with his hands clean and his face calm. He put a peppermint on the step beside Eden's boot and walked on. Which was the only report he ever gave.

The watch went on, and the sky lightened by slow degrees, and the dark beyond the wall became grey land. The birds on the warment lifted their heads one at a time. Felix came back from the stores and stood at the stair with his hands in his pockets. He looked at Eden and then at the line of boots on the step. He did not ask who had put a peppermint there. Because a peppermint on a step was a thing a person could understand without asking.

Near dawn the groundskeeper came up the stair with a broom over his shoulder. He was early and he was humming, and he stopped when he saw the watch still at the wall. He stood with his hands on the broom and his face open and friendly. He hummed the last note again. The note went down and up, and his chest moved with it.

Eden watched the man's shadow on the stone. And the shadow held the shape of the broom and the round cap. And it did not move when his chest moved. And it stayed still while the tune went on. And Eden put his hand on the wall and counted his breaths. And the breaths were right and the shadow was wrong. And he did not say anything. Because a man who was about to be sure of a thing should not hurry it.

When the watch ended, Clara went down the stair ahead of the others. Rowan went after her with the spare glove in his hand, and he called her name once, and she stopped and turned. He held the glove out and said that he wanted it back only if she did not need it, and she said that she needed it, and he smiled and put it in his pocket anyway. Eden watched them from the parapet. And Lysette came up beside him and put the round sheet in her coat. And she said that the report was finished and that she had written nothing about Clara's hands. And he said that she had been kind. And she said that she had been accurate. And that the two things were different and she was not going to discuss it.

"Did you see the groundskeeper? she asked.

"Yes."

"What did you see?"

Eden looked at the stair and then at the yard far below. And he said, "He hums a tune I know, and his shadow does not keep time with him. Lysette shut her book and said nothing for a moment. And then she said that she had heard the tune in the stores office on the first week of the rotation. And that she had written a line about it because a man who hummed out of tune was a thing a person remembered. And she asked whether Eden knew the tune. And he said that he did. And he did not say where from. And she wrote a line in her book and underlined it twice. And she did not ask the next question, which was a thing she was learning to do.

At breakfast the next morning Rowan asked Clara whether her hand was warm. And she said that it was. And he asked whether it would stay warm. And she said that she did not know. He said he could fetch more gloves from the stores, and she said the stores had none. He said he would make some, and Felix said Rowan could not sew, and Rowan said he could sew a straight line, and Felix said a straight line was not a glove. Clara laughed into her cup, and Rowan looked at her and then at the table, and he said that he would learn, and she said she believed him. The table went quiet in a comfortable way, and Lysette watched all of it from the end of the bench with her book closed and her cup untouched.

Eden sat with them and ate the bun that Felix had left on the step the night before. And it was hard and cold. And he ate it in small pieces. And Felix watched him and said that the bun had been intended for the morning and that this was the morning. Eden said that he had eaten it in the morning, and Felix said the night belonged to yesterday and a man could not eat yesterday's bun today. Eden said that he had, and Felix put his face in his hands and said he would never learn to win an argument with him. Clara passed the tea, and Rowan took the cup and gave it to Clara, and Lysette wrote one line in her book, and she did not let anyone see what the line said. He went to his post and stood there with the scarf under his coat. The sky was grey and the birds were quiet. He counted them twice and got the same number. He wrote the number down and closed the book. The wall was quiet. He waited. The cold came in under the door.
