# Chapter 46: The Wrong Side

The barracks were on the wrong side of the fire. That meant forty people had to cross the yard before the Mawgate finished opening.

Eden stood at the stores door with a burning curtain dragging past his boot. He counted the people as they came out of the huts. There were soldiers in their shirts, cooks, two messengers, a physician, and four children from the lower city who had been sleeping in the warm room by the kitchens. The number was forty.

"Forty," he told Okoru. "Not one more."

Okoru looked at the yard and did not ask how Eden had counted. "Where do they go?"

"The side gate in the south wall. The main gate is burning."

"Then they go to the side gate. One line. Nobody runs." Okoru turned to the prefects and said it again, louder. He had a burn across one shoulder, and he had stopped chewing anything at all. "Lance Nine is the nearest. Wren, you have the yard."

Four people had no boots. The cook's boy and two of the children were barefoot, and the physician had left her shoes by the brazier.

"Then they cross the snow barefoot," Okoru said, "and we carry them when they fall."

The physician, a thin woman with her sleeves already rolled, took the cook's boy on her back without being asked and walked out into the yard. Two soldiers closed up behind her with their shields turned toward the fire. Eden watched the small group go and noted the order of it. In a minute someone would ask who had gone first, and he wanted to know the answer.

The yard was a square of trampled snow, with the stores hut burning at its east end. The kitchen step stood at the west end, where the line was forming. The side gate was a door in the south wall. In the north lane, between the Husks and the step, stood a supply wagon with its horse still in the shafts. In the middle of the yard the broken floor of the hut was spitting grey. Husks were climbing out of it, small and fast and silent. Behind them came Embers, the grafted men, with grey flesh grown through their sleeves. The two kinds moved together without colliding. Eden had never seen that before, and he had no time to ask why.

The Embers had muskets. One raised his gun at the cooks and fired. The gun made a sound like a wet stick breaking, and the ball went nowhere. The air above the fire was thick with a haze that fouled the locks. He fired again, and the barrel bent inward. He threw the gun down and drew a knife.

"No more guns," Felix said from the step. "Now it is only blades and arrows."

"Keep your arrow for something worth it," Eden said.

* * *

The danger was the north lane. The Husks had seen the line at the kitchen step. They were running along the lane toward it, six of them, in a row. Between them and the step there was only the wagon and its shadow. The stores fire threw the wagon's shadow across the snow in a long dark sheet with a clean edge.

Eden had the plan before the first Husk reached it. A Door could open in any shadow that held still. A moving shadow could not hold a Door. He needed the wagon to stop.

He ran to the wheel. A soldier was driving the horse out of the yard. Eden put one hand on the wheel and said, "Stop the horse."

The driver looked at a boy in a scorched coat with a black lance in his hand. He stopped the horse.

The wagon's shadow lay still. The six Husks stepped onto it, one after another, still coming.

Eden knocked once on the ring, and **[Exa]** folded into the black shield. He did not wait for them. He knocked twice on the snow beside the wheel.

The shadow opened under their feet. It was a black doorway as wide as the wagon, with firelight along one edge. The six Husks dropped through it one after another. None came out. A grafted Ember at the edge put out a grey hand to pull one back. The hand went into the dark and did not return, and he pulled away with a cry.

Eden held the Door open for a count of ten, until the north lane was empty. Then he shut it. The cold sat in his chest where the heat of the yard could not reach. He took one breath through his scarf and counted again. Forty. The number held.

A cook was counting under her breath near the step, one finger at a time. When she reached forty she began again from one, as if the number might change if she stopped.

Felix had been watching from the step. He lowered the bow an inch. "Six Husks went into a shadow," he said.

"Yes."

"I saw it. I do not think anyone else did. They were all counting."

"Yes."

"Then I did not see it," Felix said, and put the arrow back in the quiver, and said nothing more. It was the second time in a week he had chosen not to write something down.

Nobody had seen the Door. They had seen a boy put his hand on a wagon, and then six Husks had stopped coming. Eden did not correct what anyone thought.

Behind him, a shout broke out at the stores door. Three Embers had come round the burning hut and cut off the doorway. Okoru stood alone against them with his stick in one hand and no other arm. Two prefects had run. Okoru did not look back.

Eden began to step that way. Okoru saw it and barked one word. "Yard!"

"Sir."

"I said you have the yard." Okoru knocked an Ember's blade off line with the stick and drove his shoulder into the man's chest. "I can hold a door. Hold the yard."

Eden turned his back on him. It was the hardest thing he did that night, and he did it without any sign on his face.

* * *

At the kitchen step, ten paces west, five Husks had come in through the broken fence behind the line. They were walking at the children.

Rowan stood in front of them with his spear. Clara stood one step behind his left shoulder. Neither of them had waited for an order.

What Eden saw was this. Rowan's hands glowed. The glow ran into the spear. That was **[Ithe]**: a burst of gold light from the tip, strong enough to push a body back, with a cold price for the man who threw it. It lasted one heartbeat. It was the first time Eden had seen it used in a fight.

Rowan planted the spear in the snow and put up both hands. Gold light ran up his arms and burst from the spear head in a short, hard wave. **[Ithe]** hit the Husks in the chest, and they stopped as if they had walked into a wall. They fell back across the snow, and their shadows swung hard against the kitchen wall. For a moment Rowan was the brightest thing in the yard, and every Ember across the lane turned to look at him.

The light went out at once. Rowan's hands dropped. He staggered one step, and Clara caught his sleeve.

"You are cold," she said.

"I know."

"Do not do it again."

"We will see." Rowan picked up the spear with one hand. The other was not answering properly.

What Eden saw next was a thin green line. **[Wyl]** was Clara's. Anything that crossed the line stopped at the line, and whatever force it threw at the line came back into Clara's own body. He had never seen her use it in a fight. Now he watched her feel it.

Clara set her staff in the snow. A green line ran out from the staff across the yard and stopped at a crack in the stone, where two Husks were about to climb through. **[Wyl]** held them on the far side of the line. Every blow they struck came back into Clara, one after another. She did not move her feet.

Her mouth went tight. A thin line of blood showed under her nose where the blows had come back into her. She wiped it with her wrist and set the staff down again farther along the crack. The green line ran to a second group of Husks and held them too. For one second the snow under her knees turned green, like grass in spring, and then brown again. She did not look down.

Felix stood at the step with an arrow on the string. He had no clean shot through the smoke. "I saw that," he said.

"Do not write it down," Clara said.

"I was not going to." Felix took the arrow off the string. "I am beginning to understand why Eden never explains things. Someone would write them down."

* * *

The main gate burst open, and a Lance came over the burning timbers, late and furious. Valerius was at the head of them with his sword drawn and his coat scorched down one side. He was a prince's son, and he had come from the north camp when he saw the sky.

He stopped when he saw the yard. For one breath a thin skin of frost ran across the wall beside him, white and sudden, and then it melted into water. He looked at the frost, then at his own hand, which shook once. He looked at Eden. Eden's face showed nothing, and Valerius, who had expected a question, said nothing either. Behind him the Lance came through the gate in a tight line with their shields up. None of them mentioned the frost. They had been taught to ignore what their officers did not mention, and tonight that training was worth more to Valerius than a second sword.

"The king wants this camp held until dawn," Valerius said.

"We are moving forty people out through the side gate."

"Then I will hold the main gate until you finish." He took one look at the empty lane and the line of cooks, and turned his sword toward the burning timbers. "Get your people out."

A Husk reached the gate behind the second prefect. Valerius took off its head with one cut and did not slow.

The children reached the side gate with the cooks and the physician. On the far side the prefects counted them again, and the number was still forty. Eden stood at the kitchen step, and smoke went up between him and the people he had sent through. He watched the Mawgate's rim widen another foot in the broken floor. It had no edge that a man could cut. It was a hole in the world that kept getting larger.

A boy's voice came from the wagon, high and clear above the fire. It was the conscript whose fingers Clara had healed in the storm. He was standing on the wagon's tail board in the north lane, thirty paces from the step, with his coat over his arm. He had no idea that a new line of Husks had turned toward him.

Clara saw him first. She dropped her staff and ran out past the green line, into the open yard. She put herself between the boy and the grey hands before anyone could call her back.

Rowan shouted her name. He took two steps after her, and Eden put a hand on his chest. Rowan knocked the hand away and kept going.

Eden saw it all from the lane and could not reach them in time. Felix stood at the step with an arrow on the string and no shot. Lysette came up beside him with her book shut and the pencil in her hand. Both watched two figures running across the yard, with the grey line closing behind them.
