# Chapter 46: The Wrong Side

The barracks were on the wrong side of the fire, which meant forty people had to cross the yard before the Mawgate finished opening. Eden stood at the stores door with a burning curtain dragging past his boot. He counted them as they came. Twenty-three soldiers, six cooks, four prefects, two messengers, a physician and four children from the lower city. The children had been sleeping in the warm room by the kitchens.

"Forty," he said, to Okoru. "That is the number."

Okoru looked at the yard and did not ask how Eden had counted. "Who has no boots?"

"The cook's boy. Two of the children. The physician left her shoes by the brazier."

"Then they cross the snow barefoot and we carry them when they fall."

The physician, a thin woman with her sleeves already rolled, took the boy with no boots on her back without being asked and went out into the yard at a walk. Two soldiers closed up behind her with their shields turned to the fire side. Eden watched the small procession go and noted the order of it, because in a minute someone would ask who had gone first. Okoru put a hand on his stick and turned to the prefects. "Side gate. One line. Nobody runs."

"Who has the children?" Lysette asked, at the line by the kitchens.

"I do," a cook said. "I have had them since the floor went up."

"Keep them together. Do not let them look behind them." Lysette wrote the number on her palm because her book was shut and there was no time to open it. "Four."

"I counted four as well."

"Then the number is useful." She moved along the line and put her hand on the shoulder of the smallest child, who had no boots and was making no sound at all. "Walk."

A prefect said, "Sir, the main gate is burning."

"Then use the other door." Okoru did not look at him. "I gave an order." His coat had a burn across the shoulder and he had stopped chewing anything at all. He turned to Eden. "Lance Nine is the nearest unit. You have the yard."

Rowan came through with his spear and a cloak over his arm. Clara followed with her hands glowing faintly from work she had done without being asked. Felix carried a sack of bread over one shoulder and the bow in his hand. Lysette brought up the rear with her red band straight and her book shut. There was no time to write.

Now the yard was a field of moving shadows. The oil line burned along the north ditch, and the stores hut burned behind Eden, so every person crossing the snow threw two shadows. Husks came up out of the broken floor in the middle of the yard in a slow grey flood. Grafted Embers came with them. The two kinds moved together without colliding, which Eden had never seen before and did not have time to understand.

The yard had three lanes. The cooks and children went along the south wall to the side gate. The kitchen step lay at the west end, with the line forming at it. The wagon stood in the north lane, between the Husks and the step. Eden marked it and stood at the wagon's wheel.

An Ember fired a musket at the cooks. The gun made a sound like a wet stick breaking, and the ball went nowhere, because the fire's mana haze had fouled the lock. He fired again and the barrel bent inward. He threw the gun down and drew a blade.

"Guns are done," Felix said from the line. "Now it is only blades and arrows."

"Keep your arrow for something worth it," Eden said.

A soldier went down under a grey arm, and two cooks dragged him by his coat toward the side gate. The Husks behind him did not stop. They ran down the north lane at the kitchen step, and the only way along that lane was across the shadow of the wagon.

Eden had seen that in the first minute. A Door could take a whole stretch of lane, if the lane was a shadow and the shadow held still. He knocked once on the ring, and **[Exa]** folded into the black shield, tray-sized, the iron ring on the outside. He put it edge-on against the nearest Husk's arm and drove the arm up and away. The weight ran up his arm to the teeth. He kept his feet, and the soldier crawled out from under the grey fingers and ran.

Then he put his hand on the wagon's wheel. "Stop the horse."

The driver stopped the horse. A boy in a scorched coat with a black lance in his hand was not a person to argue with in a yard like this.

The wagon's shadow lay across the snow in a long dark sheet with a clean edge, where the stores fire cut across it. Now it was still. Eden waited until the first six Husks were on the dark patch. Then he knocked twice on the snow beside the wheel.

The shadow opened under their feet. It was a black doorway as wide as the wagon, with the firelight along one edge. The six Husks dropped out of the yard one after another, and none of them came out. A grafted Ember at the edge stopped with his grey hand out. His hand went into the dark and did not come back, and he pulled away with a cry.

Eden held the Door open for the count of ten, until the north lane was empty of Husks. Then he shut it. The cold sat in his chest where the heat of the yard did not reach. He took one breath through his scarf and counted forty again. The number held.

Beside him a cook was counting under her breath, one finger at a time. When she reached forty she began again from one, as if the number might change if she stopped.

* * *

At the kitchen step, ten steps west, five Husks had broken through behind the line and were walking at the children. Rowan stood in front of them with the spear. Clara stood a step behind his left shoulder.

Rowan planted the spear in the snow and held up both hands. Gold light ran up his arms and burst from the spear head in a short hard wave. **[Ithe]** hit the Husks in the chest, and the grey bodies stopped as if they had walked into a wall. The Husks fell back from the children across the snow, and their shadows swung hard against the kitchen wall. Rowan was now the brightest thing in the yard, and the Embers across the lane turned to look at him.

The light went out at once. Rowan's hands dropped to his sides, and he staggered one step before Clara caught his sleeve.

"You are cold," she said.

"I know."

"Do not do it again."

"We will see." Rowan took the spear in one hand, because the other was not answering properly.

Clara put her staff down in the snow. A green line ran out from it across the yard. It stopped at a crack in the stone where two Husks were about to climb through. **[Wyl]** held them on the far side of the line. Every blow the Husks struck came back into Clara, one after another. She did not move her feet.

Clara's mouth had gone tight and a line of blood showed under her nose where the blows had come back into her. She wiped it with her wrist and put the staff down again farther along the crack. The line ran out to a second group of Husks and held them too. The snow under her knees had gone green again for one second and then brown, and she did not look down at it.

Felix had come up to the step beside them with his bow. "I saw that," he said.

"Do not write it down," Clara said.

"I was not going to." Felix put an arrow on the string and took it off again because there was no clean shot through the smoke. "I am beginning to understand why Eden never explains things. Someone would write them down."

"Someone would try." Clara lifted the staff as a grey arm came over the crack. "That is the difference."

Eden walked back along the lane to the kitchen step. The prince's Lance came over the burnt main gate late and furious. Valerius was at the head of them with his sword in his hand and his coat scorched down one side. A wall of frost had risen from his shoulder when he saw the camp burning. It stayed on the stone for one breath and then melted into water. He looked at the frost, then at Eden, and said nothing about either.

"My father sent word," Valerius said. "The king wants the camp held until dawn."

"We are moving forty people through it."

"Then I will hold the main gate until you are finished." Valerius looked over the yard once and saw what Eden had seen: the empty lane, the fire, the line of cooks crossing the snow. The frost on his own shoulder that had come and gone without his asking. He said nothing about the frost and turned his sword toward the gate.

"Get your people out," he said. "My Lance will hold the main gate."

"I have the yard."

"Then hold it." Valerius turned to his own Lances and gave three orders in a row, each short enough to be heard over the fire. His men moved at once. One of them dropped a bundle of ward-plate gloves in the snow and another picked it up and passed it along without stopping. The prince did not look at either of them.

A Husk reached the main gate behind the second prefect. Valerius took its head off with one cut and did not slow. The frost on his shoulder came back for a heartbeat and went again. A man of his Lance, a broad boy, looked at it and then at his prince and quickly looked at the Husk instead, which was what a good soldier did with a thing he had not been told to see.

Behind them the yard went on burning. The children reached the side gate with the cooks and the physician. On the far side the prefects counted them again, and the number was still forty. Eden stood at the kitchen step. Smoke went up between him and the people he had just sent through. He watched the Mawgate's rim widen another foot in the broken floor. The rim had no edge that a man could cut, because it was not a shadow and not a thing thrown by light. It was a hole in the world that kept getting larger. The warmth coming out of it had reached the step through the snow.

A boy's voice came from the wagon, high and clear above the fire. It was the conscript whose fingers Clara had healed in the storm. He was standing on the wagon's tail board in the north lane, thirty paces from the step, with a coat over his arm. He had no idea that a new line of Husks had turned toward him.

Clara saw him first. She dropped her staff and ran out past the green line into the open yard. She put herself between the boy and the grey hands before anyone could call her back. Rowan shouted her name and took two steps after her. Eden put a hand on his chest to stop him. Rowan hit the hand away and kept going.

"Do not both of you go," Eden said.

"You stay with the yard," Rowan said, and did not look round. "I can reach her."

Felix stood at the step with an arrow on the string and no clean shot. Lysette came up beside him with her book shut and the pencil in her hand. Both of them watched the two figures running across the yard, with the grey line closing behind them.
