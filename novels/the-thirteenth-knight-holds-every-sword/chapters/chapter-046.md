# Chapter 46: The Wrong Side

The barracks were on the wrong side of the fire, which meant forty people had to cross the yard before the Mawgate finished opening. Eden stood at the stores door with a burning curtain dragging past his boot. He counted them as they came. Twenty-three soldiers, six cooks, four prefects, two messengers, a physician and four children from the lower city. The children had been sleeping in the warm room by the kitchens.

"Forty," he said, to Okoru. "That is the number."

Okoru looked at the yard and did not ask how Eden had counted. "Who has no boots?"

"The cook's boy. Two of the children. The physician left her shoes by the brazier."

"Then they cross the snow barefoot and we carry them when they fall."

The physician, a thin woman with her sleeves already rolled, took the boy with no boots on her back without being asked and went out into the yard at a walk. Two soldiers closed up behind her with their shields turned to the fire side. Eden watched the small procession go and noted the order of it, because in a minute someone would ask who had gone first. Okoru put a hand on his stick and turned to the prefects. "Door by the gate. One line. Nobody runs."

"Who has the children?" Lysette asked, at the line by the kitchens.

"I do," a cook said. "I have had them since the floor went up."

"Keep them together. Do not let them look behind them." Lysette wrote the number on her palm because her book was shut and there was no time to open it. "Four."

"I counted four as well."

"Then the number is useful." She moved along the line and put her hand on the shoulder of the smallest child, who had no boots and was making no sound at all. "Walk."

A prefect said, "Sir, the gate is burning."

"Then use the other door." Okoru did not look at him. "I gave an order." His coat had a burn across the shoulder and he had stopped chewing anything at all. He turned to Eden. "Lance Nine is the nearest unit. You have the yard."

Rowan came through with his spear and a cloak over his arm. Clara followed with her hands glowing faintly from work she had done without being asked. Felix carried a sack of bread over one shoulder and the bow in his hand. Lysette brought up the rear with her red band straight and her book shut. There was no time to write.

Now the yard was a field of moving shadows. Along the ditch the oil line burned, and the fire in the stores hut threw a second light upward. Every person crossing the yard cast two shadows: one from the flame below and one from the flame behind. Husks came out of the broken floor in a slow flood. Grafted Embers came with them. The two kinds moved together without colliding, which was a thing Eden had never seen before and did not have time to understand.

A soldier went down under a grey arm and two cooks dragged him by his coat to the gate.

Eden went in after the third man who fell. He put **[Thessa]** edge-on to the stone, drove a Husk's arm up and away, and held the shield there with his shoulder while the soldier crawled out from under it. Its weight ran up his arm to the teeth. He kept his feet. He looked back once, saw a black shield and a boy behind it, and ran for the gate with his hands over his head.

Lysette put a baton under a Husk's wrist and turned it until the grey fingers opened. She stepped over the man on the snow. She did not stop to help him up because the line behind her was still moving. Felix shot once into the throat of an Ember at the coal pile. He fell into the fire. Felix did not look at what the fire did to him.

An Ember fired a musket at the cooks. The gun made a sound like a wet stick breaking and the ball went nowhere, because the mana haze over the fire had fouled the lock. He fired again and the barrel bent inward with a dull crack, and he threw the gun down and drew a blade instead.

"Guns are done," Felix said. "Now it is only blades and arrows."

"Then keep your arrow for something worth it," Eden said.

He needed the wagon. It was the only thing in the yard with a moving shadow large enough to hold a Door. A supply wagon stood at the north end with its shafts toward the gate and a canvas cover half over it. The fire behind it threw the canvas shadow across the snow in a long dark sheet with a clean edge. Wagon and shadow both moved as the horse strained at the traces. The edge of the shadow moved with it, which meant the Door would have to be cut while the wagon was still. Eden put his hand on the wheel and told the driver to stop. The driver did. A boy in a scorched coat with a black lance in his hand was not a person to argue with in a yard like this. Eden knocked twice on the air beside the wheel.

The Door opened in the wagon's moving shadow. The Husks nearest the wagon went through it because they followed the dark as water follows a channel. A grafted Ember followed them, then stopped at the edge with his grey hand out. His hand went into the dark and did not come back. Eden did not watch to see what happened on the far side.

The Door left a cold place in his chest where the heat of the yard did not reach. He took one breath through the scarf and counted forty again in his head, and the number held.

Beside him a cook was counting under her breath in the old way, one finger at a time, and when she reached forty she started again from one, as if the number might change if she let it. A Door was a door, and what was through it was not his to look at while forty people stood in the yard. The far side of the Door was the throat of the Mawgate: warm, grey and full of the sound that had sung under the cistern. Every Husk that went in stayed in. The shadow closed behind the last of them with a sound like a mouth shutting.

Rowan planted his spear in the snow and held out both hands. Gold light went up his arms and burst from the spear head in a short hard wave. The **[Ithe]** drove the Hushborn back from the children by the kitchen step. The light went out at once and Rowan's hands dropped to his sides, and he staggered one step before Clara caught his sleeve.

"You are cold," she said.

"I know."

"Do not do it again."

"We will see." Rowan put the spear back in the snow and held it with one hand, because the other was not answering properly.

Clara put her staff down on the burning ground. A green line ran out from it across the snow and stopped at a crack in the stone where two Husks had been about to climb through. The **[Wyl]** held them on the far side of the line. Every blow the Husks struck came back into Clara, one after another. She did not move her feet.

Clara's mouth had gone tight and a line of blood showed under her nose where the blows had come back into her. She wiped it with her wrist and put the staff down again farther along the crack. The line ran out to a second group of Husks and held them too. The snow under her knees had gone green again for one second and then brown, and she did not look down at it.

"I saw that," Felix said.

"Do not write it down."

"I was not going to." Felix put an arrow on the string and took it off again because there was no clean shot through the smoke. "I am beginning to understand why you never explain things. You do not explain them because someone would write them down."

"Someone would try." Eden put the shield up as a grey arm came over the wagon wheel. "That is the difference."

The prince's Lance came through the gate late and furious. Valerius was at the head of them with his sword in his hand and his coat scorched down one side. A wall of frost had risen from his shoulder when he saw the camp burning. It stayed on the stone for one breath and then melted into water. He looked at the frost, then at Eden, and said nothing about either.

"My father sent word," Valerius said. "The king wants the camp held until dawn."

"We are moving forty people through it."

"Then I will hold the gate until you are finished." Valerius looked over the yard once and saw what Eden had seen: the wagon, the fire, the line of cooks crossing the snow. The frost on his own shoulder that had come and gone without his asking. He said nothing about the frost and turned his sword toward the gate.

"Get your people out," he said. "My Lance will hold the gate."

"I have the yard."

"Then hold it." Valerius turned to his own Lances and gave three orders in a row, each short enough to be heard over the fire. His men moved at once. One of them dropped a bundle of ward-plate gloves in the snow and another picked it up and passed it along without stopping. The prince did not look at either of them.

A Husk reached the gate line behind the second prefect. Valerius took its head off with one cut and did not slow. The frost on his shoulder came back for a heartbeat and went again. A man of his Lance, a broad boy, looked at it and then at his prince and quickly looked at the Husk instead, which was what a good soldier did with a thing he had not been told to see.

Behind them the yard went on burning. The children reached the gate with the cooks and the physician. On the far side the prefects counted them again, and the number was still forty. Eden stood by the wagon with the Door shut behind him and smoke going up between him and the men he had just sent through. He watched the Mawgate's rim widen another foot in the broken floor. The rim had no edge that a man could cut, because it was not a shadow and not a thing thrown by light. It was a hole in the world that kept getting larger. The warmth coming out of it had reached the wagon wheel through the snow.

A boy's voice came from the kitchen step, high and clear above the fire. It was the conscript whose fingers Clara had healed in the storm. He was standing on the step with a coat over his arm and no idea that the line of Husks had turned toward him.

Clara saw him first. She dropped her staff, ran out past the green line into the open yard, and put herself between the boy and the grey hands before anyone could call her back. Rowan shouted her name and took two steps after her. Eden put a hand on his chest to stop him. Rowan hit the hand away and kept going.

"Do not both of you go," Eden said.

"You stay with the yard," Rowan said, and did not look round. "I can reach her."

Felix stood at the wagon with an arrow on the string and no clean shot. Lysette came up beside him with her book shut and the pencil in her hand, and both of them watched the two figures running across the yard with the grey line closing behind them.
