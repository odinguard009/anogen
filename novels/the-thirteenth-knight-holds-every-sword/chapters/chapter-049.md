# Chapter 49: Would Bells Ring

The camp had been burning for hours, and dawn was coming. The sky over the Wall had gone from black to the colour of old iron.

The place was the lane between the tents, ten steps wide and open at both ends. Lance Nine stood on the left, with Okoru behind them. Halvard Strent, the vice-headmaster, and a line of soldiers stood on the right. Desmond Harrow stood ten steps ahead of Eden in the snow. He had one arm. His coat hung open, and the grey had climbed to his jaw. In his left hand he held a knife against the throat of a conscript boy with a red band on his sleeve.

The foe was a dying man. That was what made him dangerous. He had nothing left to lose. The stake was the boy. The clock was the knife, which was half an inch from the skin, and the fall of a single hand.

"Do not shoot," Halvard Strent said, to a teacher who still held a blade. "I know. That is why I am telling you not to. Put it down and stand where you are. I am sorry." The teacher put the blade down.

The boy had stopped shaking. Eden noted that first. A hostage who stopped shaking had decided how he was going to die.

* * *

"Say it," Harrow said to Eden. He had shifted the knife a little, and the boy's chin went up to keep the blade away. "Say my name and let it come. I know you can."

Eden said nothing.

"Ask the boy what I am." Harrow's eyes were bright. "He has looked at me for an hour, and he has not said a word."

A soldier called out that the man had a knife at a boy's throat. Harrow laughed, because that was not the answer he had wanted.

"Then I will say it myself." He straightened, as if to show the camp his whole height. "Desmond Edric Harrow. That is my full name. There is a story the Crowns tell. When a man speaks his whole name at the right moment, in the right place, bells ring by themselves, and a power wakes up in him. I have waited my whole life to say it here."

He shouted it across the yard. "Desmond Edric Harrow! I give it to you!"

The words went out over the camp and came back off the stone. The bell tower stood beyond the burning rows with its empty frame showing through the smoke. A bird flew off the crossbar. A strip of canvas flapped once in the heat. No bell rang.

Harrow's face changed. He had expected something, and nothing had happened. He pushed the knife harder and shouted again. "Desmond Edric Harrow! Say it, Wren. Say it—"

Eden did not move.

"Would bells ring for me?" he said, in a very flat voice, to nobody. He had asked the Dragon the same question once, and had not been answered. It was not a joke, and it was not a threat. It was only a question he did not have the answer to. Harrow stared at him, angry and puzzled, and his grip on the boy's collar slipped by the width of a finger.

That was the bait. Eden had made himself look small, and quiet, and unsure. The enemy had looked at what he wanted to see.

* * *

Eden took off his left glove.

He did it slowly, one finger at a time, and laid the glove on a crate where the snow had not reached. His bare hand was pale in the dawn light. There was a small crease across the palm where the lining had pressed.

He had decided what to do. He went polite, which was a thing the Dragon had taught him. It was not courtesy. It was a way of making his face and his voice go very still, until anyone near him felt that they were looking at something that was not going to hurry.

"Mr. Harrow," he said, with his hands at his sides. "Let the boy go. I will not ask again."

He looked at Harrow. It was not a glare. He put his eyes on the man's face the way he would look at a ward about to fall. He stopped moving everything except his gaze.

Harrow's mouth shut on the next word. His hand on the boy's collar loosened. He had not decided to loosen it. The knife moved away from the boy's throat and hung in the air where his hand had left it.

The boy dropped the bucket. A prefect caught him by the coat and pulled him back through the line. The boy was sixteen at most. He sat down in the snow with his knees up and said, to nobody, in a small clear voice, "He said the bells would ring." Then he put his face in his arms. A teacher knelt beside him and did not try to make him stand. Nobody in the lane looked at the silent tower, though every one of them had listened for it. They looked at the boy, because he was the only thing in the yard they knew how to help.

Felix stood at the far side of the lane with **[Thessa]** on his arm and an arrow in his hand that he had not shot. He had seen the whole thing: the knife drifting away, the boy falling forward, Harrow's face. He said nothing. He looked at the shield in his hand, then at the snow under his boots, and then at everything except the space between Eden and Harrow. He put the arrow back in the quiver. His face did not change.

* * *

Harrow did not fall. He turned the grey stump toward Eden and fumbled in his coat with his left hand. He was close to the end of what the graft could give him. He had one more move, and he meant to use it.

Harrow did not wait for him. A strip of grey flesh shot out of his empty palm, long and thin as a whip, and cracked at Eden's face. Eden stepped left. The strip tore the scarf from his chin and left a burn across his cheek. It snapped back, and Harrow staggered, because every use cost him. That was the enemy ahead: he looked as if he was winning, and he was only spending the last of himself.

Eden drew **[Inead]**. The gold sword came into his bare hand, and the dawn light caught it. The fire behind him lit Harrow's neck from the side. In that light a seam showed at the join where the graft met the skin: a line no wider than a hair, bright as a thread pulled through cloth.

That was **[Lampwick]**. The sword followed whatever seam its own light showed, and it needed two things: light, and a seam. Eden had the light from the fire at his back and the gold from the blade. He had the seam on the man's neck. Nothing more was needed.

He stepped in. The gold went up the line at Harrow's throat in one clean cut and stopped at the base of the skull. Harrow folded to his knees and then onto his side. His chest kept moving. The grey in his eyes went out like a lamp turned down.

There was no noise when he fell. Somewhere a piece of canvas slid off a tent ridge with a soft slap. A soldier at the gate dropped a bucket. Those were the only sounds.

Eden let **[Inead]** go. The gold went out of the smoke. The grey on Harrow's neck stopped shining and turned the colour of wet ash.

The soldiers along the line had seen only white light. Some of them had looked away. A prefect said later that the lanterns had gone out all at once, and that when they came back the Crown was on the ground. Valerius stood with his hands open and did not tell anyone what he had seen. When a prefect asked him for an order about the fallen man, he gave one about the children instead.

Strent came last, with his list under his arm and snow on his shoulders. He looked at the fallen man for three breaths. Then he said, in a low voice that carried to nobody but Eden, "I am sorry." It was the same apology he used for a dropped pencil. It was not clear whom it was for.

A cold ache came into his temples, the Frost that followed every use of his gold sword. He stood still and let it pass, as he always did, and no one saw him do it.

Rowan walked over slowly, with his spear low. He looked at the fallen man, then at Eden's cheek. "You are burned."

"It is nothing."

"It is on your face. It is not nothing." Rowan did not ask about the white light. He had a look Eden knew, the look of a boy deciding not to ask, and Eden was sorry to see it. He had given Rowan one more thing to decide about.

Clara came behind him and put two fingers on Eden's cheek. The burn closed in the time it took to breathe twice. She swayed, and Rowan took her arm. "I had a little left," she said.

Eden put his glove back on, one finger at a time, in the same order he had taken it off. The leather was cold and stiff, and it took a moment to settle. Nobody had looked at the bare hand while it showed, and he noted that, and did not trust it to happen again.

* * *

Okoru stood a little apart with his stick under his arm. He had seen the glove come off. He did not look at the hand. He looked at the snow where the glove had lain. Then he looked at Eden's face, and for the first time in months he did not chew anything.

Lysette came through the line with her book under her arm and stopped six paces from the man on the snow. She had felt the moment in the air before she saw it: the sound going out of the yard, the boy falling, a shape that was not a shape. She knelt in the snow with her palm flat on the ground and her eyes shut, and **[Witness]** came over the snow as pale ghost-light.

The snow was uneven. She pushed it flat with her hand before the replay would lie on it, because the light needed a still place. It took four seconds, and the real yard stayed empty and ordinary between the two men, with smoke going through it.

The replay showed the boy's head falling. It showed the knife moving away, and Harrow's hand opening. Between the two men it showed a dark place where the light did not sit right, and a white line across it that came and went in one breath.

"Stop," Okoru said from behind her. "You have seen enough."

"I have not." She moved her hand an inch to the left. "Something is wrong with the light."

"It is dawn. Everything is wrong with the light."

"I know what I saw. I will write it as smoke and a white line. That is all it can be."

"Write that," Okoru said. "And nothing else."

She held the replay until her wrist shook. Then she held it two seconds longer, because the thing she wanted to see had not arrived yet.

The dust in the replay hung in the air. It did not fall when the light moved. It did not settle when the boy fell forward. It stayed where it was, the way dust stays in a room no one has entered for a year. Lysette put out her hand, and the dust did not move for her either.

Then the replay went out. The yard was only a yard, with a fallen man in it, breathing shallowly, and the sun coming up grey over the Wall.
