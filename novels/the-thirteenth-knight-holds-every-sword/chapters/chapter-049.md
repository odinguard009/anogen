# Chapter 49: Would Bells Ring

"Do not shoot," Strent said to the teacher with the blade still in his hand. The teacher said something about the knife at the boy's throat. Strent said, "I know. That is why I am telling you not to shoot. Put it down and stand where you are. I am sorry." He said it with an apology at the end, as always, and the teacher put the blade down.

The boy with the bucket had stopped shaking. That was the first thing Eden noted. A hostage who stopped shaking had decided how he was going to die. The knife was under his jaw and Harrow's left hand had the collar in a grip that would not slip.

The second thing Eden noted was the bell tower. It stood beyond the burning rows with its empty frame showing through the smoke, and it had not rung since the fire began. Nothing in the camp had rung since the Mawgate opened.

"Say it," Harrow said. He had shifted the knife half an inch and the boy's chin was up to keep the blade away. "Say my name and let it come. I know you can."

Eden said nothing.

"Ask him," Harrow said to the soldiers. "Ask the boy what I am. He has looked at me for an hour and he has not said a word."

A soldier said, "Sir, he has a knife at a boy's throat," and Harrow laughed, because that was not the answer he had wanted and he did not have the time to be angry about it.

"Would bells ring for me?" Eden asked, in a very flat voice, to nobody in particular. He had asked the Dragon the same question once in a dream and had never received an answer from that door.

Harrow's mouth shut on the next word. "What?"

"If someone said my name." Eden looked past him at the empty frame of the tower. "They have not rung all night, and you are not the only one with a name they do not know."

Harrow stared at him for half a second. There was anger in his face and no answer in his mouth. Then he shouted his own name again.

"Desmond," Harrow shouted, to the camp and the smoke and the soldiers standing with their blades down. "Desmond Harrow. That is my name. Do you hear me? I give it to you."

The words went across the yard and came back off the stone and went out into the grey country. The bell tower did not ring. A bird flew off the crossbar of the gate. A strip of canvas flapped once in the heat. No bell answered.

Harrow's face changed. He had expected something. He had expected the world to make a sound when he said a name like that. When nothing happened he pushed the knife harder against the boy's throat and shouted the name again.

"Desmond Harrow. Desmond. Say it. Say it—"

Eden stopped walking.

He had come four steps into the open between the tents with **[Exa]** across his arm. He had measured the distance to the knife, the angle of Harrow's wrist and the weight of the man on his feet. He had measured the wrong thing. The knife was not the problem. The man holding it was already spending himself, and the grey at his neck had climbed to his jaw while he shouted.

Eden looked at him. It was not a glare and not a threat. He stopped moving everything except his eyes. He put them on Harrow's face the way he had looked at a ward about to fall or a spear tip that had skipped off a shield.

The shouting stopped. Harrow's mouth stayed open for half a second and then shut. His hand on the boy's collar loosened by the width of a finger. He had not decided to loosen it. He had not heard anything. The blade moved away from the boy's throat and hung in the air where Harrow's hand had put it.

Felix stood at the far door with **[Thessa]** on his arm and an arrow in his hand that he had not shot. He saw the dark shape in the smoke and the gold line that came and went under it. He saw the boy's head fall forward as the knife moved away. He said nothing. He had promised himself at Silverfell that he would not write down the things he saw at night. He kept the promise by not letting himself see them properly: he looked at the shield in his hand, and at the snow under his boots, and at the boy being pulled back through the line, and at everything except the space between Eden and Harrow. He put the arrow back in the bag and kept the shield where it was, and his face did not change at all.

Eden took off his left glove. He did it slowly, one finger at a time, and laid the glove on the top of a crate where the snow had not reached it. Then he went polite, which was a thing the Dragon had taught him and which he used only when he had made up his mind. His hands stopped shaking when he took off the glove. His left hand was pale in the dawn light and there was a small crease across the palm where the lining had pressed it. He put the glove down on the crate without smoothing it. A smooth glove would have been a thing a person had noticed before deciding.

"Mr. Harrow," he said, with his hands at his sides and the scarf still at his mouth. "Let the boy go. I am not going to ask you again."

Harrow's hand opened. The boy dropped the bucket and fell forward into the snow. A prefect caught him by the coat and pulled him back through the line. The boy said something when he was safe. The prefect bent down to hear it, and then looked at Eden with his mouth shut.

Eden pulled his glove from the crate and put it back on, one finger at a time, in the same order he had taken it off. The leather was cold and stiff and took a moment to settle. Nobody looked at the hand while it was bare. He noticed that, and noted that it was not luck.

Harrow did not fall. He turned the grey stump toward Eden and reached inside his coat with his left hand, and what came out was not a second knife but a small strip of grey that had grown out of his palm. Eden drew **[Inead]** under his arm where the firelight would catch it. The gold showed a seam in Harrow's neck where the graft met the skin, a line no wider than a hair and bright as a thread pulled through cloth.

**[Lampwick]** was the sword following the seam its own light showed. It needed a seam and it needed light. It went where the light made the dark edge plain, and nowhere else. Eden had one of the two, and the fire at his back gave him the other. The gold went up the line at Harrow's throat and stopped at the base of the skull. Harrow folded to his knees and then onto his side, with the crown still pinned to his coat and the hand still half open. His chest kept moving. The grey in his eyes was going out like a lamp turned down.

There was no sound when he fell. The fire went on burning in the tent behind it and a piece of canvas dropped off the ridge with a soft slap. A soldier at the gate dropped a bucket, and those were the only noises in that part of the yard. Eden let **[Inead]** go and the gold went out of the smoke. The grey on Harrow's neck stopped shining and went the colour of wet ash.

The soldiers along the line saw only white light. Some of them looked away. A prefect at the gate said later that it was the lanterns going out all at once that had done it. That a shape had passed through the smoke and the next thing the Crown was on the ground. Valerius stood with his hands open and watched the gold go out. He did not write anything down. He did not tell anyone what he had seen. A prefect asked him for an order about the fallen man and he gave one about the children instead. The prefect went away satisfied because an order had been given. Valerius looked at the empty frame of the bell tower for one more breath and then turned to his own Lance and began to count them.

Strent came up last, with his list under his arm and snow on his shoulders. He looked down at the fallen man for three breaths, and then, in a low voice that carried to nobody but Eden, he said, "I am sorry." It was the same apology he used for a dropped pencil. It was not clear whom it was for. Then he put a hand on the nearest soldier's sleeve and asked, in the mild way of a man arranging chairs, whether somebody could see to the well.

Two soldiers brought a door and set it down beside Harrow, and Okoru told them to wait until the Committee had looked at the ground. Eden watched them stand there with their hands at their sides. He picked up his shield from the snow, and found that his hand had closed around the strap hard enough to leave a mark in the glove.

Lysette came through the line with her book under her arm and stopped six paces from the man on the snow. She had felt the moment in the air before she saw it: the sound going out of the yard, the boy falling, the shape that was not a shape. She knelt in the snow with one hand flat on the ground and her eyes shut, and **[Witness]** came over the snow as ghost-light, thin in the dawn.

She had to push the snow flat with her palm before the replay would lie on it, because the wind had got into the drift and the ghost-light needed a still place. The gesture took four seconds, and while she did it the space between the two men in the real yard stayed empty and ordinary, with smoke going through it.

The replay showed the boy's head falling forward. It showed the knife moving away and Harrow's hand opening. It showed a dark place between them where the light did not sit right. A white line across it that came and went in one breath. "Stop," Okoru said, from behind her. "You have seen enough."

"I have not." She moved the hand an inch left so the replay showed the space again. "There is something wrong with the light."

"It is dawn. Everything is wrong with the light."

"I know what I saw." She kept her wrist steady with her other hand. "I am writing it as smoke and a white line. That is all it can be."

"Write that." Okoru looked at the snow where the replay lay. "And nothing else."

She held the replay until her wrist shook. Then she held it two seconds longer, because the thing she needed to see had not arrived yet and she did not know when it would.

The dust in the replay hung still for a breath. It did not fall when the light moved. It did not settle when the boy fell forward. It stayed where it had been in the air, the way dust stays in a room nobody has entered for a year. Lysette put out her hand and the dust did not move for her either. Then the whole replay went out. The yard was only a yard with a fallen man in it, breathing shallowly, and the sun coming up grey over the Wall.
