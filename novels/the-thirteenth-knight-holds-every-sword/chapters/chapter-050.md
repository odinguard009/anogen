# Chapter 50: Liora's Boy

The count took four hours in the grey morning light and was done twice. Forty people had crossed the yard, and thirty-nine were alive at the second bell of the morning. The physician wrote the names on a sheet and crossed one out, and a prefect took the sheet to the gate and brought it back because there was no dry place to keep it. The physician had lost her shoes in the fire and had wrapped her feet in strips of blanket, and she did not sit down until the second count was finished. People came up to her and asked about the missing name and she said the same sentence each time: "He died in the night. I could not save him." By the fifth time she said it, the sentence had stopped being an answer and had become a thing she was reading from a paper.

Clara was alive. She sat on a crate outside the infirmary with her right arm in a sling. A dark line marked the edge of the wound the grey had left. She argued with Rowan when he tried to carry her inside.

"I can walk seven steps," she said.

"You cannot walk seven steps."

"I can walk four and then sit down on the step."

"Then I will carry you four steps." Rowan picked her up with his good arm and carried her inside. She told him he was an idiot. He said yes. Neither of them looked at the soldiers who were listening.

Eden stood at the edge of the yard with the scarf in his hand and watched the count go on around him. The Mawgate had stopped widening at some point in the night, though no one could say when or what had stopped it. The hole in the stores floor was still there with its rim of cold stone, and the tents had burned down to black frames, and the snow beyond the gate had gone grey with dust that did not blow away in the wind.

The nest was under the camp, and it was not finished. Vesk said so three times in the space of a minute, standing where the light was poor, with his coat pulled tight.

"It is a mouth," he said. "It eats and it wakes. You shut it by taking what it eats."

"How?" Rowan asked.

"You do not ask me. I cannot go down there."

Eden went down through the Door in the stores floor after the third bell, with a lamp at his belt and **[Exa]** across his arm. The far side was warm and full of the smell he knew now: the exchange, the dye-works, the construct core, the groundskeeper's coat, Harrow's open arm. The tunnel below had a floor that pulsed under his boots and walls that had once been a burrow of some kind and had been built into something else by grafting. He did not look at the things hanging from the roof. He had one job, and a job was a line of steps. The tunnel floor had a beat to it, and he matched his steps to the beat so that the sound of his boots did not give him away to whatever had made the place. Twice the wall moved toward him and he put the shield between his shoulder and the movement, and twice it went back without touching him, because a thing that eats does not always know what is in front of it. He counted the steps to the heart and counted them again on the way back, and the numbers were the same.

He found the heart where the warmth was strongest, behind a fold of grey flesh that moved when he came near it. It was not a stone and not an animal. It was a knot of the same stuff packed into the construct's core, but harder here, with threads running into the walls. The light from his belt made no shine on it at all. **[Lampwick]** showed a seam along its underside where the light went in and did not come back, and Eden put **[Inead]** into that seam and turned once. The knot came apart in his hand into grey pieces that had no weight, and the walls around it stopped pulsing in four directions and then in the last one, and the tunnel went cold.

He came up through the Door with dust on his sleeves and put the pieces in a bucket of water that a soldier held out without being asked. The water went grey and then clear. The rim of the hole in the stores floor folded inward on itself like a mouth closing, and the snow over it settled without a sound.

Strent met him at the stair with a cup of tea and a sheet of paper. "I am sorry," he said. "The office wants a form for the hole in the floor. I have written 'closed' in the space marked CONDITION, but I do not know if that is the word they use."

"It is a word."

"Then I will leave it." Strent looked at the grey on Eden's sleeves. He did not ask about it. "You should sleep. I will write the rest."

A sergeant came past with a coil of rope and asked whether the oil line should be taken up. Eden told him to leave it until spring, and the sergeant wrote that down as an order and went away. The lower-city children sat in the sun by the kitchen door with two blankets and a heap of bread between them. The smallest one counted the loaves over and over. Nobody had given him anything else to do.

By the fourth bell the camp had begun to make its ordinary noises again. A cook shouted about bread, two prefects argued about a form, and Felix stood by the gate with a roll in his hand and a face that had not changed all night.

"Eaten today?" he said, when Eden came past.

"No."

"That is not a technically. That is a no." Felix broke the roll in two and gave him the larger half. "You can have it. I am not asking where you went."

A soldier called from the infirmary door. Harrow was on a cot inside with his coat cut away and his grey arm stump wrapped in cloth, and the grey had climbed his neck to the corner of one eye. He was dying, and he knew it, and he had been asking for someone by name since the sun came up.

"Wren," Harrow said, when Eden came in. The voice was mostly air now. "Come here. I want to say it properly, and I cannot hold the voice long."

Eden stood at the foot of the cot. Lysette stood by the window with her book closed and her hands in her pockets. She had not gone near the body all morning and her boots were still wet from the yard.

"You asked me what I was," Eden said.

"No." Harrow's good eye found him. "I asked the wrong thing. She stopped reading me."

"Who?"

"Liora." The grey at his eye moved when he said the name, like something under paper. "She made my blade. She read me while she made it, and then she stopped. That is how I knew she had chosen someone else." Harrow took a breath that did not go all the way down. "Hunger is a god. That is what she told the forge. And the Tailor sends regards to Liora's boy."

Lysette's pencil moved once in her pocket and stopped.

Eden did not look at her. He looked at the grey at the corner of Harrow's eye and at the hand on the cot that was no longer quite a hand, and he counted the spaces between the man's breaths. Harrow said one more word, very low, and Eden knew it was his mother's name, which no one in this camp had spoken aloud before that morning. Then the breathing stopped and the grey on his face went still.

Lysette came across the room and knelt in the snow by the infirmary step, where the replay had lain the night before. She put her hand flat on the stone and shut her eyes. The ghost-light came back thin and pale in the morning. It showed the boy falling and the knife moving away. It showed the patch of wrong light between the two men, and a white line that came and went in one breath and left no mark on the snow. Her wrist shook at the corners and she held it steady with her other hand.

The replay held longer than the others had held. It showed the boy's head falling and the knife going away, and the white line came back twice, once bright and once so thin that a person would have called it a mistake. Lysette moved her hand three inches to the left and the white line moved with it. She wrote one word on the back of her hand and shut her eyes again.

She opened her eyes. The replay showed a space where a person had stood and no person in it. She looked at that space for a long time, and then at Eden, and then back at the space. She said nothing. She shut the book and stood up with snow on her knees, and walked toward the gate with the red band straight on her sleeve.

Eden stayed where he was with the scarf in his hand. He had done everything he had come to do. The camp was saved and the nest was closed and forty people were counted, and nothing in any of it required him to ask the question that came into his mouth.

"Lysette," he said.

She stopped at the gate without turning round.

"Do you think the lanterns will be hung again next year?" he asked. He did not need the answer. He had seen the men take the frames down in November and he knew the rule about the first bell. He asked it because she was standing with her back to him and because his thumb had found the worn place in the scarf where her hand had held it, and his pulse had started counting in his throat.

"Yes," she said. "They hang them at dawn. I told you in December."

"Yes," Eden said. "You did tell me."

He stood with his hand closed over the scarf. She went through the gate and the snow filled the place where her boots had been. Then he put the scarf around his neck, the way he had worn it all winter, and went to help the cook carry the bread.
