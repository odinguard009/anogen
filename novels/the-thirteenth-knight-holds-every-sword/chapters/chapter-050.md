# Chapter 50: The Question

The morning after the fire was quiet in and a house is quiet when a great deal has been said. Nobody wants to say more. The men moved through the yard in small groups with their hands on their kit. Their faces turned toward the square where the ground had been warm for a week. Eden stood at the edge of the square with his gloves sealed. His hood down. He watched the engineers work. He counted the men going past him. Stopped at thirty-nine because a man with a stretcher came between him. The gate and he lost the count.

The nest was under the square where the pump had been. They closed it at noon with a line of ward-plate men on the steps. A second line at the south gate. The engineers packed the mouth with stone. Grey salt and a mana charge that hummed when the current went through it. The smell of wet iron and old bread went away by degrees. The men who had been on the wall came down. Stood in the yard with their hands out to the weak sun. Nobody said much because they had all been cold for a long time. Were only now beginning to feel it.

The cook came to the square with a pot. A ladle and set the pot on a flat stone. She called out that there was soup for anyone who wanted it. That she was not asking twice. Three men came over with their cups. Then five more, and a older student wrote a number on a sheet because the cook always wanted the number of cups written down even when there was no form for it. Rowan sat on the step of the infirmary with his hands wrapped around a cup. His spear across his knees. And Clara sat beside him with her shoulder bound. Her staff in the snow. They drank their soup without talking. Which was a kind of rest after the week they had had.

Harrow lay on a stretcher in the infirmary tent with the grey stopped at his jaw. His breath coming in short pulls. And Valerius stood at the door with his blade under his arm. And Strent sat on a crate beside the bed with his clipboard on his knee. Wrote nothing. Eden came in with a cup of water in his hand. The dying man's eyes moved toward him. The grey in his face kept its shape while the rest of him went still.

"You," Harrow said, and his voice was wet and low. "Come closer. I know your name."

Eden set the cup on the crate and did not come closer.

"Eden Wren," Harrow said, and then he said a second name in the same breath, and it was not Wren, and the word sat in the tent with nothing to carry it, and Strent's pen stopped above the paper, and Valerius's hand went to his blade hilt.

"Her ledger went shut. Hunger is a god. The Tailor sends regards to Liora's boy. He said it in pieces with his breath between them. The grey at his jaw moved once. Then his eyes went still. The tent was quiet except for the snow on the canvas.

Halvard Strent wrote one line and shut the book. And Valerius said that he did not understand the words. And Strent said that neither did he. That he would send the line to the palace with the rest of the report. And Eden stood beside the crate with his hand on the cup. His face still. He thought about a woman who had made a shield that came back to the hand that held it. About a Dragon that would not say whose hand had mended the mark under the strap. About a name that had been spoken by a dying man who had no right to it.

Valerius came out of the tent behind him. Stopped at the door. He said that Harrow had spoken a name that was not Eden Wren. And that he had heard it plainly. And Eden said that he had heard it too. Valerius said that a dying man could say anything. That a name was only a word. And Eden said that some names were doors. And Valerius looked at him. Said that he had waited six weeks for a true sentence and that this one would have to do. And then he walked away toward the gate with his blade under his arm.

He went out into the yard. The camp was counting itself in groups of ten. The cook stood on the step with her ladle across her chest. Called the numbers as the men came past. And Felix came out of the kitchen with a bun in his hand. Put it in Eden's.

"Eaten today? Felix asked, and his voice was steady, and his aura was up without his knowing it, and the snow that had been falling on his shoulder slid off and landed on the boards instead of melting.

"Yes," Eden said, and he took the bun.

Felix looked at the snow on the boards. Then at his own shoulder. He said that the snow had done that before. At the cistern, and that he had not asked about it then and was not asking now, and he walked back into the kitchen with his hands in his pockets, and the corner of his mouth was up, and Eden saw it and did not say anything about it.

Lysette came out of the infirmary with her book under her arm. Her face set. She went to the place by the water barrel where the snow had been pressed flat. She knelt and put both hands on the ground. The Witness came up pale around her: the knife. The boy, the grey arm, the half circle of the spear,. A dark shape in the place where a man should have stood. With a line of gold light along the snow. The replay held longer than it should. The grey dust in it hung still above the pressed snow while one real flake fell through it. Landed on Lysette's hand. Then the picture went. She stayed kneeling with her hands flat on the ground.

When she stood up she looked at Eden across the yard. She did not ask the question that was in her face. She put the book in her coat. Walked to the gate with her shoulders straight. And Eden watched her go. The scarf was under his coat. His hand found it through the cloth. He held it and it were an answer he had written. Did not want to read.

He went after her as far as the gate. Stopped before she reached the road. He said, "Lysette."

She turned, and her face was careful, and she waited.

"Did you get the soup I paid for at the bakery? he asked, and he knew the answer, and he knew that she knew he knew, and he asked it anyway, because it was a question that did not need an answer and she had never refused him one.

Lysette looked at him for a while. Then she said, "You did not eat any of it,". She walked down the road with her head up. Her book under her arm. And Eden stood at the gate with the scarf in his hand. The question still between them. For the first time in his life he wanted someone to see him clearly. He was afraid of it. He did not go after her.

The bell rang over the camp for the evening watch. The men took their posts. The snow kept falling on the covered mouth of the nest where the mana charge hummed under the stone. And Eden went up to the wall with the scarf folded in his pocket. His hands sealed in his gloves. He stood where he could see the road. Did not look away from it until the lights of the palace came on in the city below.

The engineers finished at dusk. Covered the mouth of the nest with a slab of stone. A layer of grey salt. And the mana charge hummed once when they sealed it. Then went quiet. And a man who had been standing too close said that the sound reminded him of a bell heard through a wall. And nobody asked him to explain. The cooks served a second meal. And the men ate in the open because the barracks were still smoke-blackened. And the boy with the burnt sleeve sat at the end of the kitchen step with a cup in both hands. A blanket around his shoulders. And he watched the wall and he had never seen it before. Was not sure he liked it.

Rowan came to sit beside Eden after the meal. And he said that Clara was asleep. That he had been awake for two days. Did not want to sleep yet. And Eden said that he did not want to sleep either. And Rowan said that this was the first thing they had agreed on in a month. That it should be written down. And Eden said that Lysette would write it down if he asked her. And Rowan said that he would not ask her because she had enough to write already. And then he put his head back against the wall and went to sleep with his mouth open, and Eden sat beside him and did not move so as not to wake him.

Lysette stayed at the gate until the road was dark. And then she came back to the wall with her book under her arm. The round sheet folded in her coat. And she stood two paces from Eden. Looked at the city lights coming on below the Wall. She said that she had written the report. That the line about the light was still in it. That she had not crossed it out. And Eden said that a true line was a true line. And she said that this was not a thing he was allowed to say to her because he knew what true meant. She did not know what he meant by it. And he said nothing, and she stood with her shoulders straight and the wind moving the loose strand of her hair, and after a while she said that she was going to bed and that he should come down before the morning bell, and she walked away along the wall, and he watched the white collar until it was lost in the dark.

He put the scarf to his face before he went down. And it smelled of soap and mint and smoke. And he folded it along her creases. Put it in his pocket. And he went to his bed. Lay down with his boots on and his gloves sealed. And he slept without the war dream. And when he woke the snow had stopped. The sky was clear and the camp was already loud with men counting the living.

The boy with the burnt sleeve came to the wall after the second meal and stood beside Eden with his blanket around his shoulders. He asked whether the thing under the square was gone, and Eden said that it was, and the boy asked whether the smell would come back, and Eden said that he did not know, and the boy said that his mother had taught him that a person who did not know could still say true things, and Eden said that this was right, and the boy put his hand on the wall and then took it away because it was cold, and he went back to the kitchen step, and the cook called after him to come in out of the wind.

Lysette came up the wall later with her book under her arm and her round sheet in her coat, and she handed Eden a small square of folded cloth, and he opened it and found the glove she had taken in the infirmary washed and dried and mended at one finger with a different thread. She said that she had mended it badly and that he could complain to her mother if he wanted to, and he said that he would not, and she said that he had not complained about anything all winter and that she had begun to think he did not know how, and he said that he knew how and was choosing not to, and she said that the difference was a kind of joke, and she wrote a line in her book and walked away down the wall, and he folded the glove into his pocket and stood watching her until the dark took her.
