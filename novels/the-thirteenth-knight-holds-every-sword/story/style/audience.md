# Audience setting (owner order, 28 Nov 2026)

This file is a setting, not a chapter edit. It applies to every future chapter unless the owner changes it.

## Who reads this

- Minimum age: **15 years**.
- Most readers are **not native English speakers**.
- Many readers are learning English as a second or third language, including readers from countries with fewer English resources.
- A reader may stop and put the book down if a word, name, or idiom needs a dictionary.

## Language rules that follow from this audience

1. Use the most common everyday word. If two words mean the same thing, use the shorter, more common one.
2. Do not use an idiom unless its meaning is clear from the words in the same sentence. If it needs explaining, do not use it.
3. Do not use a literary comparison that only works in English. A comparison must name two things the reader can see.
4. Do not use a rare word to be elegant. Use the plain word, even if the sentence is less "beautiful".
5. Every special word (job, group, place, ability, title, creature) gets one plain sentence of explanation at first use. If the word could be mistaken for something else, say what it is NOT. Example: "Lance Nine, a group of five students, not a weapon."
6. One idea per sentence. Prefer 8–13 words. A sentence over 18 words must be split.
7. Dialogue carries one idea per line, with a plain tag: "he said", she asked", "Eden said".
8. Repeat the same plain word for the same thing. Do not swap synonyms to avoid repetition.
8b. When a canon word is harder than an everyday word, use the everyday word in narration. Example: write **group**, not *lance*, for a team of students. Keep the canon word only as a name on first use, with a plain explanation.
8d. Describe by seeing and doing, not by analogy. No *rectangle*, *knuckle*, *pale/clean edge*, *grey-blue*, or *the way it...* for a thing. Write the action, the object, one visible detail, then the name.
8c. Do not describe a mood with a comparison. Say what a person sees, does, or says. Example: not "the air was thick with waiting", but "the men stopped talking and looked at the gate."
9. No slang that needs cultural background. No wordplay that needs English grammar knowledge.
10. Keep the story exciting. Simple language does not mean a simple plot.

## Check before publishing a chapter

- Would a 15-year-old learner understand this sentence without a dictionary?
- Is any name or special word explained where it first appears?
- Is any idiom or comparison only understandable to a native speaker?
- Did we use two different words for the same thing?
