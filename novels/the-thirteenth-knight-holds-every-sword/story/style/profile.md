# Style profile (swappable; engine reads only this file for style)

## LEVEL: simple English for learners (owner's order 2026-11-25; applies on top of READABILITY and wins any conflict)
The owner is not a native English reader. Write as if for readers who recently learned English (about CEFR A2-B1). The story stays exciting; only the language gets simpler.
- Use the most common everyday words (roughly the 2,000-3,000 most common English words). If a word is rare, formal, literary or old-fashioned, replace it with a plain one. Examples: "stairs" not "flights"; "went quiet" not "fell silent"; "angry" not "incensed"; "hide" not "conceal".
- Idioms and sayings: at most ONE easy one per chapter, as spice (owner's order 2026-11-25), and only if the sentence around it makes the meaning clear. Never inside fights, plans, key emotions or clues. No slang that needs cultural knowledge; no hard ones ("flights of stairs", "break the ice", "under the weather", "flight risk"). Say the plain meaning instead. Avoid phrasal verbs when one plain verb works ("look for" is fine; "brush off", "pan out" are not).
- One idea per sentence. Mostly 6-12 words. Simple past tense, active voice. Subject first. No inverted sentences, no passive chains, no sentences with more than one "which/that/who" clause, no semicolons, no parentheses.
- Concrete words for things people can picture (door, rope, knife, bread). Abstract nouns only when needed. Say numbers and places clearly.
- Figurative language: at most one very simple comparison per page ("fast like a horse"). No stacked or poetic metaphors. Jokes must work from plain words, not wordplay.
- World words (invented names and terms): at most 1-2 new ones per chapter; the first time, explain them in plain words inside the sentence ("the Hush, the black fog that eats land").
- Dialogue: short lines, normal spoken English, one idea per line. People can still be funny, rude or shy.
- Fights: say who does what, in order, with simple verbs (hit, jump, block, run, fall). No long poetic action sentences.
- Keep the story exactly: events, names, facts, hooks. Only the language changes.

## CLARITY AND PICTURE (owner's order 2026-11-25; applies to chapters written from now on; wins over Voice, Human feel and Protagonist voice when they conflict)
Why: chapters 3-4 were still hard to follow although sentences averaged only 7 words. The cause was not sentence length. It was: 20-25 invented names and terms per chapter, idioms and jokes that need cultural knowledge, rare nouns (assessor, ledger, refectory, registrar), little description of places and faces, and choppy 6-7 word sentences with no linking words. The reader must be able to "see" every scene without guessing.
1. NAME BUDGET: at most 12 distinct capitalized names and terms per chapter (the checker fails above 16). Background people get no name ("the clerk", "the old woman"). At most 2 new named people per chapter. Each new person gets one plain tag the first time (job or look).
2. WORLD TERMS: at most 2 new ones per chapter. Say the plain thing first, then the name: "the black armor, Umra". One thing, one name; never swap synonyms. Explain every new term in the same or the next sentence.
3. PLAIN NOUNS: if a school-level English reader would not know the word, swap it or explain it in 3-4 words (ledger = book; assessor = tax checker; refectory = dining hall; atrium = big front hall; registrar = office clerk).
4. IDIOMS AND JOKES: at most one easy idiom per chapter (spice only; see LEVEL). Nothing like "flight risk", "give a finger", "like a sack of flour". A joke must work from plain words and be easy to catch on first read.
5. LINKED SENTENCES: aim for an average of 9-12 words, range 5-18. Use simple linking words (and, but, because, so, then, when, after) so ideas connect. Do not write more than 3 sentences in a row under 6 words, except in a fast fight.
6. DIALOGUE: 25-40% of the words in every chapter. Speaker is clear. One idea per line. A hint or riddle is allowed once per scene, and the plain meaning follows within two lines.
7. PICTURE RULE (description): every scene opens with 2-3 short sentences: where, what time, what the light looks like, one sound or smell, who is there. Every named person gets 2 visible traits on first appearance (face, hair, clothes, size) and one way of moving or talking. In long scenes, say again where people stand. Monsters get size, color and shape compared to a thing everyone knows ("as tall as a door", "like a big dog"), never "too many elbows" alone.
8. FIGHT PICTURE: before the action starts, say where each person stands, how far apart (in steps), and what is around (wall, stairs, barrels). Then one clear move per sentence. If it is too dark to see, say how the hero knows what happens (sound, touch, light from a match).
9. SAY THE PLAN: when the hero does something clever, one plain sentence says what he wants and why, before or right after ("I needed light. Light makes edges. Edges make doors.").
10. Keep chapter length 1800-2500 words; description replaces side details, not story events.

## FIRST USE OF A WEAPON, ARMOR OR ABILITY (owner's order 2026-11-25; permanent; same priority as CLARITY AND PICTURE)
- The first time a named character uses a weapon, armor, relic or ability on the page, describe it in 2-4 plain sentences INSIDE the action (never a pause for exposition): (1) what it looks like, (2) how the body uses it (grip, stance, gesture, sound), (3) what it does to the target or the space, (4) its limit or cost, if the character knows it.
- Use the same pattern for every new character with a fighting power when they first act, so the reader can picture what this person can do before the scene asks them to win.
- Later uses stay short and only add what is new (a new form, a new limit, a new trick). Never repeat the full description.
- Track it: story/bible/first-use.md lists each weapon and ability and the chapter where its first-use description was given. Before writing a use, check that file; after writing a first use, add the line.

## POWER, TECH AND REVEAL CANON (owner's order 2026-11-25; permanent)
- Aura, mana, Tenet, class/job and spell words follow story/bible/power-system.md exactly (one word, one meaning; no levels or ranks; a class/job reading is one rare italic line only its owner sees).
- Tech details (guns, veinlines, bellwire, presses, engines) follow story/bible/tech.md; at most 2 new tech terms per chapter; a gun never decides a knight's fight on its own.
- Every reveal follows story/bible/reveal-ladder.md: hint, doubt, evidence, confirm, in that order and with its gaps; never confirm early.
- Awakening (Ondra) vocabulary follows power-system.md section 14. Its first appearance on the page needs the full first-use description and heavy earlier build-up; it never appears in a chapter without earlier hints on the ladder.

## SPECIAL ATTACK LABEL (owner's order 2026-11-25; permanent)
- When a named signature attack (or a named form on its first or deciding use) lands, the narration shows its name as a bold label in square brackets: **[Latch]**. Put it at the moment the move lands, on its own short line or inside the action sentence.
- It is a narration marker, not speech: never inside dialogue quotes unless the character really says the name.
- First use: the attack's first-use description (look, body, effect, limit) in the SAME scene. Later uses: the label plus one fresh detail. The label never replaces description.
- At most 1-2 labels per chapter. Names and owners: power-system.md section 13. Bold brackets = attack; italics = class/job reading; never mix them.

## SCENE FEEL AND ARMOR (owner's order 2026-11-25; permanent; same priority as CLARITY AND PICTURE)
SCENE FEEL (mood):
- Before writing each scene, pick its mood in 2-3 words (tense, warm, sad, funny, cold, quiet-before-trouble). Then let everything carry it: the place and light, the sounds, sentence length and speed, the verbs, what people do with their hands. One scene, one main mood; a change of mood needs a clear turn inside the scene.
- Tense = short, fast sentences, sounds, close small details. Warm or sad = slower, objects, pauses, plain words about the body. Funny = good timing, plain words, a straight face. Cold or scary = what is missing (no sound, no light), one strange detail.
- End a scene on a beat that keeps the mood (an action, a sound, a line of dialogue). No moral and no summary sentence.
- Keep the mood the outline and bible give for the chapter; comedy never breaks a serious scene.
ARMOR (follow story/bible/armor.md; do not invent new armor facts):
- Whenever canon has armor worn, called or hit, show it. The call: the knock, the sound, the cold that turns warm, plates closing from the feet up (3-6 short lines, one detail unique to this time). The look: color, shape, helm, glove, size, in plain words (plate, helm, glove, shield, lance). The body: weight, noise, how his walk or jump changes. The fight: which part takes the hit (shoulder plate, arm guard), the sound, what it does to the hit. The cost: the cold or Frost, or the Tolls felt as heavier joints, never listed as numbers.
- No Raiment in public unless canon says so. Do not add armor that canon does not have. Armor-up scenes are aura moments: show how people nearby react.
HUMAN FEEL (at simple level; reinforces "Human feel" below):
- Simple words, but a real person's voice: small opinions, dry jokes in plain words, odd specific details (a chipped cup, a stale roll), things a person would notice and a camera would not.
- Uneven rhythm: sometimes one short line alone, sometimes a longer sentence with and/but/because. Not a steady polished beat.
- People interrupt, dodge, answer a different question, trail off. Each speaker sounds different by word choice and line length, never by hard words.
- Allowed: a flat joke, a wandering thought, a small mistake. Not allowed: a tidy moral at the end, "not X but Y" more than once, lists of three as a habit, stacked similes, repeated gestures (sigh, clenched fists), explaining a feeling after showing it.

## READABILITY (owner's order 2026-11-25; highest priority, overrides any rule below that conflicts)
- Audience: teenagers and young adults reading web novels / light novels on a phone. Reference feel: Holy Emperor's Grandson is a Necromancer, Semi-Coercive Imperialist, The Academy's Weapon Replicator, Highschool DxD, Mahouka, Infinite Dendrogram, Bleach, Dandadan (ideas of pace and clarity only; never copy).
- Plain, everyday words. Pick the simple word every time: "use" not "employ", "hard" not "arduous", "hide" not "conceal". If a word would make a teen reach for a dictionary, replace it. Invented world terms are fine but explain them by action in one short line, max 1-2 new ones per chapter.
- Short sentences, mostly 8-18 words. Max one long sentence per paragraph; no sentence over 30 words. Few commas and clauses stacked together; no semicolons.
- Short paragraphs: 1-4 lines on a phone, usually 1-3 sentences. Break big blocks.
- More dialogue: aim for 25-40% of the words in quiet scenes, still plenty in fights. Quick back-and-forth, one idea per line. Each speaker sounds different and talks like a real teen or young adult (contractions, slang-lite, teasing), not like a legal document.
- Fights: clear and fast. Say what happens in order, who is where, what the move is. Short punchy lines; no abstract or poetic descriptions of combat.
- Feelings and humor must be easy to catch on first read: keep subtext, but make the joke or the emotion land without needing a second read. Wit stays dry but simple.
- Keep metaphors rare, concrete and easy (one per page at most). No academic, legal, military-jargon or archaic wording unless a character is deliberately a stiff noble, and even then keep it short.
- Keep the human feel rules below (uneven rhythm, no tidy endings, no AI tics), but never at the cost of clarity.

## Voice
- First person, past tense. Dry, observant, understated humor; feelings shown through small actions rather than stated.
- Tactical narration in fights: read the terrain, name the options, pay the cost.

## Prose
- Concrete nouns and strong verbs; avoid stacked adjectives and filler adverbs.
- Vary sentence length. Short paragraphs in action; longer in quiet beats.
- Dialogue carries subtext; people rarely say exactly what they mean.

## Chapter shape
- Open in motion or with a concrete image, never a weather or waking-up cliche.
- End on a hook that is different in kind from the previous chapters' hooks.

## Freshness
- Do not reuse a simile, a gesture (sighing, clenching fists) or a sentence pattern more than once per arc.
- Each chapter: at least one surprising specific detail that belongs only to this world.

## Human feel (owner's priority: must not read as AI-written)
- Voice first: the narrator has opinions, dry asides, small unfair judgments. He notices odd, specific things a person would notice, not what a camera would.
- Uneven rhythm on purpose: fragments, a one-line paragraph, then a long run-on when the mind races. Never a steady, polished cadence.
- Do not explain a feeling after showing it. Do not end paragraphs or scenes with a tidy moral, summary or aphorism. Cut the last sentence if it only restates.
- No rule-of-three lists as a habit; no "not X, but Y" or "It wasn't X. It was Y." more than once per chapter; no stacked similes.
- Dialogue: people interrupt, dodge, answer a different question, trail off. Each speaker sounds different (vocabulary, length, verbal tics). Tags mostly "said"; action beats instead of adverbs.
- Contractions in narration and dialogue. Plain words over fancy ones. Concrete nouns (a chipped mug, a stale roll) over abstractions.
- Allow small mess: a flat joke, a wandering thought. Avoid symmetry between paragraphs and chapters.
- Vary openers; never three sentences in a row starting with a name or "He".
- Emotional peaks get restraint: fewer words, the body and objects carry it.

## Protagonist voice (Eden; original blend: cold analyst, lazy-surface hider, hard realist, fierce protector)
- Narrates like a quiet engineer of outcomes: notices exits, shadows, who is lying, what it costs. Understatement and literal answers as humor ("Eaten today?" "Technically.").
- Lazy on the surface (naps, skips, shrugs), prepared underneath (scouted three nights ago). Never boasts; never explains his plan before it works.
- Realist: no illusions about nobles, nations or himself; chooses the least bad option and says so. Keeps a few hard rules anyway (no kill without confirmation; never kill humans with the Emperor's knights).
- Flat feelings, shown by what he does: remembers everyone's tea, stands between, smiles only at his household. Healing shows as small slips: a laugh he didn't plan, a sentence he can't finish near Lysette.
- Fights calm and minimal (style "Lintel and Lamp": shield to read, lance to open, light to end); variety grows with retainers and relics (bible/combat-style.md).
- Snaps when someone hurts his people on purpose, or uses a hostage or a child: he goes polite and very quiet, takes off his left glove, and stops hiding. Then his resolve, not his mood, raises his power.

## Battles and aura
- Mix across each arc roughly: 30% flex (he wins easily; let the reader enjoy it), 35% puzzle (terrain, rules, timing), 25% strained (god-hosts, elites: he must truly try), 10% costly (he wins, something real is lost). Tags per arc: bible/arcs-3.md.
- Aura moments are rare (2-4 per arc), earned by earlier hiding, and always include reaction shots: who goes silent, who kneels, what a veteran's hands do. Never narrate the aura in adjectives alone.
- Battle & armor: every fight shows at least one new, creative use of his power (bible/battle-craft.md); never default to "a bigger weapon". Armor-ups (bible/armor.md) take 3-6 lines: the knock, the sound, cold then warm, one unique detail; then weight, texture, changed movement; the Tolls felt, not counted; the cost lands afterward.
- Human realism: proportionate reactions, mixed feelings, ordinary flaws; antagonists are credible people with reasons. No humiliate-and-reveal loops (mock, reveal, crowd gasps): at most once per arc, never the same shape. Politics: stakes, clock and rule stated once; every scene changes the situation (bible/intrigue-craft.md).
