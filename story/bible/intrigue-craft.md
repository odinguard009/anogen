# Intrigue craft: making politics and spycraft exciting (reference)

## Anti-boredom rules
- No exposition meetings. Every political scene has a conflict inside it and ends with the situation changed (a vote lost, a secret moved, a friend compromised).
- Short, punchy scenes; cut between rooms when two things happen at once.
- The reader always knows the stakes, the clock and the rule of the game, stated once in a concrete line ("seven of twelve masters, by the noon bell").
- Track information: the reader should know who knows what. Use asymmetry: the reader knows more than a character (dread) or less (surprise), never both muddled.
- Reversals come from preparation shown earlier, not luck. Allies out-think, not out-punch.
- Personal stakes: every political arc threatens someone in the main circle (a reputation, a marriage, a friendship, a life).
- Humor and character inside politics: Seraphine's asides, the Duke's interruptions, Ysolde's forms, Valerius's memos.

## Scene patterns
- The Count: votes counted aloud, faces watched; a vote flips for a human reason.
- The Clock: a bell, a moon, a ration count, a train of carts.
- The Clause: a treaty line read twice; the second reading changes everything.
- The Cutout Chain: messages through three hands; the hero follows the chain one link per scene.
- The Ledger: a book of debts or names; whoever holds it holds people.
- The Offer: a temptation that solves a real problem at a real price.
- Two Rooms: parallel scenes, each a move in one game.
- The Canary Trap: each suspect gets a slightly different secret; the leak reveals who.
- The Tell: reading a negotiator's hands, tea, pauses.
- The Quiet Win: the opponent realizes he has lost three scenes after it happened.

## Human realism (for every scene, not only politics)
- Proportionate reactions: people are startled, then practical; few gasp in unison.
- Avoid humiliate-and-reveal loops (villain mocks, hero reveals, crowd stunned). At most once per arc, never in the same shape, and the mocker has a believable reason.
- Antagonists are credible: real grievances, fears or convictions; they make smart moves and mistakes like people do. No cartoon cruelty or stupidity.
- Mixed feelings: relief and resentment, love and irritation, loyalty and doubt in the same person.
- Ordinary flaws and needs: hunger, tiredness, pride, pettiness, jokes at the wrong time.
- Consequences linger: people remember slights and favours; reputations change slowly.
- Aura moments stay, but reactions vary: silence, a joke, a practical question, someone pretending not to be impressed, someone angry he hid it.
